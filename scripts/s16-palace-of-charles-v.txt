# title: Stop 16. Palace of Charles V
# stop: 16
N: Stop sixteen. The Palace of Charles the Fifth.

N: You are on the open esplanade, facing the west front of a vast square palace. It belongs to a different world from everything else on this hill. This is the Renaissance, set down in a Nasrid palace city.

N: The front is sixty-three metres wide. The whole building is a square of that size.

...

N: Look at the lower storey. The stone is left rough and heavy, in deep blocks. This is called rusticated stone. Now look up, to the upper storey. Here the columns are Ionic, lighter and smoother.

N: Now look closely at the flat pillars of the lower storey. Set into them are bronze rings.

...

N: Find the doorways. They are framed in stone from Sierra Elvira. On the facade, look for a carved battle scene. It shows the Battle of Pavia, in northern Italy, in fifteen twenty-five.

N: There Charles's army crushed the French and captured King Francis the First himself. It was the high point of his power, and he had it carved onto his palace here.

...

A: Several things are said about this front. The main west portal is said to have been finished under Philip the Second by Juan de Herrera. The facades are said to carry green marble medallions, and reclining winged figures above the doorways. Treat these as unconfirmed.

A: You will also notice that the palace sits at a slight angle to its neighbours. One story says the archbishop objected to it crowding the church on the site of the old mosque. That too is unconfirmed.

...

N: Who built this, and why here? Charles the Fifth was the grandson of Ferdinand and Isabella. He was king of Spain from fifteen sixteen and Holy Roman Emperor from fifteen nineteen. He ruled much of Europe and the Americas.

N: In fifteen twenty-six he married Isabella of Portugal in Seville. He then spent several months in the Alhambra, and decided to build a second royal residence here.

N: The designer was Pedro Machuca, trained in Rome. Work began in fifteen twenty-seven. Machuca directed it on site from fifteen thirty-three until his death in fifteen fifty. His son Luis built the courtyard.

N: Charles never lived in it. Neither did his son, Philip the Second, who moved the court to Madrid. Work stopped for fifteen years after the Morisco rebellion of fifteen sixty-eight. In sixteen thirty-seven it was abandoned without a roof. Restoration began in nineteen twenty-three, and the building was finished only in the middle of the twentieth century.

...

A: It is often said that a special tax paid by the Moriscos largely funded the work.

...

N: Who were the Moriscos? After fourteen ninety-two, Granada's Muslims were promised freedom of religion. Between fourteen ninety-nine and fifteen oh two, they were forced to choose baptism or exile. Those who stayed became Moriscos, Christians in name only. Step by step, their language, dress and customs were banned.

N: In fifteen sixty-eight that pressure exploded in a great revolt in the Alpujarras. Those are the valleys on the far side of the Sierra Nevada, the mountains you saw from the Alcazaba.

N: The revolt was crushed, and Granada's Moriscos were scattered across Castile. Between sixteen oh nine and sixteen fourteen they were expelled from Spain altogether. This half-built palace is, among other things, a monument to that story.

...

N: Now walk inside, to the courtyard.

...

N: You are standing in a circle, about thirty metres across, set inside the square. Two rings of columns run around you. Doric below, plain and sturdy. Ionic above. A circle inside a square was a Renaissance ideal.

N: Machuca split the plan along a diagonal, into an emperor's side and an empress's side. Each had its own staircase, and the two met at an eight-sided chapel.

N: Walk to the centre and look up at the open sky. Now think back to the Court of the Lions. That court is all columns and light. This one is all mass.

...

A: Try this. Stand dead centre and speak or clap softly. Many visitors notice an echo. You will also hear this court compared to Hadrian's Villa at Tivoli.

A: And one famous line. Charles is often quoted as saying, on first seeing the Alhambra, unfortunate the man who lost all this. No early source records it.

...

N: When you are ready, follow the signs to the Alhambra Museum. That is stop seventeen.
