# title: How to read a Nasrid wall
# stop: 0
N: How to read a Nasrid wall.

N: This track is for the queue. Every room in the palaces uses the same vocabulary. Read each wall from the floor up. Tiles at the bottom, carved plaster in the middle, wood or honeycomb overhead. Then the writing, which runs through them all.

...

N: Start at the floor. At eye level and below, the walls are clad in glazed tiles, cut to shape and fitted into geometric patterns. The Spanish word for this cut tilework is alicatado.

N: The patterns start simple. Picture one square. Lay a second square over it, turned forty-five degrees. Now you have an eight-pointed star.

N: Extend its lines, and you get longer stars, octagons and crosses. Rotate, mirror and repeat. Choose which lines to keep and which colours to fill. That is how one square grows into the interlacing star patterns called lazo.

...

N: These patterns have had a long afterlife. The Dutch artist M. C. Escher sketched the tiles here in nineteen twenty-two. He came back to draw them again in nineteen thirty-six. They fed straight into his interlocking prints.

N: A century earlier, the architect Owen Jones measured and drew the Alhambra's ornament. His books, and the Alhambra Court he built at London's Crystal Palace, spread Nasrid patterns through Victorian design.

...

A: You will hear that every kind of symmetry is here. Mathematicians say every repeating flat pattern belongs to one of exactly seventeen types. The claim is that all seventeen appear on these walls.

A: Careful surveys disagree, and some count fewer. Either way, the craftsmen got there centuries before the mathematics was written down.

...

N: Why did geometry matter so much? One historian's answer, in a recent documentary, is that it was useful first. Astronomy and geometry told people the prayer times and the direction of Mecca. They guided pilgrims and traders. They were practical, even religious, skills long before they became an art.

...

N: Now the middle band. Above the tiles, the walls are carved plaster, called stucco. You will see plant scrolls, lattices, and bands of writing.

...

N: Now the ceiling. It comes in two kinds. One is carpentry, thousands of small wooden pieces fitted into star patterns.

N: The other is muqarnas. These are small, prism-shaped cells of plaster, stacked into honeycomb vaults. They seem to drip from the dome.

N: Muqarnas first appeared in the eastern Islamic world, around the tenth and eleventh centuries. They spread from Iran to Morocco. Built from a small set of standard shapes, they turn a dome into something like a sky of stars. That was exactly the effect the Nasrids wanted over a ruler's head.

...

A: Often told, though we could not confirm it. A recent documentary describes the method. Seven or eight basic prism shapes were cast in plaster moulds. The pieces were trimmed, polished, and fixed one by one with plaster paste, working upward from the walls. It is hard to imagine without a written plan, yet no Nasrid drawings survive.

...

N: Last, the writing. Look for a short phrase in a neat frame, repeated again and again. It is the Nasrid motto, wa-la ghaliba illa Allah. It means, There is no victor but God.

N: You will see it so often you stop counting. Nobody has published a reliable total.

...

A: You will hear one figure most of all. Over nine thousand times. Treat it as folklore until someone publishes a count.

A: The story goes that the motto began with the dynasty's founder, Muhammad the First. Coming home from a campaign, he was hailed as victor. He answered that there is no victor but God.

A: His title really was al-Ghalib billah. But the scene itself is tradition, and accounts disagree on when and where it happened.

...

N: Around the motto run verses from the Qur'an, and poems by court poets who were also statesmen.

N: Ibn Zamrak wrote the poems on the Comares facade and the fountain of the Lions. Ibn al-Jayyab wrote verses in the Generalife and the Torre de la Cautiva. A poem in the Throne Room is credited either to him or to Ibn al-Khatib.

N: Often the building speaks for itself, in the first person. One window says, I am the fresh eye of this garden.

...

A: One myth to drop now. You will hear that Nasrid art shows no living creatures. Most of the ornament is abstract. But wait until you meet twelve stone lions, and a ceiling full of painted kings, knights and ladies.

...
