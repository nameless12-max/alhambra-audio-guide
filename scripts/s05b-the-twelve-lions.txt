# title: Stop 5, continued. The twelve lions
# stop: 5

N: Stop five, continued. The twelve lions.

N: You are standing at the fountain in the centre of the court. Twelve stone lions stand in a ring, facing outward. On their backs they carry a twelve-sided basin. Walk slowly once around them.

...

N: Why lions? Ibn Zamrak's poem on the rim gives the answer. It likens the flowing water to the ruler's generous hand, and the lions to his warriors. They look ready to pounce, the poem says, but respect for the emir holds back their fury.

N: So the fountain is a portrait of power. Wild strength, tamed and in the sultan's service, pours out water to all four sides of his garden. On a dry hill, water was the ultimate luxury.

...

N: The idea had deep roots in al-Andalus, as Muslim Spain was called. Four centuries earlier, the Umayyad caliphs of Córdoba built a palace-city, Madinat al-Zahra. There, a marble basin was ringed by twelve gold animal figures. A lion was among them.

N: Muhammad the Fifth liked the motif. In thirteen sixty-seven he gave his new hospital in the city below, the Maristán, a pair of lion water-spouts. Those two are now in the museum you will visit later. Twelve stand here.

N: Why twelve? No Nasrid text says. The number matches the twelve-sided basin and that old model from Córdoba. Readings of the lions as the zodiac or the months of the year have no evidence behind them.

...

A: Often told, though thinly sourced, is that the lions fall into three carving styles, four lions in each. Look at the manes, the muzzles and the tails, and judge for yourself.

...

N: Now listen. Restorers found that the water once rose through a pierced stone cylinder at the centre. It slipped out of the lions' mouths almost silently. It was more like a spring than a fountain. That matches the poem's picture of water so clear and still that it seems solid.

...

N: The fountain has had a busy afterlife. An upper basin was added in sixteen twenty-six. It now stands in the Jardín de los Adarves, a garden in the Alcazaba, where you will see it later. A jet of water rose from the top from eighteen thirty-five until nineteen sixty-six.

N: During Napoleon's occupation of Granada, from eighteen ten to eighteen twelve, a French commander planted a flower garden in this court. It was removed in eighteen forty-six, because the damp threatened the building.

N: In two thousand and seven the lions were taken away for restoration, while the original water system was rebuilt. They came back at the end of two thousand and eleven. The restored fountain was unveiled in July twenty twelve.

...

N: Most scholars date the lions to the reign of Muhammad the Fifth.

A: You will hear that the lions are older than this palace. A theory published in nineteen fifty-six linked them to the Naghrela family. Samuel ibn Naghrela, and then his son Joseph, were Jewish viziers to the Zirid kings of Granada in the ten hundreds. Joseph was killed in the massacre of ten sixty-six.

A: The theory held that the lions came from Joseph's palace. It rested on a poem by Solomon ibn Gabirol. The poem describes a basin resting on lions, like the bronze sea carried by twelve oxen in Solomon's Temple. It is a beautiful idea, but it is disputed. The carving is fourteenth-century Nasrid work, and most scholars have set it aside.

A: You may also hear that the fountain worked as a water clock. That is a guess with no documents behind it. And one more story we could not confirm, that a traveller in fourteen ninety-four counted thirteen lions here.

...

N: When you are ready, follow the channel that runs toward the south side of the court. If you came in from the west, that is on your right. Go through the doorway into the square hall. That is stop six.
