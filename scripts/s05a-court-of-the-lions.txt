# title: Stop 5. The Court of the Lions
# stop: 5

N: Stop five. The Court of the Lions.

N: You begin in the Sala de los Mocárabes, a long entrance hall. Ahead of you, through the arches, is the brightest court in the palace. Before you go in, look up.

...

N: The vault above you is plaster. It replaced the original Nasrid honeycomb vaulting, called muqarnas in Arabic. Its Spanish name, Mocárabes, gives this hall its name. The original was wrecked by the same explosion of fifteen ninety that blew out the glass of the throne room.

A: You will hear different dates for this plaster vault. Some sources say seventeen fourteen, others the sixteen hundreds. Nobody has settled it.

...

N: Now walk into the court. Stand under the arcade at the near end, the west end. Line yourself up with the fountain and the far pavilion.

...

N: This was the private palace of Muhammad the Fifth, begun around thirteen seventy-seven beside the official palace you have just left. The court is about thirty-five metres long, and slightly out of square. A pavilion projects into it at each end. One is behind you. The other faces you across the fountain.

A: The palace is thought to have been called Qasr al-Riyad al-Sa'id, the Palace of the Happy Garden. That name is often given, but thinly sourced.

...

N: Think back to the Court of the Myrtles. There you had solid walls and still water. Here you have thin columns and moving water. Listen for it.

...

N: The Myrtles runs north to south. This court runs east to west, probably because it had to fit between buildings, streets and gardens that were already here. Yet its two grandest halls still face each other on the north to south line. The Dos Hermanas is on your left. The Abencerrajes is on your right.

...

N: Now look down at the paving. Four channels run in from the surrounding halls and meet at the fountain, dividing the court into quarters. They are usually read as the four rivers of Paradise. A visitor in fifteen oh two saw six orange trees here, and the rest paved in marble.

...

N: Now look at the columns. There are one hundred and twenty-four of them, in white marble from Macael, in the mountains of Almería. Those mountains were then part of the kingdom.

N: Now find a corner of the court. There the columns bunch together in twos, threes and fours. The grouping breaks the rhythm of the arcades, so the court reads less like a building and more like a grove of trees.

...

A: Some writers find hidden geometry in the plan, based on the square root of two. Others say the deep eaves block the high summer sun but let the low winter sun into the halls. Both claims are often told, and thinly sourced.

...

N: The fountain carries a poem, carved around the rim of its basin. It is by Ibn Zamrak, the court poet of Muhammad the Fifth. His verses say that water and marble are so alike you can hardly tell which one is flowing. They compare the sultan's generosity to water soaking a garden.

N: The poem also explains the lions. You will hear how in the next track.

...

N: When you are ready, walk along the channel to the fountain in the centre, and stand by the rim. Then start the next track.
