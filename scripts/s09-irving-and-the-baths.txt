# title: Stop 9. Patio de la Reja, Patio de Lindaraja and the exit
# stop: 9

N: Stop nine. The Patio de la Reja, the Patio de Lindaraja, and the way out.

N: From the Mirador, the route leads along a corridor. It passes a small courtyard, the Patio de la Reja, and then goes down into a garden courtyard, the Patio de Lindaraja. You can listen as you walk.

...

N: In the Patio de la Reja, look for a balcony facing the Albaicín and Sacromonte, if it is open on your route.

N: The rooms along this corridor are not Nasrid. From fifteen twenty-eight, Charles the Fifth built new rooms here, along a passage linking the Comares palace and the Lions palace. These are the Emperor's Chambers.

N: Around fifteen thirty-seven, two painters trained in Raphael's workshop decorated the so-called fruit rooms. Their names were Julio Aquiles and Alejandro Mayner.

N: The chambers are normally closed. They open only now and then, as the Space of the Month. If the doors are open today, it is worth the detour.

...

N: Three centuries after Charles, a famous tenant moved in. Washington Irving lived in these rooms from the fourth of May to the twenty-ninth of July, eighteen twenty-nine. A marble plaque on the door of his rooms remembers him. It was placed there in nineteen fourteen.

N: When Irving arrived, the Alhambra was half abandoned. It was a barracks for invalid soldiers, at times a prison, and home to squatters and Gitano families.

N: His Tales of the Alhambra came out in eighteen thirty-two, and it made the world look again. Restoration began in earnest in the eighteen forties. In eighteen seventy the Alhambra was declared a National Monument.

N: Long before Irving, these palaces were also a royal childhood home. Catherine of Aragon, later the first queen of Henry the Eighth, spent part of her childhood here. Sources differ on when her years here began.

N: She left in fifteen oh one to marry Prince Arthur of England. Her badge was the pomegranate, the emblem of Granada.

...

N: Close by, at the top of a tower, is a small room called the Peinador de la Reina, the Queen's Dressing Room. It is usually closed too. Its frescoes, painted in the fifteen forties, show Charles the Fifth's expedition against Tunis.

A: You will also hear that the Peinador was redecorated for the Empress Isabella in the fifteen hundreds. That is often told, but thinly sourced.

...

N: Now you are in the Patio de Lindaraja. Turn around and look back up.

...

N: Above you is the Mirador, the window bay you just crouched in. Now you see it from the garden it was named for. This is the garden that Charles's new rooms closed in.

N: Stairs from this patio lead to the Comares Baths. Like the Emperor's Chambers, they are normally closed and open only now and then.

N: If you do get inside, look up. The vaults are pierced with star-shaped skylights. They let the steam out and the air in.

...

A: Two things you will hear about these baths. First, that the resting hall is called the Sala de las Camas, the Hall of the Beds. The name comes from its two raised benches, where bathers undressed and rested.

A: Second, that musicians played to the bathers from the gallery of that resting hall. They are often said to have been blind. Both details are thinly sourced.

...

N: You leave the palaces from this garden, through a gate into the Partal gardens. If the exit has been moved, follow the staff.

N: Once you pass the gate, you cannot come back in. So take a last look. Listen for the water, and look up once more at the Mirador.

...

N: When you are ready, go through the gate and out into the gardens. That is stop ten.
