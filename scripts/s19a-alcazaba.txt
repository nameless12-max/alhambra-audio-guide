# title: Stop 19. The Alcazaba
# stop: 19

N: Stop nineteen. The Alcazaba.

N: You are at the entrance to the Alcazaba, the fortress on the western tip of the hill. Follow the marked route through it.

...

N: This is where the Alhambra began. Muhammad the First, founder of the Nasrid dynasty, started building here in twelve thirty-eight, on the site of an older castle.

N: In the eleventh century, Granada was ruled by the Zirid kings. Their chief ministers were a Jewish father and son, Samuel ibn Naghrela and Joseph. Joseph was killed in ten sixty-six, in a massacre of the Jews of Granada.

...

A: A disputed point. One scholar placed Joseph's palace here, on this hill. Not everyone accepts it.

...

N: Put your hand on the wall if you can reach it. This is rammed earth. Look for rows of small square holes. Builders packed earth, lime and gravel into wooden frames, one layer at a time. The holes mark where the crossbeams of those frames sat.

N: These plain, thick walls are the reason the palaces could be all lace and light.

...

N: Walk into the Jardín de los Adarves, the narrow garden along the southern ramparts.

...

N: This garden was once a moat, between the outer and inner walls. In the sixteen hundreds the governor, the Marquis of Mondéjar, had it filled and planted. The Vase of the Gazelles stood here once. So did the upper basin of the Lions fountain. Find that basin now.

N: Walk on to the west end of the garden, to the Torre de la Pólvora, the Powder Tower. Look for a plaque on its wall.

...

N: The lines on it are by the Mexican poet Francisco de Icaza. In English they read: Give him alms, woman, for there is nothing in life like the grief of being blind in Granada.

...

A: A local legend says this garden was paid for with gold coins, found hidden in jars inside the walls. It is only a legend.

...

N: Now follow the route into the Plaza de Armas, the parade ground. Look down at the low walls at your feet.

...

N: These are the foundations of the Barrio Castrense, the soldiers' quarter. Trace the outlines of houses, a bakery, a bath, a cistern and workshops. Below them lie silos and dungeons, cut underground.

N: At the eastern end stands the keep, the Torre del Homenaje. It guarded the main entrance. It is about twenty-six metres high, with five vaulted floors. Tradition says the founder lived on its top floor.

N: At the western prow stands the Torre de la Vela. These two are the oldest towers of the fortress. Vela means watch, and that was its job. After the earthquakes it may be closed, so follow the signs, and climb it if it is open.

...

N: Look up at its bell. Bell registers date the present bell to seventeen seventy-three. It seems to have been set in its present position in eighteen forty, and it was rebuilt after lightning damage in eighteen eighty-two.

N: The bell told farmers on the plain below when their turn came to take irrigation water at night. It also served as a clock and an alarm. Today it rings only on set days, such as the second of January. Folklore says that a single woman who rings it on that day will marry within the year.

N: The gunpowder blast in the Darro valley in fifteen ninety damaged this tower too. It was repaired the same year.

...

A: A few more things you will hear, all thinly sourced. After the conquest, the Count of Tendilla is said to have adapted the fortress for cannon. The rounded bastion by the Powder Tower is the usual example. Another tower, the Torre de las Armas, is said to have linked the fortress to the Albaicín by a bridge over the river. We could not confirm these.

...

N: Now walk out onto the ramparts, or the terrace of the Puerta de las Armas, and look out.

...

N: Across the Darro valley, on the next hill, is the old quarter of the Albaicín. To the west, the city spreads out into the Vega, the farming plain. On a clear day, turn around. The Sierra Nevada rises behind you.

N: Stay here on the ramparts for the next track. That is stop nineteen, continued.
