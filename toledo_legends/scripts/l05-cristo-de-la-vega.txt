# title: Stop 5. Cristo de la Vega: the witness
# stop: 5
# room: Ermita del Cristo de la Vega, outside the walls on the plain below the north-west of the old town

## A promise at the foot of the cross

N: Stop five. The little church of the Cristo de la Vega stands outside the city walls, on the open plain below the north-west of the old town, a walk downhill from the Cambrón gate. At night it is closed and the gates are locked. If the tour comes here, you will see a white chapel behind a wall, with the dark city rising behind it.

...

N: Inside, out of sight tonight, is a carved Christ on the cross. And something is wrong with him. One arm is not nailed to the cross. It hangs down, open, as if reaching for something.

N: Why? Here is the story. A Spanish poet, José Zorrilla, made it famous almost two hundred years ago, from an old Toledan tradition.

## Diego and Inés

A: Inés de Vargas loves a young man, Diego Martínez. He loves her, or says he does. But he has to go to war in Flanders, to make his name.

A: Before he goes, she brings him here. To this Christ. And in front of the cross, Diego swears. When I come back, I will marry you.

...

A: Three years pass. No letter. Then one day, soldiers come marching home through the city gate, and Inés runs to look. And there he is. Diego. A captain now, made by the king.

A: She runs to him. And he looks at her as if she were a stranger. He says: I never promised you anything.

## The witness

A: Inés goes to court. The judge asks her: who saw this promise? Was there a witness? And she says: yes. There was one. The Christ of the Vega.

A: So the judge, and the lawyers, and the whole city, come down to this church. And before the crucifix, the judge asks. Do you swear that this man promised to marry this woman?

...

A: And a voice comes from the cross. Yes. I swear it.

A: And in the popular version, the Christ tears his right hand free of the nail, and lowers it, as if laying it on a book to take the oath. And it never went back.

N: Zorrilla named his poem after an old Spanish saying. A buen juez, mejor testigo. To a good judge, a better witness.

...

N: And Diego and Inés? Zorrilla does not give them a wedding. Shaken by what they had seen, both turned away from the world, and spent their lives in religious houses.

## What is true

N: This is legend. Zorrilla's poem tells of a voice. The lowered arm belongs to the popular telling, and the figure inside today is said to be a later replacement.

N: But think of her. A woman with no proof, against a captain of the king. And the one witness she had hung on a cross.

## Walk on

N: Next, a poet who walked these streets at night, and a soldier who kissed the wrong statue. That is stop six.
