# Toledo_Legends tour: full text


4215 words, about 35 minutes with pauses.


## Toledo after dark: the legends · Room Anywhere in the old town, before the tour

*473 words, about 3.4 min*


### A city that keeps its secrets

Good evening, Monica. The sun has gone down behind the river, the day crowds have gone home, and Toledo is starting to change.

You have seen this city by daylight. Churches, synagogues, a cathedral, the old mosque. Christians, Jews and Muslims, living side by side on one rock above the Tagus for centuries.

Tonight is for the other Toledo. The one that people whisper about.

For hundreds of years, all over Europe, this city's name meant magic. Students were said to come here to learn to raise the dead. Kings were said to lose their kingdoms here by opening the wrong door. Statues here move. Crucifixes speak. A wooden man once walked these streets.

Why here? Think about where you are standing. A maze of lanes too narrow for the moon. Three faiths, each with its own scholars and its own secrets. Libraries full of books in languages most of Europe could not read. A city like that breeds stories after dark.


### A Toledan night

The Spanish have a phrase for a night when nobody sleeps. Una noche toledana. A Toledan night.

One story behind the phrase is the darkest in the city. Listen.

> *Legend voice:* More than a thousand years ago, Toledo kept rebelling against the emir in far-off Córdoba. So the emir sent a new governor, a man named Amrus. He announced a great feast to welcome the prince, and invited the leading men of Toledo to his palace. They came in, one by one, through a single gate. And one by one, as they entered, they were killed. Their bodies went into a pit dug in secret. Hundreds of them. Toledo did not sleep that night. And it did not rebel again for a long time.

Arab chroniclers tell this massacre as history, and some scholars doubt it. And the phrase may come from something smaller. An old dictionary from four centuries ago blamed it on the mosquitoes, which ate visitors alive in the summer heat.


### How to use these tracks

At seven, you join the legends tour. It will be in Spanish.

Each track here tells you one story in English, before you get there. Listen over a drink, or standing at the place itself. Then, when the guide stops, drops their voice and points, you will know exactly what they are pointing at. You will know when the horse kneels, and when the statue moves.

One promise. When I tell you a legend, you will hear it in another voice. When I tell you what really happened, it will be me. Keep them apart, and enjoy both.

Some of the places are shut at night, and you will only see their doors and walls. That is how legends should be met. From outside, in the dark.


### Walk on

Start with the little mosque by the old gate, and a horse that would not take another step. That is stop one.


## Stop 1. Cristo de la Luz: the horse that knelt · Room Mosque of Cristo de la Luz, near the Puerta del Sol gate, north side of the old town

*474 words, about 3.4 min*


### The horse that would not move

Stop one. The small brick mosque of Cristo de la Luz stands on a steep lane just inside the old walls, near the Puerta del Sol gate. It closes before dark, so tonight you will see it from outside: a little square building of red brick, with arches over arches in its walls.

It is older than almost anything around you. An inscription on its front gives the year it was finished, nine hundred and ninety-nine. A thousand years ago, this was a neighbourhood mosque.

Now the story. Picture a spring day, almost a century later. Toledo has opened its gates to a Christian king.

> *Legend voice:* King Alfonso rides in at the head of his army, in triumph, up this very slope. Beside him rides his greatest knight, they say, the Cid. And then, here, outside this little mosque, the king's horse stops.

> *Legend voice:* It will not go on. The king spurs it. Nothing. And then, in front of the whole army, the horse slowly bends its front legs, and kneels in the street. Facing the wall of the mosque.

Why would a horse kneel to a wall? Hold that question.


### The light in the wall

> *Legend voice:* The king orders the wall opened. The soldiers strike at the stones. And behind them they find a hollow, a hidden niche. Inside it, a crucifix. And beside the crucifix, an oil lamp. Still burning.

> *Legend voice:* Here is the secret, as the legend tells it. Nearly four hundred years before, when the Muslim armies came, the Christians of Toledo hid their Christ in this wall to save it, and lit a lamp beside it. They sealed it up. They died. Nobody remembered. And all that time, in the dark, the little flame kept burning. Waiting for someone to come back for it.

That is why the building is called Christ of the Light. And they say the king held the first Christian Mass here, and left his shield behind, with a cross on it, hanging in the church.

Now the history. The king was real, and he did take Toledo in ten eighty-five. The mosque really did become a chapel. But the legend appears only after the conquest, told by the winners. Some tellings even forget the lamp. Others put the Cid on the horse. The kneeling horse belongs to the story, not the record.


### The stone in the street

When you are outside, look down at the paving in front of the building. Toledans say one white stone marks the spot where the horse went down on its knees. Look for it with the guide's torch.

And stand a moment with this. The lamp of the legend burned for a faith that was hidden. The building that kept it was built by another faith. Whose light is it?


### Walk on

Next, down under the streets near the cathedral, to a palace no king was ever meant to open. That is stop two.


## Stop 2. The Cave of Hercules: the king who broke the locks · Room Cueva de Hércules, Callejón de San Ginés, a few minutes from the cathedral

*477 words, about 3.4 min*


### The house of many locks

Stop two. The Cave of Hercules is under a narrow alley called Callejón de San Ginés, a short walk from the cathedral. It opens only for a few hours in the daytime, so tonight you will stand at a plain door in the alley and look down, in your mind, into the dark.

> *Legend voice:* Long ago, they say, the hero Hercules came to Spain and founded this city. He was strong, but he was also a magician. And under this hill he built an enchanted palace. Inside, he left a secret. Then he sealed the door with a lock.

> *Legend voice:* Every king who ruled in Toledo after him came here and added one more lock. Century after century, the door grew heavier with iron.

Until a king came who wanted to know. What could be worth so many locks?


### Rodrigo opens the door

> *Legend voice:* His name was Rodrigo, the last king of the Goths. He believed there was treasure inside. His lords begged him not to. He broke the locks anyway, one after another, and walked in with a torch.

> *Legend voice:* There was no gold. There was only a chest. And in the chest, a folded cloth. He unfolded it. Painted on it were horsemen he had never seen. Strange faces. Turbans on their heads. Bows and lances in their hands. And words beneath them. Whoever opens this, men like these will take his kingdom.

Imagine him holding that cloth. The torch shaking. Every lock on the floor around him, broken by his own hand.

> *Legend voice:* Within a year, the riders came. They crossed the sea from Africa, and Rodrigo rode out to meet them, and he was never seen again. The Goths' Spain was gone.


### The girl by the river

> *Legend voice:* And why did they come? Toledo has a second story for that. Go down to the river, by the old bridge of San Martín, and you will find a ruined tower called the Bath of La Cava. They say that from it, King Rodrigo watched a young girl bathing in the Tagus. Florinda, the daughter of Count Julian. He wanted her, and he took her. Her father, who guarded the strait for the king, swore revenge. And he opened the way across the sea for the invaders.

Now the history. Rodrigo was real. He really was the last Gothic king, and he really did lose his kingdom in battle in seven hundred and eleven. The locked palace, the painted riders and Florinda are legend, written down by chroniclers long after.

And the cave itself? Archaeologists say it is a Roman water tank, part of the system that fed the city. The tower by the river is the stump of a medieval bridge.

Stand at the door and ask yourself the king's question. Would you have opened it?


### Walk on

Next, the city of magicians, and the man who could make you live a whole life in an afternoon. That is stop three.


## Stop 3. Toledo, school of magic: Don Illán and the Dean · Room Old town lanes; the guide may stop near the cathedral or a cellar

*476 words, about 3.4 min*


### The city where they taught the dark arts

Stop three. This story has no single address. The tour may tell it in a lane near the cathedral, or at the mouth of a cellar.

In the Middle Ages, across Europe, if a man said he had studied in Toledo, people crossed themselves. The Toledan arts meant necromancy. Students were said to learn it in caves beneath this city.

Here is the real root of the rumour. After the Christian conquest, scholars came here from all over Europe. Toledo had what no one else had: Arabic books on stars, medicine, mathematics, the Greek philosophers. Christians, Jews and Muslims sat together and turned those books into Latin. Astronomy looked like sorcery to people who could not read it. So Toledo became the city of magicians.


### The Dean and the magician

A Spanish prince wrote the most famous of these stories seven centuries ago. Listen.

> *Legend voice:* A churchman, the Dean of Santiago, rides all the way to Toledo. He has heard of a master of magic, Don Illán. He begs to be taught. Don Illán looks at him and says: men like you rise high, and then forget who helped them. The Dean swears he will never forget.

> *Legend voice:* So Don Illán agrees. And he calls to his housekeeper. Roast us two partridges for supper. But do not cook them until I tell you.

> *Legend voice:* Then, the lesson begins. Messengers arrive. The Dean's uncle, the archbishop, is dead. The Dean is made bishop. Then archbishop. Then cardinal, in Rome. Then Pope. Years pass. And at every step, Don Illán asks one small favour. A post for his son. And every time, the Dean says: not yet. Next time.

> *Legend voice:* At last, the Pope loses patience. He calls his old teacher a heretic and a sorcerer, and threatens him with prison. He will not even give him food for the road home.

> *Legend voice:* Don Illán says quietly: then I will eat the partridges I ordered tonight. And he calls out to his housekeeper.

> *Legend voice:* And the Dean is sitting in a cellar in Toledo. The same evening. The same chair. No Rome. No crown. The partridges are still raw. It was all a spell, to see who he really was.

He was ashamed, says the book, and did not know what to say. Don Illán sent him home without his lesson.


### The table of Solomon

> *Legend voice:* One more treasure belongs to this magic city. They say the conquerors of Toledo found here the table of King Solomon, made of gold and jewels, and on it the secret name of God. Two generals fought over it. One broke off a leg to prove he found it first.

That is legend too. Arab chroniclers wrote it down, and the table has never been found.

Every one of us has made the Dean's promise to someone. Would you have kept it?


### Walk on

Next, down to a quiet street and a well with bitter water. That is stop four.


## Stop 4. The Bitter Well: Raquel and Fernando · Room Calle del Pozo Amargo, a small lane in the south of the old town

*446 words, about 3.2 min*


### A garden with a well

Stop four. The Bitter Well gives its name to a short street, Calle del Pozo Amargo, in the south of the old town, towards the river. The well is in a tiny square, open to the street, so you can see it at any hour. Look for a low stone ring, closed over, and a plaque on the wall.

Picture this lane when it was a garden behind a wall. A well in the middle, with water so sweet the neighbours came for it. And a girl who came to the well every evening.

> *Legend voice:* Her name was Raquel. She was the daughter of a Jewish family, the most beautiful girl in Toledo, they say. And one evening, at the garden wall, a young Christian man named Fernando saw her. And she saw him.

> *Legend voice:* Nothing could have been more dangerous. A Jewish girl and a Christian boy. In this city, the three faiths lived side by side. But they did not marry.

So what can two people do, when they cannot be seen together? Hold on to that.


### Meeting in the dark

> *Legend voice:* They meet at the well. At night. When the house is asleep, Raquel slips out to the garden, and Fernando climbs the wall. They talk across the stone ring of the well, in whispers. Every night. For months.

> *Legend voice:* And then her father finds out. He does not shout. He does not lock her in. He does something worse. He pays a man with a knife.

> *Legend voice:* The next night, Fernando does not come. Raquel waits by the well until dawn. And the next night. And the next.

> *Legend voice:* She never learned what happened. Or maybe she did. She sat by that well and wept, night after night, and her tears fell into the water. And the water turned bitter. From then on, nobody could drink from it.

> *Legend voice:* Some say that one night, looking down into the well, she saw his face in the water, and leaned in to reach him. And did not come back up.


### What is true

This is a legend, and it changes with every teller. Even the father's name is different from one book to the next. No record shows that Raquel or Fernando ever lived.

But the well is real. A medieval well from a Jewish house. Its stone ring was sealed in the nineteen sixties, and you can still see it here. And the street still carries the name of bitter water.

Put your hand on the stone if you can. Then ask: what would you have done, if you were Raquel, and he never came?


### Walk on

Next, out beyond the walls, to a Christ who came down from his cross to keep a promise. That is stop five.


## Stop 5. Cristo de la Vega: the witness · Room Ermita del Cristo de la Vega, outside the walls on the plain below the north-west of the old town

*465 words, about 3.3 min*


### A promise at the foot of the cross

Stop five. The little church of the Cristo de la Vega stands outside the city walls, on the open plain below the north-west of the old town, a walk downhill from the Cambrón gate. At night it is closed and the gates are locked. If the tour comes here, you will see a white chapel behind a wall, with the dark city rising behind it.

Inside, out of sight tonight, is a carved Christ on the cross. And something is wrong with him. One arm is not nailed to the cross. It hangs down, open, as if reaching for something.

Why? Here is the story. A Spanish poet, José Zorrilla, made it famous almost two hundred years ago, from an old Toledan tradition.


### Diego and Inés

> *Legend voice:* Inés de Vargas loves a young man, Diego Martínez. He loves her, or says he does. But he has to go to war in Flanders, to make his name.

> *Legend voice:* Before he goes, she brings him here. To this Christ. And in front of the cross, Diego swears. When I come back, I will marry you.

> *Legend voice:* Three years pass. No letter. Then one day, soldiers come marching home through the city gate, and Inés runs to look. And there he is. Diego. A captain now, made by the king.

> *Legend voice:* She runs to him. And he looks at her as if she were a stranger. He says: I never promised you anything.


### The witness

> *Legend voice:* Inés goes to court. The judge asks her: who saw this promise? Was there a witness? And she says: yes. There was one. The Christ of the Vega.

> *Legend voice:* So the judge, and the lawyers, and the whole city, come down to this church. And before the crucifix, the judge asks. Do you swear that this man promised to marry this woman?

> *Legend voice:* And a voice comes from the cross. Yes. I swear it.

> *Legend voice:* And in the popular version, the Christ tears his right hand free of the nail, and lowers it, as if laying it on a book to take the oath. And it never went back.

Zorrilla named his poem after an old Spanish saying. A buen juez, mejor testigo. To a good judge, a better witness.

And Diego and Inés? Zorrilla does not give them a wedding. Shaken by what they had seen, both turned away from the world, and spent their lives in religious houses.


### What is true

This is legend. Zorrilla's poem tells of a voice. The lowered arm belongs to the popular telling, and the figure inside today is said to be a later replacement.

But think of her. A woman with no proof, against a captain of the king. And the one witness she had hung on a cross.


### Walk on

Next, a poet who walked these streets at night, and a soldier who kissed the wrong statue. That is stop six.


## Stop 6. Bécquer: The Kiss · Room Old town lanes near the convent of San Clemente, north-west of the old town

*474 words, about 3.4 min*


### The poet in the dark streets

Stop six. This is a story by a poet, so it has no fixed stop. But the poet does have an address. Gustavo Adolfo Bécquer lived on Calle de San Ildefonso, a quiet street near the convent of San Clemente, in the north-west of the old town. If the tour passes San Clemente, look at the church doorway. Restorers cleaning it recently found a signature in pencil, thought to be his.

Bécquer was Spain's great romantic poet, a young man from Seville. He loved Toledo at night. He walked these lanes after dark, alone, sketching ruined churches with his brother, the painter Valeriano. And out of those walks came his legends.


### The French captain

> *Legend voice:* It is the time of the war against Napoleon. French dragoons ride into Toledo, and there is no room left for them. So they are put up in an old, half-ruined church. Their horses stamp in the chapels.

> *Legend voice:* Their captain is young, handsome and bored. And one night, in the dark church, he finds something.

> *Legend voice:* On a tomb, by the light of his lamp, kneel two figures carved in stone. A knight in armour. And beside him, his wife. Doña Elvira. Her hands joined in prayer. And she is so beautiful that the captain cannot stop looking at her.

What happens when a living man falls in love with a woman of stone? Hold on.


### The supper in the church

> *Legend voice:* He comes back every night. He tells his fellow officers he has found a lady in Toledo, the loveliest in the city, and they laugh at him. So he invites them to supper. In the church.

> *Legend voice:* They light a fire with wood hacked from the carved choir stalls. They drink. They toast the lady. And the captain shows them his lady, kneeling in stone.

> *Legend voice:* The captain is drunk now. He mocks the stone knight beside her, the husband who cannot defend her. He raises his glass to him, and throws the wine in his face. And then he says: I will kiss her.

> *Legend voice:* He climbs up to the tomb. He leans towards that cold stone face. His lips come close.

> *Legend voice:* And the officers hear a terrible sound. A cry, and the crash of a body on stone.

> *Legend voice:* They run to him. The captain is lying at the foot of the tomb, blood running from his eyes, his nose, his mouth. The stone knight has struck him down, with one terrible blow of his stone gauntlet.


### What is true

The war was real. French soldiers were quartered in Toledo's convents, and some did terrible damage. But the church in the story, and the lovers on the tomb, are Bécquer's invention.

Walk on knowing this: a husband, carved in stone, still keeping watch over his wife. Who would you trust to guard you, after you were gone?


### Walk on

Next, a short street, a clockmaker, and a man made of wood. That is stop seven.


## Stop 7. Street of the Wooden Man: Juanelo's automaton · Room Calle del Hombre de Palo, a short street near the cathedral

*474 words, about 3.4 min*


### A man who was not a man

Stop seven. The Calle del Hombre de Palo, the Street of the Wooden Man, is a short lane of about a hundred metres near the cathedral. It is open at all hours. Look for the street sign, and for a plaque that remembers who once walked here.

Picture this lane four and a half centuries ago. Morning. Shopkeepers opening their shutters. And down the middle of the street, stiff and slow, walks a man made of wood.

> *Legend voice:* He bows to the people he passes. He carries a bowl. At the archbishop's palace, they say, he stops, and someone fills the bowl with food.

Who made him? And why would a machine beg for bread? Hold on to that.


### The emperor's clockmaker

His maker was real. Juanelo Turriano, an Italian clockmaker, the greatest of his age. He built marvellous clocks for the emperor Charles the Fifth, and stayed in Spain to serve his son.

And here in Toledo he did something nobody thought possible. The city sits high on its rock, and the river runs far below. Water had to be carried up by donkey. Juanelo built a vast machine of wood and metal that lifted the water of the Tagus all the way up to the Alcázar, the fortress at the top of the hill. It was finished in fifteen sixty-nine.

But here is the turn. He was owed money for it, and he was not paid in full. The great engineer grew old and poor.

> *Legend voice:* And so, the legend says, he built one last machine. A wooden servant, to walk out each day and fetch what his master could no longer pay for. The people of Toledo grew fond of it. They dropped coins in its bowl. And they named the street after it.


### What is true

Juanelo, his water machine, and his money troubles are history. He died in Toledo in fifteen eighty-five. The walking wooden man is legend. Some writers think there was only a wooden figure standing still, collecting alms. Others believe he really did build moving figures, which would not be surprising for a man who made clocks that showed the heavens.

Even then, Toledans whispered that a man who could make water climb a mountain must be a magician.


### The devil's alley

> *Legend voice:* One more street, if the tour takes you near Zocodover, the main square. There is a narrow, dark alley there called the Alley of the Devil. They say it is named for old stories of witchcraft and pacts with the devil. And on windy nights, they say, you can still hear whispering between the walls.

That one, nobody has ever proved. Listen anyway.

Think of the old man. A genius, forgotten. And his last invention is a servant who never leaves him. What would you build, if you could not ask anyone for help?


### Walk on

One more story for the walk home. That is stop eight.


## Stop 8. The Christ of the Skull: a walk home, and goodnight · Room Any dark lane on the way home; Toledans place it near Calle de las Bulas

*456 words, about 3.3 min*


### A glove on the floor

Stop eight, the last one. Listen to this on the way back, in any dark lane. Toledans place the story near Calle de las Bulas, in the south-west of the old town, where a small Christ once hung under an archway, lit by a little lamp, with a skull at his feet.

It is Bécquer again. And it begins with a party.

> *Legend voice:* The king is about to ride to war against the Moors. On the last night, there is a great feast at the Alcázar. And the most beautiful woman at court, Doña Inés, drops her glove. A perfumed glove, with gold buttons.

> *Legend voice:* Two knights reach for it at the same moment. Two old friends, born in Toledo, who learned to fight side by side. Now they are rivals for the same woman. The king has to step between them.

> *Legend voice:* So they agree to settle it that night. With swords. They walk through the sleeping city to the only light they can find. The little lamp in front of the Christ of the Skull.


### The lamp

> *Legend voice:* They draw. And the moment their blades touch, the lamp goes out. Total darkness.

> *Legend voice:* They step apart. And the lamp lights again, by itself.

> *Legend voice:* They try again. Steel touches steel. Darkness. They part. Light.

> *Legend voice:* And then a voice. From somewhere in the dark lane. Low, strange. They cannot understand a single word. But both men freeze, and their swords fall from their hands.

What does a man do, when heaven itself refuses to light his duel? Hold on.


### The balcony

> *Legend voice:* They make peace. And together they walk to her house. They are below her balcony as the sky turns grey.

> *Legend voice:* And at dawn, a rope comes down from that balcony. And down the rope climbs a man. A third man. And Doña Inés leans out to wave him goodbye.

> *Legend voice:* The two knights look at each other. And they burst out laughing. Friends again. The next day, as the army rides out, Doña Inés sees them laughing at her. And one tear of spite rolls down her face.

That is a legend, by a poet. Nobody fought here. But Toledans still tell it, and it is a good one to carry home through dark streets.


### Goodnight

So, Monica. Tonight you have heard a horse kneel, a king break the locks, a well turn bitter, a crucifix speak, and a stone knight strike. None of it is history. All of it is Toledo.

If the Spanish tour carried you past these doors tonight, I hope you knew exactly where you were.

Tomorrow, the tracks take you back to these places by daylight, with the real stories behind the walls.

Walk slowly. Watch the lamps. And if one of them goes out as you pass, keep walking. Goodnight.
