# Toledo_Sights tour: full text


4904 words, about 40 minutes with pauses.


## Toledo in five minutes: the city on the rock · Room Anywhere. Listen first.

*635 words, about 4.5 min*


### The rock

Welcome to Toledo, Monica. Before any church or painting, here is the story of the city itself. It takes five minutes, and everything else today will make more sense.

Start with the ground under your feet. Toledo sits on a hill of granite, and the river Tagus wraps around it on three sides, in a deep gorge. Only one side is open. Whoever held this rock was very hard to remove. So everyone wanted it.


### Four owners

The Romans took it first, about two hundred years before Christ. One of their historians called it a small city, but strong because of its position. They built baths, a circus for chariot races, and the water tanks you can still visit underground.

When Rome collapsed, a people from the north moved in. The Visigoths. They made Toledo the capital of all Spain. Their kings were crowned here, and their bishops met here to decide what the country would believe. Remember them. Their last king, Rodrigo, is waiting for you in tonight's legends.

Then, in seven hundred and eleven, an army crossed from Africa. Within a few years, Toledo was a Muslim city, and it stayed one for nearly four hundred years. The narrow twisting lanes you keep getting lost in are their street plan. The little mosque by the city gate is theirs.

You have just come from Granada, the last Muslim kingdom in Spain. Granada fell in fourteen ninety-two. Toledo was taken by a Christian king four centuries earlier, in ten eighty-five. It was the first great Muslim city to fall. And that is the reason it matters so much.


### The city that translated the world

Because the new Christian king did something unusual. He did not empty the city. Muslims stayed. Jews stayed. And so did their libraries.

Those libraries held books Europe had lost. Greek medicine, astronomy, mathematics, Aristotle. All of it kept alive in Arabic. So scholars came to Toledo from England, Italy and Germany, and sat down with Jewish and Muslim translators. A book would go from Arabic into Spanish, spoken aloud, and from Spanish into Latin, written down.

Think about that, with your own story. Knowledge passing from one language to the next, across a table, between people who prayed differently. Much of what Europe later called its Renaissance came through rooms in this town.

It was not paradise. There were taxes, quarrels, and massacres. But for a few centuries, three faiths shared this small rock, and built in each other's styles. You will see churches that look like mosques, built by Muslim craftsmen for Christian priests.


### The end, and the freeze

It ended badly. In fourteen ninety-two, the Jews of Spain were told to convert or leave. Ten years later, the Muslims were told the same. The city of three faiths became a city of one.

For a while Toledo was still the heart of an empire. The emperor Charles the Fifth, the one you saw on horseback in the Prado, held court here. Then his son moved the capital to a small town in the middle of nowhere, called Madrid.

The court left. The money left. And Toledo simply stopped. Nobody could afford to knock it down and build it again. That is why you are walking through a medieval city today. It was preserved by being forgotten.

One man arrived a few years after the court had gone. A painter from Greece, hoping to work for the king. The king tried him once, and did not like the result. So he stayed here for the rest of his life, and painted this city's priests, its noblemen and its stormy sky. You know him already. El Greco.


### Walk on

That is Toledo. Romans, Goths, Muslims, Christians and Jews, all on one rock, and then four centuries of sleep. Now choose your next track. The cathedral, Santo Tomé, or whatever is in front of you.


## Stop 1. Santo Tomé: The Burial of the Count of Orgaz · Room Church of Santo Tomé, Calle Santo Tomé

*795 words, about 5.7 min*


### A debt two hundred years old

Stop one. The church of Santo Tomé, with its brick tower. The painting has its own entrance beside the church; check at the door. Go in and stand back from it. It fills the wall.

This picture starts with money. A rich nobleman of Toledo, the lord of the town of Orgaz, loved this little parish church. When he died, in thirteen twenty-three, he left it a gift to be paid every year, from Orgaz, for ever.

For ever lasted about two centuries. Then the payments stopped. The parish priest of Santo Tomé, Andrés Núñez, went to law. He won.

And then he did something clever. He decided the whole city should see, every day, why that money was owed. He would put the nobleman's funeral on the wall, miracle and all. So he hired the foreigner Toledo was talking about. A painter from Crete. They called him the Greek. El Greco.


### The miracle

> *Legend voice:* Here is the legend, as Toledo tells it. At the funeral, as the body was about to be lowered, two saints came down from heaven. Saint Stephen, young. Saint Augustine, old and bearded. They lifted the dead man with their own hands and laid him in his tomb, while the mourners stared.

That is legend. The contract spelled it out: the miracle, the saints, and the crowd who saw it. The painter would pay for his own materials. The price would be set by valuers when it was done.

Look at the bottom half. The two saints in gold, bending under the weight of a body in black armour. How gently they hold him. The old saint cradles his shoulders. The young one holds his legs.

Now look closely at the young saint's robe, low down near his hem. There is a tiny picture painted on it. A man being stoned. That is Stephen's own death, the first Christian martyr, embroidered on the clothes he wears to bury someone else.


### The crowd is real

Look at the row of men behind. Black clothes. White ruffs. Long pale faces. They look like portraits because many of them were. Gentlemen of Toledo in El Greco's own day, not the dead man's. The painter put his neighbours at a funeral two hundred and sixty years old.

On the right, in a white robe, reading from a book, is the priest who won the lawsuit. Núñez put himself at the funeral he had fought for.

Now look above the row of faces, a little left of centre. Find a knight with a red cross on his chest, his hand raised. Just above that hand, one man is not watching the miracle. He is looking straight at you.

He is said to be El Greco himself.


### The boy

Now the lower left corner. A small boy in black, holding a torch. He points at the saints, and looks out at you.

This is the painter's son, Jorge Manuel. Look at the white handkerchief in his pocket. On it El Greco wrote his own name, and the year fifteen seventy-eight. Not the year of the painting. The year the boy was born.

El Greco probably never married the boy's mother. But he signed his greatest picture on his son, and put the child at the front, where no one could miss him.


### Up to heaven

Lift your eyes. Up the middle, an angel carries something small and pale, like a newborn baby. It is the dead man's soul, squeezed upward through a narrow passage of cloud. Look among the blessed at the top for faces in black. El Greco even placed the living king of Spain up there.


### The fight over the price

When it was finished, the valuers named a price. The parish thought it far too much. Arguments, appeals, lawyers. In the end El Greco gave in and accepted the lower valuation, and part of it was paid off against debts he owed.

That was his life in Toledo. He rented a huge set of rooms in an old palace by the Jewish quarter, more than twenty of them. He lived grandly. It is said he sometimes had musicians play while he dined. And he was always in court, suing patrons who would not pay.

> *Legend voice:* One more glimpse of him. When a fellow Greek was brought before the Inquisition in Toledo, El Greco was called in to interpret. A foreigner himself, translating for one of his own, before the most feared court in Spain.

He died in Toledo, still working, with debts unpaid. On his deathbed he gave his son the power to write his will.


### Walk on

Look back at the boy one last time. Four hundred years on, he is still pointing, still asking you to look.

Walk on, down the hill, to the great stone monastery with chains on its walls. That is stop two, San Juan de los Reyes.


## Stop 2. San Juan de los Reyes: A tomb left empty · Room Jewish quarter, Calle de los Reyes Católicos

*775 words, about 5.5 min*


### The chains

Stop two. The monastery of San Juan de los Reyes. Before you go in, stay outside. Find the long wall of pale stone facing the street, and look up.

Iron chains, hanging in loops high on the stone. Shackles. Manacles. From the street they look like decoration. They are not.

These chains were worn by Christian prisoners held in the Muslim kingdom of Granada. As the armies of Isabella and Ferdinand took its towns, one by one, the captives were set free, and their chains were sent here and hung on the wall of the queen's church.

> *Legend voice:* Which prisons, exactly? The stories differ. Some say Granada itself. Others name Málaga, or Ronda, or towns further east. What they agree on is the message. Look what we did. We brought our people home.

You have just come from Granada. Picture a man stepping out of a cell there, into the light, rubbing his wrists. His chains ended up here, as a trophy.


### A battle nobody quite won

Now hold a question as you go in. Why build a church this grand, and then not use it for the one thing it was made for?

Go back to the beginning. When Isabella claimed the crown of Castile, her claim was not safe. Her niece said the crown was hers, and the king of Portugal married that niece and marched into Castile to take it.

In fourteen seventy-six the two armies met near a town called Toro, on the river Duero. The fighting went on into the dark. On one side of the field the Portuguese did well. On the other, Ferdinand's men broke them. Both sides claimed the victory.

But Isabella understood something. A battle is won by whoever tells the story. She called it a triumph, gave thanks, and vowed a monastery. Here, in Toledo, the old heart of Castile. And it would be their tomb.


### The queen is not impressed

The architect was Juan Guas, the queen's master of works.

> *Legend voice:* Now a story Toledo loves to tell. The queen came to see how her church was going. She looked around and asked whether this trifle was what they had built her. In old Spanish, "esta nonada". A nothing. It is a story, and the words differ from one telling to the next. But the building did change. It grew richer, and grander, to match her vow.


### Read the walls

Walk up to the crossing, where the great stone arches meet. Look at the walls. They are covered in coats of arms, held up by huge stone eagles. The eagle is the sign of Saint John, whose church this is.

Now look for two small emblems, again and again. A yoke, the wooden collar for oxen. And a bundle of arrows.

The yoke was Ferdinand's sign. The arrows were Isabella's. The popular story says each picked the other's initial. Yugo, yoke, with a Y, the old spelling of Isabel. Flechas, arrows, with an F, for Fernando. Whether or not that is why they chose them, look how they are carved here. Side by side, again and again, like lovers' initials on a tree.


### The empty tomb

So why are they not here?

Because of Granada. In fourteen ninety-two the last Muslim kingdom in Spain surrendered, and the Alhambra was theirs. The queen loved that city. She decided to be buried there instead, in the place of her greatest victory.

When she died, her body was carried south and laid first in a convent inside the Alhambra itself. If you walked past the Parador hotel up there, that old convent is where she first lay. Later she and Ferdinand were moved to the Royal Chapel, beside Granada's cathedral. They are there still.

This church was left without its king and queen. A tomb with nobody in it.


### The cloister, burned and mended

Before you leave, walk into the cloister. Two floors of arches around a garden. Look up at the carving, at the beasts and odd creatures sticking out from the walls.

In eighteen hundred and eight, Napoleon's troops occupied Toledo and were quartered here. A great fire broke out. The second cloister was destroyed altogether. Much of what you see was rebuilt later, by restorers working from what survived. One wing of this cloister and the lace of stone along its top are largely theirs.

So look closely. Some faces up there were carved five centuries ago. Some, little more than one. See if you can tell which.

Stand in the garden. Think of that wall outside. The chains of men set free, hung on a church built for a queen who chose to lie somewhere else.


### Walk on

Walk on, uphill, across the old town, to the little mosque of Cristo de la Luz. That is stop three.


## Stop 3. Cristo de la Luz: The mosque by daylight · Room Near Puerta del Sol, Calle Cristo de la Luz

*660 words, about 4.7 min*


### A name in brick

Stop three. The little mosque of Cristo de la Luz, near the Sun Gate. You know its legend already, the king's horse that knelt in the street, and the lamp still burning behind the wall. Now see the building by daylight.

Start outside, on the street side. Find the front wall of narrow red bricks, with its three arched doorways. Above them, blind arches cross and weave like basketwork. Now look along the very top, just under the roof line. A band of brick letters runs across it.

That is Arabic, in an old, square, angular script. And it is a signature.

It says, in short, that a man named Ahmad ibn Hadidi had this mosque built, hoping for his reward from God. That it was finished, with God's help, under the direction of a builder, Musa ibn Ali, and a man called Saada. And it gives the date. The year Christians count as nine hundred and ninety-nine.

Think about that. One man's money. One builder's skill. A small neighbourhood mosque, at the end of the first millennium. Toledo had many like it. Almost all are gone. This is the one that survived, and it still carries the name of the man who made it.


### Borrowed columns

Now go inside. It is tiny. Four columns hold up the whole roof. Stand in the middle, between them.

Look at the tops of the columns, the capitals. They do not match, and they are rougher than the walls around them. That is because the builder did not carve them. He reused them. They came from older buildings, from the Christian Visigoths who ruled Toledo before the Muslims came. One was remade in a restoration a century ago.

So the oldest stones holding up this mosque were carved by Christians, and they would hold it up again when it became a church.


### Nine small skies

Now look up. The ceiling is divided into nine little squares, three by three. Each one has its own small dome. And each dome is made differently. Brick ribs crossing in a new pattern every time. Stars. Crosses. Grids.

Turn slowly and look at all nine. It is a builder showing off nine ways to make a sky. If you saw the great mosque of Córdoba, you will know this family of ribbed domes from the dome in front of its prayer niche. This is its small country cousin.


### The Christians add a room

Now look east, to the far end. The style changes. A rounded apse, a half-circle chamber, joined on in the same red brick, but later.

After the Christian conquest, the mosque passed to a crusading order, the Knights of Saint John, and about two centuries after it was built, they added this apse. Christian prayer had to face east, so the church grew a new head.

Look into the curved vault of the apse. Faded paintings, patchy. At the centre was Christ in majesty, seated in glory. Much of it has flaked away. Look for an outline, a hand, a halo. One building, joined at the seam. Muslim domes on one side. Christian saints on the other.


### The street beneath

One last thing, outside again. This hill was a Roman town before it was anything else. About twenty years ago, archaeologists digging beside the mosque uncovered a stretch of Roman street. Big granite paving slabs, a road about five metres wide, with a drain running underneath.

Look in the garden area next to the building for the opened ground. Sources disagree on exactly where it shows, so if you cannot see it, check at the door. Picture carts rolling over those slabs, nearly a thousand years before the bricks went up.

So stand here for a moment. Roman stones in the ground. Visigothic capitals in the columns. A Muslim builder's name above the door. Christian saints at the end. All of Toledo, in a room you could cross in ten steps.


### Walk on

Walk on, through the old streets, to the church of San Román. That is stop four.


## Stop 4. San Román: Arabic on a Christian wall · Room Museum of the Councils and Visigothic Culture, Calle San Román

*738 words, about 5.3 min*


### A church that speaks two languages

Stop four. The church of San Román, on the high ground at the centre of the old town. These days it is the Museum of the Councils and Visigothic Culture. Go in, stand in the middle of the nave, and look at the arches first.

Horseshoe arches, in rows, like the ones you just saw at the little mosque. Now look at the columns holding them up. They do not match. Some are older than the church by many centuries, taken from Visigothic and even Roman buildings and set up again here.

But this was never a mosque. It was built as a church, by Christians, in the Mudéjar way: the building style of Muslim craftsmen, working for Christian patrons.


### The victory painted on the walls

Hold a question. Why would a Christian archbishop want Arabic letters in his church?

Here is the scene. Early in the thirteenth century, a Christian army has just crushed a Muslim army in the south, at a place called Las Navas de Tolosa. The Archbishop of Toledo rode with that army. Back home, he has this church painted and consecrated again, to celebrate.

Now look up at the walls. The paintings are in two bands, one above the other, with a strip of lettering running between them. Look for the four evangelists, each with wings. Look for a row of bishops, the archbishops of Toledo. Look for two young saints in deacon's robes, Stephen and Lawrence.

And look around the arches for more bands of writing. Some of it is Latin, in verse. Some of it is in Arabic letters.

One answer to the question: in Toledo, Arabic was not only the enemy's script. It was the look of beauty and learning, the way fine buildings were dressed. So a victory over the Muslim south was painted in a style that came from it.

> *Legend voice:* Scholars still argue over what some of that Arabic says, and whether it was meant to be read at all. Perhaps it was there simply to look splendid.


### The end of the world

Now find the Last Judgement. In Christian belief it is the day the dead rise from their graves to be judged. See if you can find them, small figures rising to face their judge. In a church built to give thanks for a battle, the painters put the end of the world. Everyone, winner and loser, will answer in the end.


### The boy king in the tower

> *Legend voice:* Before you go further, a story about the tower outside. Toledo tells that in a time of civil war, when the city was held by rivals, a child king of Castile was brought in by surprise, and shown to the people from the tower of San Román. The city rose for him, and his enemies fled. That boy grew up to be the king who won at Las Navas. The details vary from one teller to the next. But it is a good tale to have in your head when you look up at that tower.


### The kings before the Moors

Now walk round the cases. Before the Muslims came, Toledo was the capital of the Visigoths, a Germanic people who ruled Spain from here for well over a century. Their bishops met in great councils in this city, and at one of them a king gave up his old faith and took the Catholic one. That is why the museum carries the councils' name.

Look for the belt buckles, the jewellery, the carved stones.

And look for the copies of the gold crowns of Guarrazar. Check at the door if you cannot find them.

> *Legend voice:* The story goes that one summer in the eighteen fifties, after heavy rain near a village outside Toledo, a farming couple found gold glinting in the earth. It was a buried hoard of crowns and crosses, hidden, it is thought, when the Muslims arrived. Pieces were sold off quietly, some to France.

The originals are now in Madrid and Paris. Kings hung them over altars as gifts to God. On the finest, gold letters hang from the rim, spelling out a king's name and the words "offers this". If the copies here include it, find those letters.

One crown, of another king, was stolen from the royal armoury in Madrid in the twentieth century. It has never been found.

Stand under the arches once more. Roman columns. Visigothic gold. Arabic letters. Christian saints, and the Last Judgement. One small church, holding every Toledo at once.


### Walk on

Walk on, downhill, to the cathedral. That is stop five.


## Stop 5. The Cathedral: Six things not to miss · Room Cathedral of Toledo, Plaza del Ayuntamiento

*892 words, about 6.4 min*


### How to use this stop

Stop five. The cathedral of Toledo. Six things, six stories, in any order. Pause between them, find the next one, and press play.

This was the seat of the most powerful churchman in Spain, the Archbishop of Toledo. Everything here was made by people with money, who wanted to be seen spending it on God.


### One. A hole in the roof

Walk round behind the high altar, into the passage at the back. Stop when the light changes. Then turn, and look up.

Marble clouds. Angels tumbling. Rays of gold. And above it all, real daylight, pouring through a hole in the ceiling.

This is the Transparente. Behind the high altar the bread of the Mass was kept, the most holy thing in the church. But it sat in the dark. So, in the seventeen hundreds, a sculptor named Narciso Tomé did something outrageous. He cut a hole in the Gothic vault of this cathedral and let the sun in.

Some people at the time were thrilled. Some were horrified. Which are you? Find the Last Supper, carved high up in the marble, and watch how the light lands on it.


### Two. The red robe

Now go to the sacristy. Walk to the far end of the long room. The painting on the end wall.

El Greco's Disrobing of Christ. One of his first commissions in Toledo. If you saw his saints at the Prado, this is where his Spanish story starts.

Look at the robe. That red. It burns out of a crowd of grey faces and steel. Christ looks up. The soldiers close in. Someone is already reaching for the cloth.

The cathedral did not like it. They argued about the price, and it went to valuation. And, as the story goes, they objected to the three Marys in the corner, who are not in the Gospel at this moment. Look at the three women, lower left. They stayed.

A young foreigner, fighting the richest church in Spain. The picture has hung here ever since.


### Three. The war in wood

Now walk to the choir, the enclosed space in the middle of the nave. Look at the lower row of wooden seats, and above each seat, the carved panel.

Every panel is a scene from one war. The conquest of Granada. Towns falling. Walls. Ladders. Turbaned defenders on the battlements. Knights with banners riding in.

A carver named Rodrigo Alemán made them, only a few years after the war ended. More than fifty scenes. The war was still news.

You have just walked through the Alhambra, the prize at the end of this war. Find one panel and look at the people on the walls. Someone is about to lose their city.


### Four. A tower of silver

Now find the Treasury chapel. Behind the glass, a tower taller than a person, in silver and gold. The great monstrance, the Custodia.

It was made to carry the holy bread through the streets at Corpus Christi, and still goes out each year.

Look into its heart. At the centre there is a smaller piece, in gold. That one belonged to Queen Isabella. After her death, the cathedral acquired it, and the goldsmith built this great tower around it.

> *Legend voice:* The guides love a story. The gilding, they say, was done with the first gold Columbus brought back from the Americas. Nobody has proved it. But look at the shine, and think of where gold came from in those years.


### Five. The room of the archbishops

Now find the Chapter House, off the walkway behind the high altar. The room where the canons met to govern. Look up first.

A ceiling of carved and painted wood, gold and colour, in the Mudéjar style. Then the walls, painted all over by Juan de Borgoña. Scenes from the life of the Virgin. And at the far end, the Last Judgement. A message for the men who sat here: you will answer for what you decide in this room.

Now look lower, along the walls, under the frescoes. A long row of painted faces. These are the archbishops of Toledo, one after another, reaching back toward the earliest centuries. The early faces are imagined; there was nothing to copy.

The man who built this room was Cardinal Cisneros, archbishop, regent of Spain, the queen's confessor. A row of predecessors around the room where he ruled. He was telling everyone who walked in that he stood at the end of a line more than a thousand years long.


### Six. The old Christians

Last, find the Mozarabic Chapel, near the main west doors. It may be closed outside its service; check at the door.

When Toledo was a Muslim city, its Christians kept their own old way of worship, different from Rome's. They were called Mozarabs. When Christian kings took Toledo, the new rulers wanted the Roman way instead.

> *Legend voice:* Now the legend. The king, they say, ordered a trial by fire. Two prayer books were thrown into the flames, the Roman and the Toledan. The Toledan book would not burn. And so the old rite was allowed to survive.

That is legend. What is true is this. Cardinal Cisneros, the man of the Chapter House, founded this chapel to keep the old rite alive. It is still celebrated here.

Of all the gold in this building, that may be the rarest thing. A way of praying older than the cathedral, still spoken inside it.


### Walk on

When you are ready, the closing track is stop six. Play it wherever you are.


## Stop 6. Toledo: The city of three cultures · Room Anywhere, ideally a viewpoint over the river

*409 words, about 2.9 min*


### The postcard and the truth

Stop six, the last. Play this wherever you are. If you can, find a spot where you can see the river bending round the rock.

You will hear it everywhere in Toledo. The city of three cultures. Christians, Muslims and Jews, side by side. It is on the posters and in the shops. Is it true? The honest answer is yes, and no.


### Yes

Think of what you saw today. A mosque whose builder signed his name in brick. A church with Arabic letters round its arches. And in the Jewish quarter you walked through, two synagogues still stand, one built by Muslim craftsmen, one decorated like a palace of Granada.

In this city, scholars of three faiths worked together, turning books from Arabic into Latin and Castilian. Astronomy, medicine, philosophy, much of it Greek learning kept alive in Arabic, came back into Europe through Toledo. That was real. It made this city.


### No

But living side by side is not the same as being equal. Jews and Muslims lived under Christian law, protected in good times, and in bad times by nobody. In thirteen ninety-one, riots swept the Jewish quarters of Castile. Synagogues were taken.

Then, fourteen ninety-two. The year Granada fell. That spring, Isabella and Ferdinand signed the order expelling every Jew who would not convert. They signed it in the Alhambra. The palace you walked through.

Families who had lived here longer than anyone could remember had a few months to go. Many left, for Portugal, North Africa, the lands of the Ottomans. Their descendants kept speaking a form of Spanish for five hundred years. The Muslims of Castile were forced to convert a few years later, and a century after that, their descendants were expelled too.

> *Legend voice:* Some Sephardic families are said to have carried the keys of their Spanish houses with them, and kept them for generations. It is hard to prove. The story has never died.


### What is left

So the three cultures are here, in the stone. The people who made it are mostly gone. The beauty is real. The loss is real. Toledo asks you to carry both.


### Goodbye, Toledo

Look at the river one last time, wrapped round the city like an arm.

Thank you for walking it with me, Monica. Take the train back to Madrid. And if you go back to the Prado, find El Greco again. You have stood in his city now, in front of his son. His saints will look different.

Goodbye, Toledo.
