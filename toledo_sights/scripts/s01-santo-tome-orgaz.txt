# title: Stop 1. Santo Tomé: The Burial of the Count of Orgaz
# stop: 1
# room: Church of Santo Tomé, Calle Santo Tomé

## A debt two hundred years old

N: Stop one. The church of Santo Tomé, with its brick tower. The painting has its own entrance beside the church; check at the door. Go in and stand back from it. It fills the wall.

...

N: This picture starts with money. A rich nobleman of Toledo, the lord of the town of Orgaz, loved this little parish church. When he died, in thirteen twenty-three, he left it a gift to be paid every year, from Orgaz, for ever.

N: For ever lasted about two centuries. Then the payments stopped. The parish priest of Santo Tomé, Andrés Núñez, went to law. He won.

N: And then he did something clever. He decided the whole city should see, every day, why that money was owed. He would put the nobleman's funeral on the wall, miracle and all. So he hired the foreigner Toledo was talking about. A painter from Crete. They called him the Greek. El Greco.

## The miracle

A: Here is the legend, as Toledo tells it. At the funeral, as the body was about to be lowered, two saints came down from heaven. Saint Stephen, young. Saint Augustine, old and bearded. They lifted the dead man with their own hands and laid him in his tomb, while the mourners stared.

N: That is legend. The contract spelled it out: the miracle, the saints, and the crowd who saw it. The painter would pay for his own materials. The price would be set by valuers when it was done.

...

N: Look at the bottom half. The two saints in gold, bending under the weight of a body in black armour. How gently they hold him. The old saint cradles his shoulders. The young one holds his legs.

N: Now look closely at the young saint's robe, low down near his hem. There is a tiny picture painted on it. A man being stoned. That is Stephen's own death, the first Christian martyr, embroidered on the clothes he wears to bury someone else.

## The crowd is real

N: Look at the row of men behind. Black clothes. White ruffs. Long pale faces. They look like portraits because many of them were. Gentlemen of Toledo in El Greco's own day, not the dead man's. The painter put his neighbours at a funeral two hundred and sixty years old.

N: On the right, in a white robe, reading from a book, is the priest who won the lawsuit. Núñez put himself at the funeral he had fought for.

...

N: Now look above the row of faces, a little left of centre. Find a knight with a red cross on his chest, his hand raised. Just above that hand, one man is not watching the miracle. He is looking straight at you.

N: He is said to be El Greco himself.

## The boy

...

N: Now the lower left corner. A small boy in black, holding a torch. He points at the saints, and looks out at you.

N: This is the painter's son, Jorge Manuel. Look at the white handkerchief in his pocket. On it El Greco wrote his own name, and the year fifteen seventy-eight. Not the year of the painting. The year the boy was born.

N: El Greco probably never married the boy's mother. But he signed his greatest picture on his son, and put the child at the front, where no one could miss him.

## Up to heaven

N: Lift your eyes. Up the middle, an angel carries something small and pale, like a newborn baby. It is the dead man's soul, squeezed upward through a narrow passage of cloud. Look among the blessed at the top for faces in black. El Greco even placed the living king of Spain up there.

## The fight over the price

N: When it was finished, the valuers named a price. The parish thought it far too much. Arguments, appeals, lawyers. In the end El Greco gave in and accepted the lower valuation, and part of it was paid off against debts he owed.

N: That was his life in Toledo. He rented a huge set of rooms in an old palace by the Jewish quarter, more than twenty of them. He lived grandly. It is said he sometimes had musicians play while he dined. And he was always in court, suing patrons who would not pay.

A: One more glimpse of him. When a fellow Greek was brought before the Inquisition in Toledo, El Greco was called in to interpret. A foreigner himself, translating for one of his own, before the most feared court in Spain.

N: He died in Toledo, still working, with debts unpaid. On his deathbed he gave his son the power to write his will.

...

## Walk on

N: Look back at the boy one last time. Four hundred years on, he is still pointing, still asking you to look.

N: Walk on, down the hill, to the great stone monastery with chains on its walls. That is stop two, San Juan de los Reyes.
