# title: Stop 3. Cristo de la Luz: The mosque by daylight
# stop: 3
# room: Near Puerta del Sol, Calle Cristo de la Luz

## A name in brick

N: Stop three. The little mosque of Cristo de la Luz, near the Sun Gate. You know its legend already, the king's horse that knelt in the street, and the lamp still burning behind the wall. Now see the building by daylight.

...

N: Start outside, on the street side. Find the front wall of narrow red bricks, with its three arched doorways. Above them, blind arches cross and weave like basketwork. Now look along the very top, just under the roof line. A band of brick letters runs across it.

N: That is Arabic, in an old, square, angular script. And it is a signature.

N: It says, in short, that a man named Ahmad ibn Hadidi had this mosque built, hoping for his reward from God. That it was finished, with God's help, under the direction of a builder, Musa ibn Ali, and a man called Saada. And it gives the date. The year Christians count as nine hundred and ninety-nine.

...

N: Think about that. One man's money. One builder's skill. A small neighbourhood mosque, at the end of the first millennium. Toledo had many like it. Almost all are gone. This is the one that survived, and it still carries the name of the man who made it.

## Borrowed columns

N: Now go inside. It is tiny. Four columns hold up the whole roof. Stand in the middle, between them.

N: Look at the tops of the columns, the capitals. They do not match, and they are rougher than the walls around them. That is because the builder did not carve them. He reused them. They came from older buildings, from the Christian Visigoths who ruled Toledo before the Muslims came. One was remade in a restoration a century ago.

N: So the oldest stones holding up this mosque were carved by Christians, and they would hold it up again when it became a church.

## Nine small skies

...

N: Now look up. The ceiling is divided into nine little squares, three by three. Each one has its own small dome. And each dome is made differently. Brick ribs crossing in a new pattern every time. Stars. Crosses. Grids.

N: Turn slowly and look at all nine. It is a builder showing off nine ways to make a sky. If you saw the great mosque of Córdoba, you will know this family of ribbed domes from the dome in front of its prayer niche. This is its small country cousin.

## The Christians add a room

N: Now look east, to the far end. The style changes. A rounded apse, a half-circle chamber, joined on in the same red brick, but later.

N: After the Christian conquest, the mosque passed to a crusading order, the Knights of Saint John, and about two centuries after it was built, they added this apse. Christian prayer had to face east, so the church grew a new head.

...

N: Look into the curved vault of the apse. Faded paintings, patchy. At the centre was Christ in majesty, seated in glory. Much of it has flaked away. Look for an outline, a hand, a halo. One building, joined at the seam. Muslim domes on one side. Christian saints on the other.

## The street beneath

N: One last thing, outside again. This hill was a Roman town before it was anything else. About twenty years ago, archaeologists digging beside the mosque uncovered a stretch of Roman street. Big granite paving slabs, a road about five metres wide, with a drain running underneath.

N: Look in the garden area next to the building for the opened ground. Sources disagree on exactly where it shows, so if you cannot see it, check at the door. Picture carts rolling over those slabs, nearly a thousand years before the bricks went up.

...

N: So stand here for a moment. Roman stones in the ground. Visigothic capitals in the columns. A Muslim builder's name above the door. Christian saints at the end. All of Toledo, in a room you could cross in ten steps.

## Walk on

N: Walk on, through the old streets, to the church of San Román. That is stop four.
