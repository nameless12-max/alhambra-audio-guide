The Alhambra, Nasrid Palaces First

A Walking Guide for November 2026

This walk starts where the ticket system insists you be on time: the Nasrid Palaces. From there it moves outward, from council hall to throne tower and the private Palace of the Lions, then to the older Partal and the tower-houses on the walls, across to the Generalife summer estate, and back along the old town street to the emperor's palace and the fortress where it all began. At each stop you'll get where to stand, what to look at, and why it matters. Where a story is legend, or experts disagree, the guide says so. Boxes marked Often told, thinly sourced hold details that are widely repeated, and too good to leave out, but that we couldn't trace to a solid source. Allow four to five hours; the official estimate for a general visit is about three, but this walk lingers.

Before you go — November 2026

Hours. Winter hours run from 15 October to 31 March. Day visits are 08:30 to 18:00 every day, and the ticket office opens at 08:00. Plan your last stops around that 18:00 close.

Your Nasrid time slot is strict. The official rule: if you are not at the Nasrid Palaces entrance at the time printed on your ticket, you lose that visit. No grace period is published. Entry is capped at about 300 people per half hour.

One entry per area. You can enter each ticketed area (Nasrid Palaces, Partal, Generalife, Alcazaba) only once. You can leave and re-enter the complex as a whole, but once you leave the Nasrid Palaces you cannot go back in.

ID. Bring your original passport or national ID card. It is mandatory, and tickets are issued against it. Children under 12 need no ID.

Bags. Backpacks bigger than 40 × 40 cm are not allowed. Lockers at the Puerta del Vino are free with a same-day ticket, if available.

Cameras. Selfie sticks, flash and tripods are banned inside the Nasrid Palaces and other enclosed spaces. Baby buggies are not allowed in the Nasrid Palaces or the Generalife palace.

Food, drink, toilets. Eating is allowed only in certain areas, including the Plaza de los Aljibes. The official services list shows only vending machines, so bring water. Toilets are at the Access Pavilion, by the Puerta del Vino, and at the Generalife.

The Alhambra Museum (inside the Palace of Charles V) is free and closed on Mondays. It opens Wednesday to Saturday 08:30–18:00, and Tuesday and Sunday 08:30–14:30 only. If you visit on a Tuesday or Sunday, see the museum before your Nasrid slot: it is right beside the palace entrance.

The earthquakes and what is closed

A swarm of earthquakes began near Alhendín, just south of Granada, on 14 August 2026. The largest were a magnitude 5.0 on 15 August and a 4.8 on 18 August, when the complex was evacuated and closed for the day. There was no significant structural damage, but some battlements fell in the Alcazaba, and decorative plaster fell in the Nasrid Palaces, mostly in the Throne Room (the Salón de Embajadores).

The complex reopened on 19 August on adapted routes. At the latest reports:

  • Salón de Embajadores (Throne Room): closed "until further notice" as of a 25 August 2026 report. No reopening had been reported by mid-November.
  • Sala de la Barca, its antechamber: reported to have reopened around 26 August.
  • Alcazaba: reopened on 23 September on an adapted route.
  • Torre de la Vela: still "temporarily closed" for technical inspections as of 28 September 2026.
  • Jardín de la Umbría in the Generalife: closed since 2018. A roughly three-month restoration was announced in July 2026. Treat it as a bonus if it is open.

The official ticket site warns that routes "may be modified as required by conservation, safety, and visitor management needs." Expect some scaffolding too: restoration of the Mexuar roofs and of decorative elements in the Court of the Lions has been under way or announced.

On foot from the city, by the Cuesta de Gomérez. The steep street climbs from Plaza Nueva to the Puerta de las Granadas (Gate of the Pomegranates), a Renaissance gate of 1536 built over an earlier Islamic one. It opens into the Alhambra woods, whose three-path layout was made in 1729 for a visit by Philip V. The Nasrids in two minutes

Everything in the palaces was built by one family, the Nasrids, rulers of the last Muslim kingdom in the Iberian peninsula. In the 1230s and 1240s, as Christian Castile and Aragon swallowed Córdoba, Valencia and Seville, Muhammad I ibn Nasr carved out a kingdom in the mountains around Granada and started building on this hill in 1238.

The kingdom survived for 250 years by being useful and careful. Its sultans paid tribute to Castile as vassals, sometimes fought beside it, and leaned on Morocco when they needed help. Granada filled up with Muslims leaving the conquered cities and grew rich on silk, trade and farming. The palaces you're about to see are the luxury of a small, wealthy and permanently threatened state. Much of the finest work belongs to just two rulers, Yusuf I (1333–1354) and his son Muhammad V (1354–1359 and 1362–1391). The end came in 1492, after a decade of war made worse by civil war inside the ruling family.

How to read a Nasrid wall

Read this in the queue. Every room uses the same vocabulary.

Bottom: the tile dado. At eye level and below, walls are clad in glazed tiles in geometric patterns. These patterns have had a long afterlife. The Dutch artist M. C. Escher sketched the tiles here in 1922 and came back to draw them again in 1936; they fed straight into his famous interlocking prints. In the 1830s–40s the architect Owen Jones measured and drew the Alhambra's ornament, and his books, and the Alhambra Court he built at London's Crystal Palace in 1854, spread Nasrid patterns through Victorian design. Mathematicians still argue over these walls. Every repeating flat pattern belongs to one of exactly 17 types of symmetry, and you'll hear that all 17 can be found here. Careful surveys disagree, and some count fewer. Either way, the craftsmen got there centuries before the mathematics was written down. The patterns start simple. Lay one square over another, turned 45 degrees, and you have an eight-pointed star. Extend its lines and you get longer stars, octagons and crosses. Rotate, mirror and repeat, then choose which lines to keep and which colours to fill, and a single square grows into the interlacing star patterns (lazo) on these walls. Why did geometry matter so much? One historian's answer in a recent documentary: in the Islamic world, finding prayer times and the direction of Mecca, and navigating for pilgrimage and trade, made astronomy and geometry practical, even religious, skills long before they became an art.

Middle: carved stucco. Above the tiles, the walls are carved plaster: panels of plant scrolls, lattices and bands of writing.

Top: wood and muqarnas. Ceilings are either carpentry, with thousands of small wooden pieces fitted into star patterns, or muqarnas: prism-shaped cells of plaster stacked into honeycomb vaults that seem to drip from the dome. Muqarnas first appeared in the eastern Islamic world around the 10th and 11th centuries and spread from Iran to Morocco. Built up from a small set of standard prism shapes, they turn a dome into something like a sky of stars, which is exactly the effect the Nasrids wanted over a ruler's head.

Read the writing. Look for a short phrase repeated again and again in a neat cartouche: wa-la ghaliba illa Allah, "There is no victor but God," the Nasrid motto. It appears so often you'll stop counting. Nobody has published a reliable total, so ignore the precise figures you may hear. (The figure you'll hear most is "over 9,000 times." Treat it as folklore until someone publishes a count.) Where did the motto come from? The story goes that the dynasty's founder, Muhammad I, was hailed as ghalib, "victor," on returning from a campaign, and answered that there is no victor but God. His title really was al-Ghalib billah, but the scene itself is tradition, and accounts disagree on when and where it happened.

Around the motto run Qur'anic verses and poems written for these walls by court poet-statesmen. Ibn Zamrak (died 1393) wrote the poems on the Comares facade and the Lions fountain. Ibn al-Jayyab wrote verses in the Generalife and the Torre de la Cautiva. A poem in the Throne Room is attributed either to him or to Ibn al-Khatib. Often the building speaks in the first person: "I am the fresh eye of this garden," says one window.

Often told, thinly sourced

  • A 2026 documentary describes how muqarnas were made. Seven or eight basic prism shapes were cast in plaster moulds, trimmed and polished, then fixed one by one with plaster paste, working upward from the walls. Such a complex 3D puzzle is hard to imagine without a written plan, though no Nasrid drawings of one survive.

One myth to drop now. You'll hear that Nasrid art had no living creatures. Most of the ornament is abstract, but wait until you meet twelve stone lions and a ceiling full of painted kings, knights and ladies.

Stop 1

The Mexuar and the Oratory

about 10 minutes

Where you are

Past the ticket check, you step into the Mexuar, a long, low hall with four columns in the middle. The small Oratory opens off its far end.

Look for

  • The four columns and their entablature in the centre of the hall: all that survives of a raised lantern that once lit the room from above.
  • The tile dado: Nasrid pieces mixed with Christian ones, including a double-headed eagle.
  • A stucco frieze above the tiles that reads "The Kingdom is God's, Power is God's, Glory is God's."
  • In the Oratory: the mihrab (prayer niche), the low windows framing the valley, and an inscription urging, "Come to pray. Don't be one of the negligent people."

The story

The name comes from the Arabic maswar. Here the Council of Ministers met and the sultan dispensed justice. It was built under Isma'il I (1314–1325), Yusuf I's father, and redecorated by Yusuf I and by Muhammad V in his second reign (1362–1391), and is probably the oldest room in the palaces.

After 1492 it became a chapel. An upper floor was added in the 16th century, the lantern was lost (sources disagree over whether under the Catholic Monarchs, Ferdinand and Isabella, or their grandson Charles V), and the area behind the balustrade became the choir. The stucco frieze was salvaged from a lost portico in the neighbouring Court of Machuca, named after Pedro Machuca, the architect of Charles V's palace, who lived there while he built it. The double-headed eagle in the tiles is the emblem of Charles V as Holy Roman Emperor. Its appearance among Nasrid stars is the first of many places today where the conquerors wrote themselves into the sultans' rooms.

The architecture

The Oratory is not aligned with the city wall it sits on: it is turned toward the qibla, the direction of Mecca. The windows sit low because worshippers knelt, and the view was framed for eyes close to the floor. (The floor itself was lowered in the 20th century.)

Much of what you see is rebuilt. In 1590 a gunpowder store down in the Darro valley exploded and wrecked the Oratory, which was restored in 1917. The same blast will turn up at three more stops.

Often told, thinly sourced

  • Some guides describe an inscription near the Mexuar's south entrance praising Muhammad V as "the shadow of God" over his people. We couldn't find it in a primary source.
  • On your way in you pass the excavated foundations of two lost courtyards, thought to have held the chancery and the treasury.

Stop 2

Patio del Cuarto Dorado and the Comares facade

about 10 minutes

Where you are

You step out into a small courtyard with a low fountain in the middle. Facing you is a tall wall, carved from top to bottom, with two doorways and a deep wooden eave. The Cuarto Dorado (Golden Room) opens off the patio.

Look for

  • The two rectangular doorways framed in tile. The left one is the way into the private palace.
  • The cedar eave jutting out at the top of the wall.
  • Bands of inscription across the facade: a poem by Ibn Zamrak and the Throne Verse (Qur'an 2:255). The Throne Verse is among the best-known passages of the Qur'an. It describes God, ever-living and never sleeping, whose throne extends over the heavens and the earth, and it has long been used as a verse of protection. Over a ruler's door it says two things at once: this house is protected, and the sultan sits under a far greater throne.
  • In the Cuarto Dorado, look up at the cedar ceiling with pine-cone and shell motifs.

The story

This is a political statement in stucco. Muhammad V ordered it in 1370 to commemorate his conquest of Algeciras, the port on the Strait of Gibraltar, the year before. Here the public world of the Mexuar met the sultan's private palace, and the eave shaded the raised seat where he sat to receive people.

Muhammad V had reason to celebrate. In 1359 a palace coup drove him from the throne, and he spent nearly three years in exile in Fez, with his vizier Ibn al-Khatib beside him. He won his kingdom back in 1362 with Castilian help, and in 1369 took Algeciras. This facade, the rebuilt Mexuar and the whole Palace of the Lions belong to that second reign: a restored king building to be seen.

The architecture

The two doors are a lesson in Nasrid privacy. The right-hand door led to other quarters, possibly a treasury tied to the Mexuar. The left-hand door leads to the Court of the Myrtles, but not directly. Its passage bends, so no one standing in this courtyard can see into the palace beyond. Watch for the turns as you go through.

Often told, thinly sourced

  • The facade poem is often quoted as opening "I am a crown on the front of my door," with the gateway speaking for itself. The full translation comes from secondary sites only.
  • The Cuarto Dorado ceiling is often credited to Muhammad V's reign. Others say it was repainted and gilded under the Catholic Monarchs. The low courtyard fountain is said to be a copy of one moved here from the Patio de Lindaraja.

Stop 3

The Court of the Myrtles

about 10 minutes

Where you are

The bent passage brings you into a long, bright courtyard: a rectangular pool edged with myrtle hedges and arcades at each end. Walk to the south end and turn to face north. The massive tower rising over the far arcade is the Comares Tower.

Look for

  • The Comares Tower reflected in the still pool. Crouch near the water's edge to fit the whole tower in.
  • The myrtle hedges on both long sides, which give the court its Spanish name, Patio de los Arrayanes.

The story

This was the heart of the official palace, built mainly under Yusuf I (1333–1354) and redecorated by Muhammad V. Everything leads to the tower, 45 metres tall with walls two to three metres thick: a fortress with a throne room inside.

The architecture

The court measures about 36.6 × 23 metres, and the pool, about 34 × 7.1 metres, fills almost its whole length. The nearly still water works as a mirror, doubling the tower and pulling your eye along the axis to the throne.

Often told, thinly sourced

  • The 19th-century restorer Rafael Contreras is said to have added Persian-style domes to the porticoes, later removed by Leopoldo Torres Balbás in the 20th century. The detail for this court is thinly sourced, but the broad story is not. Two names recur through this guide. Contreras and his family restored the Alhambra through much of the 19th century, filling gaps with romantic, sometimes invented "Moorish" decoration. Torres Balbás, the conservation architect from 1923 to 1936, took a stricter line: repair only with evidence, and keep new work recognisable. Much of what you see is the result of that argument.

Myth check

You'll hear that "Comares" comes from comaraxía, the coloured glass that once filled the tower windows. That is one theory among several, and the origin of the name is still debated.

Stop 4

Sala de la Barca and Salón de Embajadores

5–15 minutes, depending on what is open

Where you are

Walk to the north arcade, under the tower. A double archway leads into the long Sala de la Barca, and beyond it lies the square Salón de Embajadores.

Status. At the latest reports, the Sala de la Barca had reopened (around 26 August 2026), but the Salón de Embajadores was closed "until further notice" after plaster fell during the August quakes. No reopening had been reported by mid-November. Check the signs: you may be able to walk through the Barca and look toward the hall, or you may be turned back at the arcade. Either way, here is what is inside.

Look for (Sala de la Barca)

  • The long, narrow room, about 24 × 4.35 metres.
  • Its curved wooden ceiling, shaped like an upturned hull. It is a replica: the original was destroyed by fire in 1890, and the replacement was finished in 1964.

Look for (Salón de Embajadores, if open)

  • The wooden dome, 18.2 metres above a room 11.3 metres square, built from 8,017 interlocking pieces into tiers of stars.
  • The nine alcoves, three in each side wall. The central alcove of the north wall, straight ahead as you enter, is where the throne stood.
  • The band of Qur'anic text at the base of the dome, from the Surah al-Mulk, which names God as Lord of the heavens.

The story

This was the ceremonial climax of the Nasrid state, built under Yusuf I. An envoy came from the bright court, through the Barca, and found the sultan in the alcove facing him. The poem in the throne alcove is attributed to Ibn al-Jayyab or Ibn al-Khatib. The 1590 explosion in the Darro valley blew out most of the hall's coloured glass.

The Alhambra was also where, on 31 March 1492, Ferdinand and Isabella signed and published the decree expelling Spain's Jews. It is known as the Alhambra Decree. An earlier version had been dated at nearby Santa Fe, the camp-town the monarchs built for the siege of Granada, on 20 March. No document records which room they signed it in. The decree gave Spain's Jews until the end of July 1492 to convert or leave. Many tens of thousands left (the totals are much disputed), ending one of the great Jewish communities of medieval Europe. Granada's Muslims were promised their faith in the surrender terms; within a decade, they too were forced to convert.

The architecture

The dome is read as an image of the seven heavens, rising in tiers to a central point: a wooden sky over a fortress room. The idea comes straight from the inscription around its base. Surah al-Mulk speaks of God who created the seven heavens one above another, and of the lamps (the stars) that adorn the lowest of them. Under that sky sat the sultan in his alcove. The message to an ambassador was plain: this throne stands under God's order of the universe, and draws its legitimacy from it.

Often told, thinly sourced

  • Tradition places the signing of the 1492 Alhambra Decree in this very hall. The documents say only "in the Alhambra."
  • The dome is said to have been painted originally, with lapis lazuli among the pigments. We found no technical study to confirm it.

Myth check

The Barca's name may come from the Arabic baraka, "blessing," heard by Castilian ears as barca, "boat." Or it may simply describe the hull-shaped ceiling. Both explanations are current.

Stop 5

Sala de los Mocárabes and the Court of the Lions

15–20 minutes

Where you are

From the Myrtles, the route leads into the Sala de los Mocárabes, a long entrance hall. It opens straight onto the Court of the Lions. Walk in and stand under the arcade at the near (west) end, with the fountain and the far pavilion in line.

Look for

  • In the Mocárabes, the plaster vault, which replaced Nasrid muqarnas wrecked by the 1590 explosion.
  • The forest of slender marble columns: 124 in all, standing alone or in clusters of two to four at the corners.
  • The four channels that run from the surrounding halls and meet at the fountain, quartering the court.
  • The twelve lions carrying a twelve-sided basin, and the poem carved around the rim.

The story

This was Muhammad V's private palace, begun around 1377 beside the official Comares palace. The court is about 35 × 20 metres, slightly out of square, with a pavilion projecting at each end.

Ibn Zamrak's verses on the rim say water and marble are so alike you can hardly tell which is flowing, and liken the sultan's generosity to water soaking a garden. The four channels are usually read as the four rivers of Paradise. A visitor in 1502 saw six orange trees here, with the rest paved in marble.

Why lions? Read the rim, and the poem answers. Ibn Zamrak likens the flowing water to the ruler's generous hand, and the lions to his warriors. They look ready to pounce, the poem says, but respect for the emir holds back their fury. So the fountain is a portrait of power: wild strength, tamed and in Muhammad V's service, pouring out water, the ultimate luxury on a dry hill, to all four sides of his garden.

The idea had deep roots in al-Andalus. Four centuries earlier, the palace-city of the Umayyad caliphs of Córdoba, Madinat al-Zahra, had a marble basin ringed by twelve gold animal figures, a lion among them. Muhammad V liked the motif. In 1367 he gave his new hospital in the city below, the Maristán, a pair of lion water-spouts. Sixteen Nasrid lions survive, and twelve of them are here. Why twelve? No Nasrid text says. The number matches the twelve-sided basin and that old Andalusi model; readings as the zodiac or the months have no evidence behind them.

Listen, too. The restorers found that water rose through a pierced stone cylinder at the centre and slipped out of the lions' mouths almost silently, more like a spring than a fountain. That matches the poem's picture of water so clear and still it seems solid.

The fountain has had a busy afterlife. An upper basin added in 1626 now stands in the Alcazaba's Jardín de los Adarves, where you'll see it later. A jet stood on top from 1835 to 1966. During Napoleon's occupation of Granada (1810–1812), a French commander planted a flower garden here, removed in 1846 because the damp threatened the structure. From 2007 the lions were away for restoration while the original hydraulic system was rebuilt; they came back at the end of 2011, and the restored fountain was unveiled in July 2012.

The architecture

Compare this court with the Myrtles: there, solid walls and still water; here, thin columns and moving water. Notice, too, that the Myrtles runs north–south while this court runs east–west, probably because it had to fit between existing buildings, streets and gardens. Yet its two grandest halls, the Dos Hermanas and the Abencerrajes, still face each other on the north–south line. The columns are white marble from Macael, in the Almería mountains, which were then part of the kingdom. Look at the corners where the columns bunch together. The grouping breaks the rhythm of the arcades, and the court reads as a grove.

Often told, thinly sourced

  • Look closely at the twelve lions. Several guides say they fall into three carving styles, four lions each, with differences in the manes, muzzles and tails. Judge for yourself.
  • Figures you'll hear: a basin about 2.5 metres across, a jet about 4 metres high (1835–1966), and a central spout that is a replica since 1981. None of these could be confirmed in an official source.
  • The Lions palace is thought to have been called Qasr al-Riyad al-Sa'id, the Palace of the Happy Garden.
  • The Mocárabes vault is sometimes dated to 1714. Other sources put it in the 17th century.
  • Some writers point out √2 proportions in the plan, and that the north–south layout uses deep eaves to block high summer sun while letting low winter sun into the halls.

Myth check

Most scholars date the lions to Muhammad V's reign. A theory published in 1956 held that they came from the 11th-century palace of the Naghrela family, Granada's Jewish viziers. It rested on a poem by Solomon ibn Gabirol describing a basin like "the sea of Solomon" resting on lions, an echo of the twelve oxen that carried the bronze sea in Solomon's Temple. It is a beautiful idea, but the carving is 14th-century Nasrid work, and most scholars have set the theory aside. The popular claim that the fountain worked as a water clock is a hypothesis with no documentary basis. And a traveller in 1494, two years after the conquest, counted thirteen lions.

Stop 6

Sala de los Abencerrajes

about 5 minutes

Where you are

On the south side of the Court of the Lions, go through the doorway into a square hall with a small marble fountain in the centre. Stand next to the fountain and look straight up.

Look for

  • The dome: an eight-pointed star of muqarnas that opens out into eight trunk-like clusters of stalactites.
  • Sixteen windows, one in each side of the drum, lighting the honeycomb from within.
  • The twelve-sided fountain, which reflects the dome when the water is still.
  • The original wooden door, and a tile dado that is 16th-century Sevillian work.

The story

The hall is named after the Abencerrajes, a powerful noble family. A 16th-century legend says they were invited to a banquet here and massacred. In the best-known version, 36 Abencerrajes were beheaded after a rival clan, the Zegríes, accused one of them of an affair with Isabel de Solís, the favourite of Sultan Muley Hacén (Abu l-Hasan Ali, the last sultan's father). Some tellings add that the blood still stains the fountain.

Behind the legend lies a real feud. Muley Hacén's passion for Isabel de Solís, a Christian captive who took the name Zoraya, split the court. His first wife, Aixa, fled with her sons in 1482, and one of them, Boabdil (Muhammad XII, who would be Granada's last sultan), rose against his father. Captured by Castile at Lucena in 1483, Boabdil was released as a vassal. The civil war between father, son and uncle did much to doom the kingdom.

The architecture

Light enters through the sixteen windows and breaks across the plaster facets, so the ceiling seems weightless. Most of the plasterwork was restored in the 16th century.

Often told, thinly sourced

  • The reddish stain in the fountain, "the blood of the Abencerrajes," is usually explained as rust or iron oxide in the stone. That's plausible, but we found no study of it.

Myth check

The massacre is a legend first written down in the 16th century. It is good storytelling, not documented history.

Stop 7

Sala de los Reyes

about 5–10 minutes

Where you are

The long hall that closes the east end of the Court of the Lions, behind the arcade. Walk along it and look up into its vaulted alcoves.

Look for

  • Three elliptical ceilings painted on leather, stretched over wooden planks.
  • In the centre vault, ten seated Muslim figures on cushions, against a gold ground.
  • At each end of that vault, two heraldic shields of the Order of the Band, a Castilian order of chivalry.
  • In the side vaults, knights, ladies, hunts, chess and jousts: a whole romance of chivalry, painted overhead.

The story

Here is your proof that the Nasrids were not against images. Who the ten figures are is debated: the first ten Nasrid kings (the reading that names the room), a council, nobles of the Order of the Band, or Muhammad V and his court. If they are kings, the paintings date from about 1396–1408. The shields are a clue to how entangled the two worlds were. The Order of the Band was a knightly order founded by Alfonso XI of Castile in the 1330s. Muhammad V was a close ally of Peter I of Castile, who had helped him back to his throne. At the same moment, in the 1360s, Peter was building his own palace in the Alcázar of Seville with craftsmen from Granada and Toledo. Its plasterwork looks so Nasrid that it carries Arabic inscriptions praising a Christian king. Christian painters here, Granadan plasterers there: the frontier was a war zone, and also a workshop.

The architecture

Wet leather was stretched over planed boards shaped into an ellipse, glued and nailed, then painted. The subjects are Muslim, but most figures and the background architecture look Christian. The painters were probably Christians with links to a Toledo workshop and touches of Italian 14th-century style, working for a Muslim patron. Attribution is still debated.

Dos Hermanas, Ajimeces and the Mirador de Lindaraja

10 minutes

Where you are

Cross to the north side of the court and go into the Sala de las Dos Hermanas (Hall of the Two Sisters). Stand near the small central fountain, look down, then up. Then go on through the Sala de los Ajimeces to the little window bay at the end, the Mirador de Lindaraja.

Look for

  • On the floor, the two great white marble slabs either side of the fountain, matched in size and colour. They are the "two sisters."
  • Overhead, a muqarnas dome of at least 5,000 pieces, rising from an octagonal drum with two windows on each of its eight sides.
  • In the Mirador, the low windows. Crouch to the height of someone seated on floor cushions and see how the view frames itself.
  • Poems that speak for the building. Here the room boasts of its "peerless, radiant dome." In the Mirador, the window calls itself "the fresh eye of this garden."

The story

The Dos Hermanas belongs to Muhammad V's private palace, and the Mirador was its jewel. It once looked out over the Darro valley. After the conquest, rooms built for Charles V went up in front of it and closed the view, enclosing the garden below.

The man whose verses fill this room and the Lions fountain did not die peacefully. Ibn Zamrak was the pupil of the great vizier and historian Ibn al-Khatib. When Ibn al-Khatib fled to Morocco in 1371, his writings were condemned in Granada and burned. In 1374 he was tried in Fez, with Ibn Zamrak in the Granadan delegation, and was strangled in prison. Ibn Zamrak rose to be vizier himself, and in 1393 he too was murdered. The walls that speak so serenely were written by men living dangerously.

The architecture

This dome and the Abencerrajes dome face each other across the court, the summit of muqarnas work. Here the cells cascade from the apex in tier after tier, lit by sixteen windows.

  • The Dos Hermanas poem by Ibn Zamrak is often translated as opening "I am a garden graced by every beauty," and goes on to bring the stars, the moon and Gemini down to touch the dome. Translations vary, and none we found is authoritative.
  • A recent documentary says Charles V dined in this hall during his long honeymoon stay in 1526, and that Pedro Machuca labelled it "His Majesty the Emperor's dining room."

Stop 9

Patio de la Reja, Patio de Lindaraja and the exit

10 minutes

Where you are

From the Mirador area the route leads along a corridor beside the Emperor's Chambers, through the small Patio de la Reja, and down into the Patio de Lindaraja, a garden courtyard below the Mirador. You leave the palaces from here through a gate into the Partal gardens. If the exit has been changed, follow the staff.

Look for

  • In the Patio de Lindaraja, look back up at the Mirador you just stood in. Now you can see it from the garden it was named for.
  • In the Patio de la Reja, a balcony toward the Albaicín and Sacromonte, if it is open on your route.
  • Doors you can't go through. The Emperor's Chambers lie along this corridor. Washington Irving stayed in them from 4 May to 29 July 1829, and a marble plaque placed on the door of his rooms in 1914 commemorates him. The Comares Baths are reached by stairs from the Patio de Lindaraja. Both are normally closed and open only occasionally as the "Space of the Month." If either is open on your day, it is worth the detour.

The story

From 1528 Charles V built new rooms here, along a corridor linking the Comares and Lions palaces. Julio Aquiles and Alejandro Mayner, trained in Raphael's workshop, painted the "fruit rooms" around 1537. Three centuries later Irving moved in; his Tales of the Alhambra came out in 1832. When he arrived, the Alhambra was half-abandoned: a barracks for invalid soldiers, a prison at times, and home to squatters and Gitano families. Irving's book made the world look again. Restoration began in earnest in the 1840s, and in 1870 the Alhambra was declared a National Monument.

The architecture

The hidden baths have vaults pierced with star-shaped skylights that let steam out and air in. Once you pass the gate you can't come back in, so take a last look.

  • The resting hall of the Comares Baths, the Sala de las Camas, is named for its two raised benches, where bathers undressed and rested.

The Partal

20–30 minutes
10

Stop 10

The Partal: Torre de las Damas, houses, oratory, Partal Alto and the Rauda

Where you are

You come out into terraced gardens on the north side of the hill. Below you is a long pool, and behind it a portico of five arches fronting a tower: the Partal and the Torre de las Damas. Stand at the far end of the pool to see the portico reflected.

Look for

  • The five-arched portico over the pool, and the lattice (sebka) stucco above the arches.
  • The slender marble columns. They date from 1965, the work of Francisco Prieto-Moreno, Torres Balbás's long-serving successor; brick pillars originally held up the arches.
  • Inside the tower, the ceiling, or rather its absence. The original carved cupola is in Berlin.
  • Next to it, the small Oratory of the Partal, raised on the wall, with a correctly oriented mihrab.
  • Along the wall, a row of small Nasrid houses. One of them, the House of the Paintings, preserves rare 14th-century wall paintings. They are normally closed; look in if they are open.
  • Uphill, the excavated outline of a palace: a long pool, room footings and the remains of a bath.

The story

This is the oldest palace still standing in the Alhambra, though only partly. Its style suggests it was built under Muhammad III (1302–1309), decades before the Court of the Lions. Partal comes from the Arabic al-Bartal, which in turn came from Latin portale, portico: the palace is named after the very feature in front of you.

"Tower of the Ladies" is a 19th-century nickname. Its last private owner, the German banker Arthur von Gwinner, removed the carved wooden cupola before giving the building to the Spanish state in 1891. It has belonged to Berlin's Museum für Islamische Kunst since 1977.

The oratory is usually attributed to Yusuf I, though tree-ring dating of its timber to 1332–33 suggests Isma'il I may have begun it. The house paintings, found in 1907, show rows of horsemen and tents with musicians, servants and women of the Nasrid court.

The palace on the hill. The ruin above the gardens is one building with two names: the Partal Alto, long called the Palace of Yusuf III, though current scholarship gives it to Muhammad II (1273–1302). After 1492 it became the home of the Count of Tendilla, the Christian governor of the Alhambra, and his successors. Tendilla's descendants, the Marquises of Mondéjar, held the governorship almost as an inheritance for two centuries. Then they backed the losing Habsburg claimant in the War of the Spanish Succession (1701–1714), the war over who would inherit the Spanish throne, and in 1717 the winner, Philip V, stripped them of office. The Marquis then demolished the palace, in 1718 or in 1734–35 (sources differ), and sold off pieces. It was excavated in 1924; about a third is still buried.

The Rauda. The sultans were buried in the royal cemetery, the Rauda. It lay north-west of the old Great Mosque, just across the lower street, the Calle Real Baja, from the royal palaces. You'll pass close to its site later. Ismail I, Muhammad IV, Yusuf I, Yusuf III and probably Muhammad V were buried there. After the conquest, Boabdil exhumed his ancestors and took them into exile, probably to a burial ground his wife owned at Mondújar. When the site was excavated in 1925, about seventy tombs were found, all but one empty. No remains of the dynasty survive. Some royal tombstones are in the Alhambra Museum.

The architecture

Compare this portico-and-pool with the Court of the Myrtles: the same idea, at an earlier and simpler stage. The gardens around you are not Nasrid at all. They were laid out between the 1910s and the 1930s, though this was a great garden in Nasrid times too.

Often told, thinly sourced

  • Some sources say only the central arch of the Partal portico is original.

Stop 11

Paseo de las Torres

Where you are

From the Partal, walk east along the path inside the northern wall. Towers project from the wall at intervals. Keep the wall on your left and keep walking toward the Generalife.

Look for

  • Torre de los Picos, with its pointed battlements. It guarded the Puerta del Arrabal, a gate leading down toward the Albaicín.
  • Torre del Cadí, then Torre de la Cautiva. The Cautiva looks like any other tower from outside.
  • Torre de las Infantas, farther east.
  • Any open door: the Cautiva and the Picos open from time to time as the Space of the Month.

The story

These were houses as well as defences. The Picos, built by Yusuf I, has three vaulted levels with stucco and paintings. The Cautiva was built at the end of the 13th century and refitted as a home under Yusuf I. Its interior is second only to Comares in richness. Its tile poems are by Ibn al-Jayyab, and one inscription sums it up: "Calahorra with a palace within… tell us it is both a fortress and a mansion to be enjoyed!" The Infantas was built, or rebuilt, by Muhammad VII toward the end of the 14th century.

The architecture

Qalahurra (calahorra) means a tower-palace: a military tower fitted out as a home. The Infantas shows how such homes stayed bearable in a Granada summer. The main rooms face north, away from the sun. The earthen walls are so thick that the day's heat takes hours to get through. Windows are small and screened with lattices, high openings let rising hot air escape, and water in the central basin cools the air as it evaporates. Here the defensive wall became a string of private apartments with views, the logic of the Comares Tower on a smaller scale.

Often told, thinly sourced

  • The Torre de las Infantas is the setting of Washington Irving's "Legend of the Three Beautiful Princesses" in Tales of the Alhambra.
  • A recent documentary claims rooms like these stayed around 25°C while it was 40°C outside, and that Nasrid watchtowers across the kingdom passed warnings to the Alhambra by smoke and mirror signals. Both are plausible, but we couldn't check either.
  • The Torre del Agua is said to have been blown up by Napoleon's troops as they withdrew in 1812, which would explain why it is a ruin.
  • At the Torre del Agua, guides say the Acequia Real crosses into the Alhambra on a small aqueduct, and the tower was built to guard it. The Camino de Ronda outside the wall is often called a defensive patrol path or moat.

Myth check

"Tower of the Captive" is a fanciful name with no historical basis. The story linking it to Isabel de Solís is legend.

Getting to the Generalife. The old access to the Generalife was near the Torre del Agua, across the ravine of the Cuesta del Rey Chico. Rey Chico, "the little king," is what the Castilians called Boabdil. Today, follow the signs across the bridge by that tower. Allow roughly 10 to 15 minutes; walking times are not published.

The Generalife

The Generalife has its own signed one-way route. Take the stops below in whatever order it gives you.

Stop 12

The gardens and the water

10–15 minutes

Where you are

You enter through the modern gardens below the palace: hedged walks, cypress walls and long pools, and an open-air theatre. Above you on the slope stands the summer palace.

Look for

  • The Jardines Nuevos, laid out between 1931 and 1951 by Torres Balbás and Prieto-Moreno. The theatre dates from 1952.
  • Down the slope, the terraced orchards (Las Huertas), farmed since the 14th century.
  • On the hilltop above the estate, the ruined fort of the Silla del Moro, a lookout that protected the water supply.

The story

The Generalife was an almunia: a country estate that was both a retreat and a working farm, a short walk from the palace. It probably began under Muhammad II at the end of the 13th century. An inscription records that Isma'il I remodelled it in 1319, and Yusuf III reworked the southern parts in the 15th century. An inscription by Ibn al-Jayyab calls it "the House of the Felicitous Kingdom."

The architecture

All of this runs on the Acequia Real, the royal channel, which draws water from the Darro about 6 kilometres upstream. A smaller channel, the Acequia del Tercio, splits off several kilometres before the estate and runs along higher ground to feed the upper gardens and the Water Stairway. The main channel runs lower, through the palace. The two rejoin past the Albercones reservoir, a medieval tank of about 400 cubic metres, and go on toward the Alhambra. It is gravity engineering on a valley-wide scale.

Often told, thinly sourced

  • On the hill above the Generalife, a recent documentary describes very deep wells, roughly 60 metres, where animal-powered waterwheels (norias) lifted water further uphill in stages. Treat the figures as approximate.
  • The same programme claims the Nasrids used a pressure device to push water about 6 metres up to the Alcazaba, and that nobody else in medieval Iberia could. We found nothing to back this up, so treat it with real caution.

Myth check

The name "Generalife" is traditionally explained as Jannat al-'Arif and often translated "Garden of the Architect." Scholars dispute this, and other readings have been proposed.

Stop 13

Patio de la Acequia and the Sala Regia

15 minutes

Where you are

Through the palace entrance you reach a long, narrow courtyard with the Acequia Real running down its whole length in a channel, beds on either side and a pavilion at each end. Stand at the south end and look north toward the taller pavilion.

Look for

  • The arcing jets. They are pretty, but they are 19th-century.
  • The mirador projecting from the middle of the western wall: probably the earliest known mirador in Nasrid architecture. Step in and look back across the ravine at the Alhambra.
  • On the north pavilion, the upper gallery added by the Catholic Monarchs in 1494.
  • The three-arched stucco entrance to the Sala Regia, with Ibn al-Jayyab's inscriptions. Inside are a wooden ceiling, a muqarnas frieze, and a projecting mirador (added by Isma'il I in 1319) looking toward the Albaicín.

The story

After a fire in 1958, excavations found the Nasrid garden about 70 centimetres below today's beds. The original beds were sunken below the walkways, planted low with things like myrtle, with deeper pits for orange trees. The old level was then covered over and replanted.

The architecture

Picture it the Nasrid way: no jets, plants at the level of your feet, water running quietly down the middle. The channel at your feet is the same water that goes on to feed the whole Alhambra. Garden historians read this display of abundant water as a statement of royal power and good stewardship: there may be no water on this hill, but the sultan has it, and he uses it to make the land fruitful, as a good Muslim ruler should. Unlike the palaces, the Generalife had no grand state rooms. It was a place for the court's pleasure, close enough for a day trip. And note the miradors, rooms built to frame a view, forerunners of the Mirador de Lindaraja.

Often told, thinly sourced

  • Beside the entrance to the north portico, two small wall niches (tacas) are said to have held cool water jars. An inscription attributed to Isma'il I compares them to maidens greeting guests. We couldn't confirm the inscription's wording.

Patio del Ciprés de la Sultana, the Water Stairway and the Mirador Romántico

Where you are

Look for

The architecture

Look out from it and you can read the whole system: the channel, the palace, the orchards, and the Alhambra across the ravine.

Part 6

The Palace of Charles V and the Alhambra Museum

30–45 minutes
16

Stop 16

Palace of Charles V

Where you are

Start on the esplanade in front of the west facade, then walk inside to the circular courtyard.

Look for

  • Outside, the rusticated lower storey and the Ionic upper storey. The main facade is 63 metres wide and 17 metres high.
  • Bronze rings set into the lower pilasters.
  • The portals in Sierra Elvira stone, and on the facade, a relief of the Battle of Pavia, one of Charles's victories. At Pavia, in northern Italy in 1525, Charles's army crushed the French and captured King Francis I himself. It was the high point of his power, and he had it carved onto his palace here.
  • Inside, the circular courtyard, about 30 metres across, with Doric columns below and Ionic above, set inside a 63-metre square.

The story

Charles V was Ferdinand and Isabella's grandson: king of Spain from 1516 and Holy Roman Emperor from 1519, ruler of much of Europe and the Americas. In 1526 he married Isabella of Portugal in Seville and spent several months in the Alhambra, where he decided to build a second royal residence. Pedro Machuca, trained in Rome, designed it. Work began in 1527, with Machuca directing on site from 1533 until his death in 1550; his son Luis built the courtyard. It is often said that a special tax paid by the Moriscos (Granada's converted Muslims) largely funded the work.

Charles never lived in it, and nor did his son Philip II, who moved the court to Madrid. Work stopped for fifteen years after the Morisco rebellion of 1568 and was abandoned in 1637 without a roof. Restoration began in 1923, and the building was completed only in the mid-20th century (sources give 1956 to 1967).

Who were the Moriscos? After 1492 Granada's Muslims were promised freedom of religion. Between 1499 and 1502 they were forced to choose baptism or exile, and those who stayed became "Moriscos", nominal Christians whose language, dress and customs were steadily banned. That pressure exploded in the great revolt of 1568 in the Alpujarras, the valleys on the far side of the Sierra Nevada that you can see from the Alcazaba. It was crushed, Granada's Moriscos were scattered across Castile, and between 1609 and 1614 they were expelled from Spain altogether. The half-built palace is, among other things, a monument to that story.

The architecture

A circle inside a square: a Renaissance ideal set down in a Nasrid palace-city. Machuca split the plan along a diagonal into an emperor's side and an empress's side, each with its own staircase, meeting at an octagonal chapel. Stand in the middle and compare it with the Court of the Lions: one all mass, the other all columns and light.

Often told, thinly sourced

  • The palace sits at a slight angle to its neighbours. One story says the archbishop objected to it crowding the church on the site of the old mosque. Unconfirmed.
  • The round court is often compared to Hadrian's Villa at Tivoli. Stand dead centre and speak or clap softly: many visitors notice an echo. The main west portal is said to have been completed under Philip II by Juan de Herrera.
  • The facades are said to carry green-marble medallions and reclining winged figures above the doorways.
  • Charles is often quoted as saying, on first seeing the Alhambra, "unfortunate the man who lost all this." No early source records it.

Stop 18

Puerta del Vino and the Plaza de los Aljibes

10 minutes

Where you are

West of the Palace of Charles V, a gate stands on its own on the edge of a wide open square. Walk around both sides of it.

Look for

  • On the west face, an Arabic plaque honouring "our Master the Sultan Abu 'Abd Allah al-Ghani billah" (Muhammad V), and above the arch a carved key with a cord. You'll meet the key again on the Gate of Justice at the end of the walk. Its meaning is debated: a sign of power to open and close, of faith as the key to paradise, or simply a Nasrid emblem. Either way, it marked an official gateway.
  • On the east face, later decoration: tile spandrels with leaves and branches in green, blue and yellow on white, a stucco frame around the upper window, traces of paint and reddish brickwork.
  • The two levels: a stone passage with a horseshoe arch below and a brick room above.
  • The square itself: cisterns lie under your feet.

The story

The gate dates from the early 14th century, usually attributed to Muhammad III, with some sources pointing to Muhammad II. It was the main entrance to the Medina, and it may once have stood among buildings that have since gone. Its east face was decorated about half a century after it was built.

The square was once a gully between fortress and palaces. In 1494 the Count of Tendilla built cisterns in it, 34 metres long, 6 wide and 8 deep, and it was later filled in. Toilets, lockers and first aid are by this gate.

Flamenco history was made here. On 13 and 14 June 1922 this square hosted the Concurso de Cante Jondo, a contest of deep song, the oldest layer of flamenco singing. It was organised by the composer Manuel de Falla and the young poet Federico García Lorca, who feared the old style was dying out. The same Falla sent Debussy the postcard of the gate in front of you.

Myth check

The name "Wine Gate" may come from residents' right to bring in untaxed wine here in the mid-16th century (1554 or 1556 in different accounts). Or it may be a garbling of an Arabic name. And Debussy never saw it. His piano prelude La Puerta del Vino (1913) was sparked by a postcard of the gate that Manuel de Falla sent him in late 1909.

Stop 19

The Alcazaba

35–45 minutes

Where you are

The Alcazaba, the fortress on the western tip of the hill, is entered from the Plaza de los Aljibes. Since 23 September 2026 it has had an adapted route covering the Jardín de los Adarves, the Plaza de Armas and Barrio Castrense, and the terrace of the Puerta de las Armas. Follow it.

Look for

  • In the Jardín de los Adarves, the narrow garden along the southern ramparts, the upper basin that once crowned the Lions fountain.
  • On the wall of the Torre de la Pólvora, at the west end of the garden, the plaque with lines by the Mexican poet and diplomat Francisco de Icaza: "Give him alms, woman, for there is nothing in life like the grief of being blind in Granada."
  • In the Plaza de Armas, the foundations of the Barrio Castrense: the soldiers' quarter, with houses, a bakery, a bath, a cistern, workshops and underground silos and dungeons.
  • At the eastern end, the tall Torre del Homenaje (the keep), which guarded the main entrance.
  • At the western prow, the Torre de la Vela and its bell.
  • The walls themselves: reddish rammed earth. Look for rows of small square holes. Builders packed a mix of earth, lime and gravel into wooden formwork, one layer at a time, and the holes are where the crossbeams of that formwork sat. Corners and arches are often reinforced with brick or stone.
  • From the ramparts and the Puerta de las Armas terrace, the view: the Albaicín across the Darro valley, the city and the Vega plain to the west, and the Sierra Nevada behind you on a clear day.

The story

This is where the Alhambra began. Muhammad I, founder of the Nasrid dynasty, started building in 1238 on the site of an older castle. The keep, about 26 metres high with five vaulted floors, is one of the two oldest towers with the Vela, and tradition says the founder lived on its top floor.

The garden was made in the 17th century by the Marquis of Mondéjar, the governor of the day, who filled the moat between the outer and inner walls. The Vase of the Gazelles stood here once.

The Torre de la Vela. As of the latest report (28 September 2026), the tower was still "temporarily closed" for technical inspections after the earthquakes. Check the signs; if it's open, climb it. Its name says what it was for: vela means watch or vigil. It is 26.8 metres high on a 16-metre-square base, with four floors plus a cellar.

On 2 January 1492, the banners of Ferdinand and Isabella were raised on it as Granada surrendered. Christopher Columbus was in Granada, still waiting for royal backing for his voyage west; he got it that April, and he later wrote that he saw the royal banners "planted by force of arms upon the towers of the Alhambra." The bell, hung in 1840 and rebuilt in 1882 after lightning damage, signalled night-time irrigation turns to farmers on the plain below and doubled as a clock and an alarm. It is still rung every 2 January, and folklore says a single woman who rings it that day will marry within the year. In 1590 the same Darro-valley powder blast you have been following all day damaged this tower too; it was repaired that year.

How Granada fell, and the Moor's sigh. The end was negotiated, not stormed. The Capitulations of Granada were signed on 25 November 1491, guaranteeing Muslims their faith, property and laws, promises broken within a decade. On 2 January 1492 Boabdil handed over the city; tradition places the handing over of the keys down by the Genil river, where the small Ermita de San Sebastián stands. Legend supplies the epilogue. Riding south into exile, Boabdil stopped on a pass for a last look at Granada and wept, and his mother Aixa told him to weep like a woman for what he had not defended like a man. The pass is still called the Suspiro del Moro, the Moor's Sigh. Look south from these ramparts toward the mountains as you hear it. The story was first written down in the 1530s as hearsay, so treat it as legend.

The architecture

The name al-Hamra, "the red one," comes from the colour of these rammed-earth walls, made with local clay rich in iron oxide. The palaces could be all lace and light only because these plain, thick walls existed.

Often told, thinly sourced

  • A local legend says the Jardín de los Adarves was paid for with gold coins found hidden in jars inside the walls.
  • The Torre de la Vela is said to have come through the earthquakes of 1522 and 1821. We couldn't confirm this.
  • Guides say you enter the Alcazaba below the Torre Quebrada ("Broken Tower"), and that a rounded 16th-century tower, the Torre del Cubo, was added to brace the keep.
  • After 1492 the Count of Tendilla is said to have spent years adapting the fortress for gunpowder artillery. The rounded bastion by the Torre de la Pólvora is the example usually given.
  • The Torre de las Armas on the north side is said to have linked the fortress to the Albaicín by a bridge over the Darro.
  • Rammed earth and plaster, all dug or made on the spot, let Nasrid builders put up buildings far faster than the cut-stone builders of Christian Europe. One documentary says a building could take two or three years.
  • Some link the name al-Hamra to the founder's nickname as well: Muhammad I was known as Ibn al-Ahmar, "son of the red one."

Finale

Out by the Puerta de la Justicia

15–20 minutes

Where you are

Leave the Alcazaba, cross the Plaza de los Aljibes and pass the Puerta del Vino to the Puerta de la Justicia, the great gate in the southern wall. Go through it slowly, if it is open as an exit. From outside, the path drops through the woods to the Cuesta de Gomérez and Plaza Nueva. Or, if you need the Access Pavilion (for a bus or a car), walk back east along the Calle Real and the Cypress Walk, about 20 minutes.

Look for

  • An open hand carved in the keystone of the great outer arch.
  • A key above the inner arch.
  • The Arabic foundation inscription, dating the gate to 1348 under Yusuf I. Foundation stones like this are rare in the Alhambra.
  • An inscription inlaid in white Macael marble with slate, the only documented use of the technique in the Alhambra.
  • A Gothic Virgin and Child in a niche above the key. It is a copy. The original, from 1501 and attributed to Roberto Alemán, is in the Museo de Bellas Artes, upstairs in the Palace of Charles V.
  • The sequence of arches as you go through, and the original metal-sheathed wooden door.

The story

Its Arabic name, Bab al-Shari'a, is usually translated "Gate of the Esplanade," sometimes "Gate of the Law." The Catholic Monarchs set their Virgin over a sultan's foundation inscription: the layering of conquest you have seen all day.

Often told, thinly sourced

  • The passage is usually described as a bent entrance, turning at right angles so attackers could not charge straight through. Watch for the turns as you walk it; we couldn't confirm the exact number of bends.
  • The Pilar de Carlos V beside the gate is said to bear the arms of Burgundy and Lorraine among its imperial heraldry.

Myth check

The hand and key are Islamic symbols whose exact meaning is debated. The hand is popularly read as the five pillars of Islam, or as a charm against the evil eye. The often-told story that judges held court under the gate has no documentary support. And the legend Washington Irving retells: the Alhambra will stand until the hand reaches down and grasps the key, and on that day the whole fortress will fall.

If you have more time

The Dobla de Oro ticket pairs the Alhambra with a set of Nasrid-era monuments down in the Albaicín. Check what is included when you buy. Over the years it has covered the Corral del Carbón, El Bañuelo, the Casa del Horno de Oro and the Palacio de Dar al-Horra. Some of these closed after the August earthquakes. According to the official ticket site, Dar al-Horra and the Horno de Oro house reopened on 24 October 2026, and the Maristán, the old Nasrid hospital, on 28 October.

Often told, thinly sourced

  • On certain days an extended visit is offered that adds the Carmen de los Porcel and the Torres Bermejas, the "Vermilion Towers" on the next hill south. The towers are among the oldest fortifications in Granada. Check the ticket site for days and access; the towers are reported not to be accessible for people with limited mobility.

Closing: what you've walked through

You began in a council chamber and ended at a fortress gate. In between you saw how one dynasty built a public stage, a private paradise and a country estate, all fed by one channel of mountain water, and what came after: chapels, an emperor's palace, Romantic writers and the restorers who put the lions back.

Much of it is repaired or rebuilt, from the Barca's ceiling to the Generalife's jets, which makes the surviving Nasrid work more remarkable, not less. Remember, too, the people this guide hasn't named: the labourers who rammed the earth, cut the tiles and carved the plaster by hand, with ropes, pulleys and no machines. We celebrate the sultans, but the walls are their work. Before you head down the hill, look back at the red walls. The motto carved inside them says no one is the victor but God. The sultans lost the city in 1492, and what they built is still standing.
