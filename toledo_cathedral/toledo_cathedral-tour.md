# Toledo_Cathedral tour: full text


5494 words, about 45 minutes with pauses.


## Stop 0. The Cathedral: A broken promise · Room Outside, by the Puerta Llana on Calle Cardenal Cisneros

*522 words, about 3.7 min*


### The king is away

Welcome, Monica. Play this outside, before you go in, or while you wait in the queue at the Puerta Llana, the plain door on the side street. It is the only door of this cathedral with no steps up to it.

Picture this spot nine hundred and forty years ago. No Gothic towers. A great mosque, the main mosque of a Muslim city. And before the mosque, people said, a Visigothic church. The ground under your feet has changed faith twice.

Then a Christian king, Alfonso the Sixth of Castile, takes Toledo. The city surrenders on terms. One of the terms is a promise. The Muslims of Toledo will keep their great mosque.

It lasted about a year.


### The queen and the monk

The king rides out of the city. Behind him stay two people who never liked that promise. His French queen, Constance. And the new archbishop, Bernard, a monk from the great abbey of Cluny in Burgundy.

While the king is away, they send armed men into the mosque. They set up an altar. They hang a bell in the minaret. The mosque is a church.

The king hears, and he is furious. One later chronicler says he swore to put the guilty to death.

> *Legend voice:* Now the legend. The Muslims of Toledo, it is said, sent their wisest man, a jurist named Abu Walid, to meet the angry king. And the jurist begged him to forgive. Let it stand, he said. Do not shed blood for our mosque. The king's anger cooled. And the cathedral never forgot. Inside, on a pillar of the main chapel, they say a carved figure honours that Muslim peacemaker.

That is legend; no document proves it. You will meet him later.


### Two and a half centuries of stone

The old mosque served as cathedral for well over a hundred years. Then, in twelve twenty-six, a king laid the first stone of this one. It took over two and a half centuries. If you walked past the main front, in the square, you saw it. Two towers were planned. Only one was ever finished. The other stops short and wears a small dome, like a hat.

The archbishops of Toledo were the richest churchmen in Spain. Almost everything inside was paid for by such men, and every one wanted to be remembered.


### How to use this tour

Nine stops follow, in the order you will walk, with a short goodbye at the end. Each stop tells you where to go. When you hear a pause, stop the track, look, then play on. Things get moved. If something is not where I say, check the floor plan.

The bell tower climb may need its own ticket or a time slot. Ask at the ticket shop before you go in.

If you only have thirty minutes, do these five. The war carved in wood around the choir. The hole in the roof behind the high altar. The painted ceiling of the chapter house. El Greco's red robe in the sacristy. And the tower of silver in the treasury.

Now go in through the Puerta Llana. Once inside, look along the walls near the door for a painted giant. When you find him, play stop one.


## Stop 1. The Nave: A giant, a stone, and a book that would not burn · Room South aisle by the entrance, then west along the nave to the Mozarabic chapel

*539 words, about 3.9 min*


### The man who keeps you alive today

Stop one. You are just inside. Somewhere on the wall near the Lions' Door, the great carved door at the end of the cross aisle, there is a painted man as tall as a house. A giant wading through water with a child on his shoulder. Find him.

Saint Christopher, the ferryman who carried the Christ child across a river.

> *Legend voice:* Why so big? Because of a belief. In the Middle Ages, people said that if you looked at Saint Christopher, you would not die a sudden death that day. So churches painted him huge, by the doors. You have just looked. You are safe until midnight.


### The night the Virgin came down

Now walk west along the nave, toward the main doors, under the forest of pillars. Look for a small chapel built between two pillars, standing almost free in the aisle, with a little roof of carved stone. Check the floor plan if you cannot see it.

Thirteen centuries ago, the archbishop of this city was a scholar named Ildefonsus. He wrote a fierce book in honour of the Virgin Mary.

> *Legend voice:* And this is what Toledo tells. One night, before dawn, he came into his church to sing the morning prayers. The Virgin was seated on his chair, surrounded by angels. She thanked him for his book, and laid on his shoulders a robe made in heaven.

That is legend. This chapel stands where the altar of the old Visigothic church is believed to have stood. Behind its grille the cathedral keeps a stone. The stone, they say, on which the Virgin set her feet.

Find it through the grille. A plain piece of rock, in a building of gold.


### The book that would not burn

Now carry on to the far corner, by the main doors, under the tower that was never finished. It may be open only for its morning service; look through the bars.

For nearly four hundred years Toledo was a Muslim city. Its Christians stayed, and kept praying in their own old way, in the words of the Visigoths. They were called Mozarabs. Then a Christian king took the city, and Rome wanted that old rite gone.

> *Legend voice:* The legend says Toledo fought back. First, a duel. Two knights, one for the old rite, one for the Roman. The Toledan knight won. The king was not satisfied. So, a trial by fire. Both prayer books thrown on a bonfire. And the old Toledan book would not burn. But the king chose Rome anyway. They say that is where Spaniards got an old proverb. Laws go where kings want.

The legend is a later story. What is true is this. The old rite did not die. A few parishes kept it, barely. Four centuries later, Cardinal Cisneros, the hard, ascetic friar who ran Spain for Queen Isabella, had its books printed and built this chapel for it. Its mass is still said here every morning.

If you can see the walls, look for battle paintings. The conquest of Oran, in North Africa, a war Cisneros led himself, an old friar riding with the army.

A way of praying older than this building, kept alive inside it.


### Walk on

Walk on. Go to the opposite corner of the church, under the finished tower, to the treasury chapel. Play stop two.


## Stop 2. The Treasury: The queen's jewel inside the tower of silver · Room Treasury chapel, at the foot of the bell tower, near the main doors

*508 words, about 3.6 min*


### A queen's things for sale

Stop two. The treasury chapel, at the foot of the tower. In the middle, behind glass, a tower of silver taller than a man. Walk all the way round it before you listen on.

Begin with a death. Queen Isabella dies, and she has left orders. Her debts must be paid and her promises kept, so her possessions go up for sale. The private treasures of the most powerful woman in Europe.

Among them is a small monstrance. A case of gold to hold the holy bread of the Mass, so that people can see it carried before them. It had been made for her, by a goldsmith in Barcelona. The canons of this cathedral buy it.

And then the man who had been her confessor, Cardinal Cisneros, the friar you met in the last stop, has an idea. He wants a setting for the queen's jewel. Not a box. A building.


### A cathedral you can carry

He hires a German goldsmith living in Spain, Enrique de Arfe. Arfe works on it for seven years.

Look at it now as Arfe meant it. It is a Gothic church in miniature. Buttresses, pinnacles, pointed arches, a spire. On it, they say, about two hundred and sixty tiny figures.

Find the heart of it. Behind the glass, in the middle of the silver tower, a smaller piece, golden. That is Isabella's monstrance. The queen's private piece, swallowed by a cathedral.

> *Legend voice:* Now the tradition. The guides of Toledo love to say that the gold of this inner piece came from the first gold Columbus brought back from the Americas, and that the queen gave it for this. Nobody has proved it. But stand here and think about it. Gold from a world the queen had never seen, melted down to hold a scrap of bread.


### One day a year

Once a year, at the feast of Corpus Christi, it comes out of this room. It is lifted onto a carriage and taken through the streets of Toledo, under awnings hung across the narrow lanes against the sun. It has made that journey for about five hundred years.

Arfe left it in silver. Later generations, it is said, gilded it and set it on a grander base. Everyone who looked after it wanted his share in it.


### The king's picture book

Look around the room for books in cases. If it is on show, one of them is a great treasure of the Middle Ages: a Bible in three volumes made in Paris for the French king Louis the Ninth, the crusader who became a saint. Thousands of tiny painted pictures. How it came to Toledo is not certain; the stories differ.

A saint king. A queen who backed Columbus. A goldsmith from Germany. All gathered in one small room, because a frugal friar wanted the holy bread to ride through the streets in a cathedral of its own.


### Walk on

Walk on. Go back toward the centre of the church, to the great enclosed choir in the middle of the nave. Find the way in, on the side that faces the high altar. Play stop three.


## Stop 3. The Choir: A war carved while it was still being fought · Room The choir, enclosed in the middle of the nave

*538 words, about 3.8 min*


### News from the front

Stop three. The choir, the walled room in the middle of the nave where the canons sang. Step in. Two rows of wooden seats run round you, one high, one low. Go close to the lower row, and look at the carved panels above the backs of the seats, at about the height of your chest.

Picture a workshop in Toledo. A German woodcarver, Rodrigo Alemán, has just been given a strange order by the canons. Carve the war.

The war is the war of Granada. The last Muslim kingdom in Spain. Isabella and Ferdinand are taking it town by town, castle by castle. And it is not over. When the carver starts, the Alhambra is still Muslim. News comes in. Another town has fallen. A new panel is begun.

Fifty-four panels. Most of them, a historian worked out, can be matched to a real town or castle, often by a name carved on them.

Look closely at one. Walls and towers like a child's castle. Ladders. Knights in armour, with banners. And on the walls, the defenders in turbans. Look for a gate opening. A surrender.

Now find a face on the losing side. You have walked through the Alhambra. These are the people who lost it. Imagine one of them about to give up the city where his grandparents are buried.


### The carver's jokes

Now look down, under the seats. Folded up, each seat shows a small ledge, so an old canon could lean while seeming to stand. On these ledges and on the armrests, look for animals, monsters, odd little faces.

> *Legend voice:* Rodrigo Alemán had a name for mischief. In another cathedral, at Plasencia, his carvings under the seats are famous for being rude. And Plasencia tells a legend about him. That he was shut up in a tower, built himself wings, and jumped. It is only a story.


### The duel upstairs

Now look up at the high row of seats, behind and above you. A generation later, another archbishop wanted the upper stalls. He did not hire one master. He hired two, and gave each one half.

On one side, Felipe Bigarny, from Burgundy, the old master. On the other, Alonso Berruguete, a Spaniard who had been to Italy as a young man, and had seen Michelangelo at work. The old style against the new.

Compare the two sides. Bigarny's figures are calm, balanced. Berruguete's twist and strain, as if the wood could not hold them. Which side is whose? Look at which side makes you nervous. That is Berruguete. The old master died before the work was done.

At the far end, the archbishop's throne, and above it Berruguete's Transfiguration, Christ rising in light. He took the place of honour.


### The White Virgin

Last, look for a stone statue of the Virgin and Child, painted white, standing on the altar inside the choir.

Look at the mother, then at the child. Their faces turn to each other. Is she smiling? Decide for yourself. She was already famous in the fourteenth century, more than a hundred years before the war around her was carved.

A mother and a baby, in the middle of a war.


### Walk on

Walk on. Leave the choir and cross to the high altar, behind the tall iron grille. Play stop four.


## Stop 4. The Main Chapel: The third king of Spain · Room Capilla Mayor, the high altar, seen through the grille facing the choir

*522 words, about 3.7 min*


### A wall of gold

Stop four. Stand at the great grille of the high altar, with your back to the choir. Look.

A wall of carved and gilded wood, rising almost to the vault. Hundreds of figures, set in rows like the windows of a building. It tells the life of Christ, from the bottom up, and at the very top, under a painted sky of stars, the crucifixion.

It was built about five hundred years ago, for Cardinal Cisneros, the friar you have already met twice. To make room for something this big, the chapel itself had to be enlarged, and the tombs of old kings had to be moved.

Look up at the top of the grille in front of you. The double-headed eagle of the emperor Charles the Fifth. If you saw Titian's Charles on horseback in the Prado, this is his badge, in iron.


### The cardinal who would not lie in a side chapel

Now look to your left, inside the chapel, in the side wall. A tomb of white marble, shaped like a Roman triumphal arch.

This is the tomb of Cardinal Pedro González de Mendoza. In his day, people called him the third king of Spain. He was Isabella's closest adviser.

> *Legend voice:* They say that when Granada fell, it was the cardinal's silver cross that was raised first on the towers of the Alhambra. It is a story told about that day.

He also had sons. A cardinal, with children, and nobody pretended otherwise. Isabella, it is reported, called them the cardinal's three beautiful sins.

Now the fight. Mendoza wanted to be buried here, in the main chapel, beside the kings. The canons of the cathedral said no. This was the place of kings. Then the queen stepped in. She and the archbishop overruled the chapter. The old wall was broken through to make space for him. And look at the style. Not Gothic. A classical arch, straight out of Renaissance Italy, one of the first in all of Castile. Even in death, the cardinal arrived first.


### Two strangers on the pillars

Now look at the pillars that frame the chapel, at the edges of the grille. Among the stone figures high up, two do not belong to any Bible story. Check the floor plan if you cannot find them.

On one side, a figure Toledo calls the alfaquí, the jurist. Tradition says this is Abu Walid, the Muslim jurist from the opening track, who begged the king to spare the men who seized his mosque. His people lost their mosque, and he is honoured inside it.

> *Legend voice:* On another pillar, a shepherd. Toledo calls him Martín Alhaja. The legend says that before the great battle of Las Navas de Tolosa, when a Christian army was trapped in the mountains, a shepherd appeared and showed the king a secret pass. The army got through and won.

Both are legends. But think about who is up there. The kings lie below. On the pillars, a Muslim peacemaker and a shepherd from the hills.


### Walk on

Walk on. Go round the right-hand side of the main chapel into the curved walkway behind it, the ambulatory. On your right, look for a doorway into a hall, the chapter house. Play stop five.


## Stop 5. The Chapter House: A room that watches you decide · Room Sala Capitular, off the right-hand side of the ambulatory

*499 words, about 3.6 min*


### The men who ran the money

Stop five. The chapter house. You enter through a small antechamber, then the hall. Before anything else, stop in the doorway of the hall and look up.

A ceiling of carved wood, gold and colour, set in deep boxes, like the inside of a jewel case. It was made by Toledan carpenters in the Mudéjar way, the craft Christian Spain learned from its Muslim builders, the craft of the Alhambra ceilings. And it was made only a few years after Granada fell.

This is where the canons met. The chapter. Picture them, a few dozen men in black, seated on the benches round these walls. They managed the income of the richest church in Spain. They hired the painters and the carvers. They argued with the archbishop. You have already met them saying no, to a cardinal who wanted a tomb among the kings.


### The Judgement on the wall

Now look at the walls above the benches. Painted all over, by Juan de Borgoña, the leading painter of Toledo in Cisneros's day. Scenes from the life of the Virgin. Find the end wall.

The Last Judgement. Souls rising from their graves. Some lifted to heaven. Some dragged down to hell. Look at the faces of the damned.

Think about where it is. Not in a chapel for the people. In the boardroom. Every time these men voted on money or on a fight with the archbishop, this was in front of them. You will answer for what you decide in this room.


### A thousand years of faces

Now look lower, along the walls, under the paintings. A long row of portraits, one after another, like a family album. Every archbishop of Toledo, in order.

The oldest faces are invented. Those men lived in Roman and Visigothic times, and nobody knew what they looked like.

Then, as you walk along, the faces become real people. Look for one in particular. A lean, old man, seen in profile, with a long nose and a hollow cheek, in a plain dark habit. Cardinal Cisneros. Check the names painted under the portraits.

This portrait is one of the few likenesses of him made in his lifetime. A friar who walked barefoot, who slept on boards, they said, under his silk. Who ran Spain twice as regent. The man behind the gold altar, the silver tower, the old rite, and this room.

And he chose to paint himself into a line more than a thousand years long. He was telling anyone who entered: I am not one man. I am the latest in a chain that goes back to the saints.


### Still counting

Now go to the other end of the row. The series has never stopped. Each new archbishop has been added. Find the last face on the wall.

Every archbishop who sits in this room knows that one day his own face will join the line.


### Walk on

Walk on. Go back out into the ambulatory, and continue round behind the high altar. Stop when the light changes, then turn and look up. Play stop six.


## Stop 6. The Transparente: The archbishop who cut a hole in the roof · Room Ambulatory, directly behind the high altar

*505 words, about 3.6 min*


### The problem in the dark

Stop six. You are in the walkway behind the high altar. On one side, a wall of marble and bronze, swirling up toward the roof. Do not look at it yet. Look up first, at the vault above your head, on the opposite side.

There is a hole. A great opening, cut through the stone vault of a Gothic cathedral, framed with painted clouds and figures. Daylight pours through it.

Here is why. On the other side of this wall, inside the high altar, the cathedral kept the holy bread of the Mass in its tabernacle. And it sat in the dark, behind the huge gilded altarpiece you saw in stop four.

An archbishop, Diego de Astorga, wanted that to change. He wanted light to fall on the holy bread, and he wanted people behind the altar to be able to see through to it. Hence the name. The Transparente. The see-through.


### The sculptor and his sons

He gave the job to the cathedral's architect, Narciso Tomé, a sculptor who worked with his sons. They worked for years. Then they did the thing no medieval builder would have dared. They cut through the vault of the ambulatory and let the sky in.

Now turn and look at the marble. Stand back. Clouds of stone. Angels tumbling down on rays of gilded bronze. In the middle, an oval opening, the window to the tabernacle, ringed with angels. Above it, find a table and figures around it. The Last Supper, carved in marble, floating in mid air.


### Eighth wonder, or a monster

When it was unveiled, the city held festivities. A poet called it the eighth wonder of the world. It cost a fortune, two hundred thousand ducats, it is said.

Then taste changed. Later critics, who loved the calm lines of ancient Greece and Rome, found it overloaded and theatrical. People still argue.

Which are you? Stand there a moment and decide.

The archbishop got what he wanted in one more way. Look at the floor in front of it, or check the floor plan. He is buried here, at the foot of his own work, under the light he let in.


### Hats hanging from the sky

Now look up again, into the vaults round about, and into the chapels opening off this walkway. Look for something small and red, hanging on cords high above the floor. A wide, flat hat with tassels.

These are cardinals' hats. When a cardinal of Toledo was buried, his red hat was hung from the vault above his tomb.

> *Legend voice:* And the guides of Toledo tell what happens next. The hat stays there, gathering dust, rotting slowly. And when at last it falls to the floor, they say, the cardinal's soul is free of purgatory, and goes up to heaven.

That is a saying, not doctrine. Count how many hats are still hanging on.


### Walk on

Walk on round the ambulatory. Just behind you is the central chapel of Saint Ildefonsus, with a cardinal's tomb in the middle. Next to it, the chapel of Santiago, with two white tombs side by side. Find that one. Play stop seven.


## Stop 7. The Chapel of Santiago: The favourite who lost his head · Room Capilla de Santiago, off the ambulatory, beside the central chapel of Saint Ildefonsus

*512 words, about 3.7 min*


### The man who ruled the king

Stop seven. The chapel of Santiago. Look through the grille. In the middle, two tombs of white alabaster side by side. A man in armour. A woman beside him. Both with their hands joined in prayer.

The man is Álvaro de Luna. For three decades he was the most powerful man in Castile. More powerful than the king.

He had come to court as a boy, and the boy king took to him. The king made him Constable of Castile, then Master of the Order of Santiago, with its castles and its armies. The great lords hated him for it. Again and again they rose against him. Again and again he won.

And he built this chapel as his own tomb, in the cathedral of the first city of Castile. A grand chapel, with his family's crescent moons in the stone. Look for them.


### The statue that knelt

> *Legend voice:* Now the legend. On his first tomb here, they say, Don Álvaro placed a figure of himself in gilded bronze. And it had a secret. During Mass, by some hidden mechanism, the bronze man rose from his tomb, and knelt, and prayed.

That it moved is legend. That there was a gilded bronze statue is recorded. And its end is recorded too. When his enemies took Toledo, they had it pulled down and melted. When Don Álvaro heard, he is reported to have laughed. There is no point, he said, in taking revenge on a statue.

The enemies would get the man himself instead.


### A basin for coins

In the end it was the queen. The king's second wife distrusted the favourite, and she worked on her husband. And one spring the king gave way. The man who had ruled for him for thirty years was arrested, tried in haste, and condemned.

Picture the main square of Valladolid, an early summer day. A scaffold. He climbs it. And afterwards his head is set high on a post and left there for days, for the crowd to see.

An old account adds one more thing. His body lay on the scaffold beside a basin. A basin for coins, collected from passers by, to pay for his burial. The richest man in Castile, buried on charity.

And the queen who brought him down? She was the mother of Isabella, then a baby of two. The queen of the choir stalls and the silver tower.


### The sad countess

His widow, Juana Pimentel, signed her letters after that as the sad countess. She, and later their daughter, finished this chapel and brought him here. The tombs you see were made decades after his death.

Look at the corners of the tombs. Small kneeling figures keep watch, at his, knights of his order; at hers, friars.

And look at his face. Calm, eyes closed, hands together. No hint of the scaffold. His family carved him the death he did not have.


### Walk on

Walk on round the ambulatory, past more chapels, toward the far side of the church. Look for the large chapel of the New Kings, and beyond it the door of the sacristy. Go into the sacristy. Play stop eight.


## Stop 8. The Sacristy: El Greco against the cathedral · Room Sacristy and picture gallery, off the left side of the ambulatory as you face the high altar

*538 words, about 3.8 min*


### The young foreigner

Stop eight. The sacristy. A long, high hall hung with paintings. Walk to the far end and stand before the painting on the end wall. A man in a robe of blazing red, in a crowd of grey faces and steel.

Toledo, the late fifteen seventies. A Greek painter in his thirties has just arrived from Italy. Few in Spain know his name; they will soon call him simply the Greek. Through a friend from Rome, the dean's son, he gets his first great Spanish commission. A painting for this room. Christ, about to be stripped of his clothes before the crucifixion.

He gives them this.

Look at the red. It is the loudest colour in the cathedral. Around him, helmets and faces pushing in. Find the hands already reaching for the robe. And at the lower right, find the man bent over, drilling holes in the cross, getting it ready.


### The quarrel

The canons did not like it. And when the time came to agree a price, they said so, in writing.

First, look at the heads. The heads of the tormentors rise above the head of Christ. Find them. To the canons, that was an insult. Nobody should stand higher than Christ.

Second, look at the lower left. Three women. The three Marys. The canons pointed out, correctly, that the Gospel does not put them here at this moment. Remove them, they said.

The painter's own expert valued the picture at nine hundred and fifty ducats. The cathedral fought it. He was awarded three hundred and fifty. Little more than a third.

And the figures? He was supposed to change them. Look again at the three women and at the heads above Christ. He never did. They are still there.

A young foreigner, against the richest church in Spain. He lost the money, and kept the painting exactly as he wanted it. If you saw his saints in the Prado, this is where that Spanish story began.


### The rest of the room

Now turn round, and look up. The ceiling is painted with a heaven full of figures, by Luca Giordano. Find the scene in it. The Virgin coming down to give Saint Ildefonsus his robe. The legend from stop one.

Along the walls, look for a set of apostles, by the Greek again. And look for Goya. A dark, crowded Arrest of Christ, a crush of faces, from the painter of the Prado's black, haunted pictures. There may be a Titian and a Caravaggio in the rooms beside; check the labels.


### Dust, ashes, nothing

Beside the sacristy is the chapel of the Sagrario. If you can see in, or check the floor plan, it holds the grave of Cardinal Portocarrero.

He stood by the bed of the last, sickly Habsburg king of Spain as he was dying without a child, and pressed him to leave his crown to a French prince. The king did. A war across Europe followed, for more than ten years.

And on his own gravestone, the cardinal ordered these words, in Latin. Here lies dust, ashes, and nothing.


### Walk on

Walk on. The last stop is the cloister. The way in is on this side of the church, toward the bell tower; follow the signs, or check the floor plan. Play stop nine.


## Stop 9. The Cloister and the Tower: A fire, and a bell that rang once · Room Lower cloister, on the north side; bell tower if your ticket includes it

*460 words, about 3.3 min*


### A market that burned

Stop nine. The cloister. Step out into the arcades round the garden. After the gold and the dark, open sky.

Before this cloister, there was no quiet here. There was a market. The alcaná, a tangle of shops and stalls pressed up against the cathedral, much of it run by Toledo's Jewish merchants. Picture the cloth, the shouting, the noise.

Then came an archbishop who wanted the ground. Pedro Tenorio. A hard man, a builder of bridges. He wanted a cloister worthy of his cathedral. The market was in the way.

> *Legend voice:* And here Toledo tells a dark story. One night, it is said, the market caught fire. The shops burned. And with the market in ashes, the archbishop could buy the land. Who started the fire? The legend says it was set on purpose, to clear the way for him. Nobody has ever proved it.

What is recorded is that work on this cloister began in the late thirteen eighties, on the ground where the market had been. Two years later, in thirteen ninety-one, mobs attacked the Jewish quarters of Castile, Toledo among them. The quiet arcades you are standing in were built in that decade.

Look at the walls now. Large painted scenes, made centuries later, of Toledo's own saints and their lives. Find the one that catches you.


### The bell that rang once

Now look up, over the roofs, at the one finished tower of the cathedral. If your ticket includes the climb, ask the staff where it starts; it may be by time slot. About three hundred steps, a spiral, up to the clock and the bells.

Up there hangs the biggest of them all. Toledo calls her la Gorda. The fat one. She weighs more than seventeen tonnes, and is nearly three and a half metres across. In her day, one of the largest bells in Europe.

She was cast in the seventeen fifties. Imagine the city waiting for her voice. Then, the first time she was rung, she cracked.

> *Legend voice:* People blamed the clapper. Too big, they said, too heavy, swung too hard. Others blamed a flaw in the casting.

What is certain is that she has been silent ever since. The largest voice in Toledo, and it spoke once.

If you go up, look down on the city from beside her. The river looping round the rock. The churches you have walked to today. And beneath you, this whole stone ship, built over a mosque, built over a church.

If you do not go up, stand in the garden and look at the tower. Somewhere near the top, behind the openings, the fat lady is hanging in the dark, cracked and silent.


### Walk on

When you are ready, play the closing track, stop ten, here in the cloister or wherever you are.


## Stop 10. Goodbye: Everyone wanted to be remembered · Room Anywhere; the cloister garden is a good place

*351 words, about 2.5 min*


### What they left behind

Stop ten, the last. Sit, if you can. Or step outside and look back at the tower.

Think of the people you met today. Not the saints. The people.

A queen and a monk who broke a king's promise while he was away. A Muslim jurist who begged for peace, and was given a place on a pillar inside the mosque he lost.

A German carver who cut a war into walnut while the war was still being fought.

A cardinal who wanted to lie among kings, and a queen who knocked down a wall for him. A friar who slept on boards and built in gold. A favourite who ruled a king for decades, and was buried with coins from a basin. A young Greek who lost his fee, and kept his three women in the picture.

And an archbishop who cut a hole in the roof to let the sun in.


### Dust and ashes

Almost all of them wanted the same thing. To be remembered. They put their faces in the chapter house, their arms on the grilles, their bodies in the best places they could buy or fight for.

Only one of them, the man under the stone that says dust, ashes, and nothing, pretended not to care. And even he made sure the words were carved.

Here is the question to take out with you. Of everything in this building, what will you remember? A gold altar? A bell that rang once? Or one face, in wood, on the walls of a town about to fall?


### Goodbye

Monica, you have walked through the Alhambra, the prize at the end of the war in the choir. You stood in front of El Greco in Madrid. And today you have seen where his Spanish story started, with a quarrel over a red robe.

Thank you for walking it with me, all the way.

When you leave, go out the way the signs show you. Step into the street, and look up one last time at the single tower. The other one was never built. The cathedral is still, in its way, unfinished.

Goodbye.
