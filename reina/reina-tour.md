## Welcome to the Reina Sofía

*569 words, about 4.1 min*


### Before we begin

Welcome to the Reina Sofía, Monica. Over the next hour, I will walk you through the stories behind some of the boldest paintings of twentieth-century Spain, and bring you to the most famous of them all, Picasso's Guernica.

Each stop is one track. When I ask you to look, I will pause. Take as long as you like. The rooms have numbers by their doors, and I will give you each one. If you lose your way, check the map or ask a guard.

Sometimes a second voice will join me. That voice tells legends and rumours. Enjoy them. Just know they are stories, not proven history.

> *Legend voice:* That is me. I will try to behave.


### The house of the dying

Now, before any painting, imagine this building with no art in it at all.

Picture long wards, row after row of iron beds. The smell of vinegar and candle wax. Nuns moving between the sick. Outside, carts bringing in the poor of Madrid, the ones with nowhere else to go.

That is what this was. In the seventeen hundreds, the king decided Madrid needed a vast new hospital. His architect, an Italian named Sabatini, planned a palace for the sick, enormous, around great courtyards.

Then the king died. The money stopped. Only a third of the plan was ever built. They opened it anyway. And for nearly two centuries, Madrid came here to be cured, or to die.


### The wrecking ball

In the nineteen sixties, the hospital closed. Its wards emptied. The great granite shell stood silent in the middle of the city, and the talk was of knocking it down.

It came very close. Then, at the last moment, the state declared it a national monument. The building was saved, not for its patients, but for its walls.

What do you do with a hospital nobody needs? Someone had a daring idea. Fill it with the most rebellious art of the century.


### Glass, and red

If you came in from the square, you passed three towers of glass and steel climbing the old stone front. They are lifts, designed with a British architect, Ian Ritchie. The old hospital keeps its stone face, and the crowds ride up past it in glass.

Behind it, the museum grew a second body, under a huge red roof by the French architect Jean Nouvel. Old stone in front. Red and glass behind. A hospital that turned into a museum, and then kept growing.


### Night shift

But a building where so many died does not forget easily. When workers began converting it, they dug into the old ground. Reports from the time say they found human remains.

> *Legend voice:* And that is where the stories start. They say that in the years after the museum opened, the guards on night duty began to dread certain rooms. Whispers in empty galleries. Screams with no one there. Doors swinging shut on their own. A lift that rose and stopped, rose and stopped, with nobody inside.

> *Legend voice:* Some swear that three nuns were found in the old chapel, mummified. And the night guards, so the legend goes, gave their ghost a name. Ataúlfo. Nobody knows who he was. Nobody has ever proved he exists. But ask a guard about him. Watch their face.

Keep that in mind as you walk. Every gallery you enter today was once a ward. People lay where the paintings now hang.


### Walk on

Take a glass lift to the second floor of the Sabatini building. That is stop one.


## Stop 1. Picasso: Woman in Blue · Room 201.02

*601 words, about 4.3 min*


### The boy with no money

Stop one. You are on the second floor of the Sabatini building, room two hundred and one point zero two. Look for a tall canvas of a woman in a long blue dress and a huge feathered hat, her face powdered white.

Before you look closely, picture a cold room in Madrid. A young man of nineteen sits in his coat, because there is no money for heat. He has already tasted Paris. He knows what he can do. Madrid does not.

His name is Pablo Picasso, and no one here has heard of him. He was born in Málaga, on the Andalusian coast you have just travelled through.

He has a plan. With a friend, he starts a magazine to shake up the city. It is called Young Art. And he paints. Night after night he paints the women of Madrid's cafés and backstreets, the ones respectable people pretend not to see.


### The big chance

Every few years, Madrid holds a great official exhibition. Ministers come. Critics come. Medals are handed out. A painter who is noticed there can make a career in a single afternoon.

The young man chooses his strongest picture. This one. He sends it in. And he waits for Madrid to notice him.

So what happened?

Look at her first. Find her face. Chalk-white powder. Red lips. Eyes that slide away from yours. She is dressed up for someone, but it is not you.

Now look at the dress. See how fast the brush moves, how loose it is. There is a defiance in it. He is not painting a lady. He is painting a woman who knows exactly what she is selling, and how little the world thinks of her.

And look at that colour. Blue. Within months, he will paint almost nothing but blue, the years of beggars, the blind, and grief. Here, the blue is still just a dress. It has not yet become a mood.


### Silence

Here is the answer. Nothing happened. Nobody noticed. The museum's own record says it simply went unnoticed.

No medal. No review. No buyer. By the time the exhibition closed, the young man had given up on Madrid and gone back to Paris. He never came back for his painting.

> *Legend voice:* There is a sharper version of the story. They say it was turned down for a prize, and that the proud young Picasso was so stung he never went to collect it. Maybe. It is the kind of thing he would do. But that part is legend.

The painting was left behind. For decades it lay forgotten. Nobody guessed who the boy had become. Only years later did someone look again and realise what they had.


### What Madrid missed

Stand back now. Think of what it would have meant. If one critic had stopped in front of this picture, Picasso might have stayed in Spain. He did not. He went to Paris, and the century followed him there.

Today, a work by him from these early years is among the most sought-after paintings on earth. This one belongs to the nation, and will never be sold. Madrid ignored it for nothing, and now it is beyond price.

So stand with her a moment. She was painted to be looked at. For half a lifetime, no one did. Does she look like she is waiting?

He never lived in Spain again. But in this building, he comes home once more, in the painting at the end of our tour.


### Walk on

Now head deeper into this floor, to room two hundred and five point zero six. Check the map or ask a guard. That is stop two.


## Stop 2. Ángeles Santos: A World · Room 205.06

*568 words, about 4.1 min*


### A girl in a quiet city

Stop two. You are in room two hundred and five point zero six, on the second floor. Look for the huge, almost square canvas, three metres each way, a tilted world of houses, stairs and stars.

Picture a provincial city in the middle of Spain. Grey streets. Church bells. Shutters closed against the heat. In one of those houses lives a teenage girl. Her father is a customs official. She has hardly been anywhere.

Her name is Ángeles Santos. And she has decided to paint the entire world.


### The book

How does a girl in a quiet city find the new art of Europe? Through a book. She gets hold of a German critic's survey of the latest painting, full of pictures she has never seen. Strange, sharp, dreamlike. She studies it. Then she starts a canvas far taller than she is.

Stand in front of it now and ask yourself one question. What kind of mind does this, at that age?

Look at the centre. The world is not round. It is a cube, floating in space, and the houses cling to it at mad angles, like they might slide off.

Now find the staircase. It climbs from the world up into the sky. Follow it with your eye. Who is on it?

Women. Look. Some carry stars up the stairs. Some light the stars with fire, like lamplighters. Find them. In this world, it is the women who keep the universe burning.

Now look down into the houses. Find the figures in rooms, the mothers, the musicians, the long strange hair. Everyone busy, everyone separate. A whole universe, and somehow everyone in it is alone.


### The revelation

She sends it to the Autumn Salon in Madrid. Nobody knows her name. The canvas is enormous. People stop dead.

The most famous showman of Madrid's literary world travels out to find her. He writes that he has found a revelation, and that the revelation is a girl of seventeen. Poets come to her door. For a few months, the quiet city has a genius, and she is a teenager.

Can you imagine it? At seventeen, the whole of literary Spain knocking on your door.


### The cost

Here the story darkens. Her painting turns wilder, rougher, angrier. Something is pulling at her.

> *Legend voice:* The story usually told is this. Soon after her triumph, she ran away from home. Her family found her, and her father had her shut away in a sanatorium. Accounts differ on the details, and it is hard to pin down. But it is told so often that it has become part of her.

When she painted again, the visions were gone. She married, she painted gentle, ordinary pictures, and for decades the world forgot that she had ever made this. The girl who painted the universe became a quiet painter of the everyday.

And yet she lived to be over a hundred. She lived long enough to see A World rediscovered, and to see crowds stand where you are standing now.


### The stairs

Look one more time at the women on the stairs, carrying their stars into the dark. A girl of seventeen painted that. And then she spent the rest of her life on the ground.

What would she have painted next, if they had let her?


### Walk on

Stay in this room. Find a smaller picture of a young woman seen from behind, at a window, looking out to sea. That is stop three.


## Stop 3. Dalí: Figure at the Window · Room 205.06

*537 words, about 3.8 min*


### The sister

Stop three. You are still in room two hundred and five point zero six. Find a young woman seen from behind, leaning on a window sill, looking out at a calm blue bay.

Picture a summer house on a rocky bay on the Catalan coast. Whitewashed walls. Fishing boats. A young man at an easel who cannot stop painting the same girl.

The girl is his sister. She is seventeen. He is twenty-one. Their mother has died a few years before, and the two of them have held on to each other. She sits for him again and again, a dozen times, maybe more. He paints her sewing, reading, staring out to sea.

He is Salvador Dalí. Not the Dalí of melting clocks and the upturned moustache. Not yet. This is the Dalí before all that.


### Look at the calm

Look at the picture. Find the curtain. It hangs perfectly straight. Now the sea. Flat, glassy, not a ripple. Find the boat on it. Now find her curls, each one painted with care.

Everything here is in order. Everything is quiet. It is a picture of a boy who loves his sister, painted as carefully as he knows how.

And now notice what he does not show you. Her face. She has her back to us. She is looking out, away from the room, away from him. It is as if he already knows she is somewhere he cannot follow.


### The guest

That same year, Dalí brings a friend home for the holidays. A poet from Granada, charming, musical, impossible not to like. Federico García Lorca.

He fills the house. He recites, he plays the piano, he makes everyone laugh. Dalí's sister is dazzled.

> *Legend voice:* And here the gossip begins. They say the sister fell for the poet, and that the poet fell for her brother. All three may have been a little in love at once. Nobody knows for sure. That is what makes the house at the bay so haunting.


### The woman who came between them

A few summers later, another guest arrives. A Russian woman, older than Dalí, married to a French poet. Her name is Gala. You will meet her at stop five.

Dalí falls for her completely. His father is outraged, and throws his son out of the family house. The sister has to choose. She chooses her father.

Twenty years go by. Dalí is now world famous, and Gala is at his side. And the sister publishes a book. Its title is simply Salvador Dalí, seen by his sister. In it she describes the quiet, sweet brother she remembers, the boy at the easel, before Gala and before the fame.

Dalí reads it as an attack. He feels his wife has been insulted. He cuts his sister off. They will go almost forty years barely speaking. Two people who once spent whole summers in one room together.


### The window

Look at her again. A girl leaning on a sill, the sea flat and blue, her brother behind her with a brush. She does not turn round.

She never would again.


### Walk on

Now find room two hundred and five point eleven, on this floor, for the women artists of the nineteen twenties. Check the map or ask a guard. Look for a crowded fairground. That is stop four.


## Stop 4. Maruja Mallo: The Fair · Room 205.11

*621 words, about 4.4 min*


### Four friends and no hats

Stop four. You are in room two hundred and five point eleven, on the second floor. Look for a crowded, spinning fairground, full of rides, stalls and very odd characters.

> *Legend voice:* Let me start with a story she told herself, years later. Picture the Puerta del Sol, the beating heart of Madrid, one ordinary day in the nineteen twenties. Crowds. Trams. Every man in a hat, every woman in a hat. That is simply how decent people go out.

> *Legend voice:* Four young friends walk into the square. Two young women painters. And two young men you have already met, Salvador Dalí and Federico García Lorca.

> *Legend voice:* In the middle of the square, they stop. And together, they take off their hats.

> *Legend voice:* In her telling, the crowd turns on them. Insults. Jeers. Even stones. As if four bare heads were an attack on Spain itself. It may have grown in the retelling. But she told it with delight for the rest of her life.

The story stuck so well that today the women of that generation have a nickname. The women without hats. Painters, poets, thinkers who refused to cover up, and who were then left out of the history books for half a century. This whole room is about bringing them back.


### The girl from the edge

The young woman who told that story is Maruja Mallo. She comes from a small town on the wild Atlantic coast of Galicia, and she arrives in Madrid hungry for everything. The art school. The cafés. The night. The street.

And she has a gift for getting noticed. Within a few years, the most powerful thinker in Spain, the philosopher Ortega y Gasset, organises a show of her paintings. A young woman painter, given that stage. It is almost unheard of.

This picture was in it.


### Into the fair

Now let yourself fall into it. This is a summer street fair in Madrid, a verbena. Imagine the noise. Barrel organs, shouting stallholders, the smell of fried dough.

Find the shooting gallery. Now find the machine where young men try to prove their strength, slamming it to ring the bell.

Now look harder. Find the giant with one eye. Find the priest, of all people, at a sideshow. Find the beggar with his guitar.

Nobody here is quite respectable. Not the church, not the soldiers, not the fine ladies. She has invited them all to her fair, and then quietly made fun of every one of them. Can you feel the laugh hiding in it?


### Exile

Then the war comes and the laughing stops. Many of her friends are killed or scattered. Lorca, the young man who took off his hat beside her, is shot.

She escapes Spain, and crosses the ocean to South America. She will not live in Madrid again for decades.

When she finally returns, almost nobody remembers her. So she makes them remember. She walks the streets of Madrid in dramatic make-up and outrageous clothes, the oldest rebel in town. And a new generation of young artists finds her, and adores her.


### The last laugh

Last year, this museum gave her the largest show of her work ever held. The girl the crowd once jeered in the Puerta del Sol came back, and filled the walls.

Look at the fair one last time. All that noise, all that colour, all those pompous people spinning round and round.

Who do you think is having the most fun here? The crowd? Or the woman who painted them?

Before you leave, look around the room. You may find more work by Ángeles Santos, the girl of stop two, in her darker years.


### Walk on

Now find room two hundred and five point thirteen, on this floor, devoted to the Surrealists. Check the map or ask a guard. That is stop five.


## Stop 5. Dalí: The Great Masturbator · Room 205.13

*650 words, about 4.6 min*


### A summer visit

Stop five. You are in room two hundred and five point thirteen, on the second floor. Look for a wide canvas with a huge soft pale head, slumped on the ground, its nose pressed into the earth, an insect clinging to its face.

Go back to the bay you saw at stop three. The same summer house. A few years later.

Dalí is now twenty-five, and suddenly famous. He and his friend from student days, Luis Buñuel, have just made a short film in Paris. It opens with a razor slicing an eye. Audiences gasp. The Surrealists, the wildest artists in Paris, want to know these two Spaniards.

And that summer, some of them come to visit Dalí at the bay. Among them is a poet, Paul Éluard, and his wife.


### Gala

Her name is Gala. She is Russian. Ten years older than Dalí. Sharp, hungry, utterly sure of herself. Dalí is shy and nervous.

The holiday ends. Her husband goes back to Paris. Gala does not. She stays behind, with Dalí.

In the weeks that follow, he paints this picture. So what is it? What happened to him that summer?


### The head

Look at the great pale head first. Find the closed eye, and the long eyelashes, resting on the ground. Experts read this head as Dalí himself, melting, overwhelmed, turned inside out by what is happening to him.

Now find the grasshopper, fixed to the face. As a boy, Dalí was so afraid of grasshoppers that his schoolmates would throw them at him to watch him panic. Here it clings to his own mouth.

> *Legend voice:* That schoolyard detail is the story Dalí liked to tell. Like most of his stories, it may be a little improved.

Now look for the ants, crawling near the mouth. To Dalí, ants meant decay. And now look for a woman's face, rising out of the head, pressed close to a man's body.

Desire and terror, in the same painting. A young man who wants something desperately, and is frightened half to death that he will get it. Does that feel strange to look at? It is meant to. Stay with it a moment.


### The price

He got her. Gala never left. She became his wife, his muse, his manager, for the rest of her life.

But it cost him. His father saw a married older woman and a ruined son, and threw him out of the family house. You remember the sister at the window. She took her father's side.

The painting was still his when he died. He left it to the Spanish state. It is one of the treasures of this museum, and it will never be sold.


### An elephant for an ashtray

One more story, for the road. Nearly forty years after this summer, Air India asked Dalí to design a gift for its passengers. He made a porcelain ashtray, a swan that turns into an elephant. As his fee, he asked for a baby elephant. And the airline sent one. A young elephant was flown from India to Europe, and delivered to the painter.


### Three friends

Remember the friends of his student days. Buñuel the filmmaker. Lorca the poet.

> *Legend voice:* They say Lorca was hurt by that famous film. Its title was An Andalusian Dog, and Lorca was an Andalusian. Buñuel later hinted that the joke was on him. Maybe it was. Lorca certainly felt left behind.

Seven summers after this painting, the war began. In its first weeks, Lorca was taken outside Granada and shot. His body has never been found.

Look one last time at the head on the ground, its eye closed. That summer he got what he wanted. In the years that followed, he lost his family, and then his friend.


### Walk on

That war leads to the painting at the heart of this museum. Follow the signs for Guernica, to room two hundred and five point ten, on this floor. Every guard knows the way. That is stop six.


## Stop 6. Picasso: Guernica · Room 205.10

*800 words, about 5.7 min*


### The empty wall

Stop six. You are on the second floor of the Sabatini building, room two hundred and five point ten. If the rooms have changed, check the map or ask a guard. You cannot miss it. It fills a whole wall, in black and white.

Do not look yet. Find a place a few steps back, and turn away from it for a moment. Listen first.

Picture a painter in Paris in the spring of nineteen thirty-seven. He is the most famous artist alive. His own country, Spain, is tearing itself apart in civil war. The Republic has asked him for one thing. A great painting for its pavilion at the World's Fair. The world will be watching.

And Picasso cannot find it. For weeks he draws a painter and a model in a studio. Pretty, quiet, pointless. The canvas waits, nearly eight metres wide. The fair is weeks away. He has nothing to say.


### A Monday in the market

Now picture a small town in the Basque hills. It is Monday afternoon, market day. Farmers have come in with cattle and carts. The square is full.

Then the planes come. They are German, the Condor Legion, sent by Hitler to help General Franco, with Italian planes beside them. For hours they bomb the town. Fighters chase people along the roads. By night, Gernika is burning.

A British reporter walks through the ruins and writes what he saw. His story crosses the world. In Paris, Picasso opens the newspapers and sees the photographs. Black, white and grey. A town that is no longer there.

Five days after the bombing, he starts to draw. He does not stop.


### Five weeks in the attic

His studio is an attic on the Left Bank, with beams and a long, dark room. The canvas is so tall he climbs a ladder and paints with long-handled brushes.

Beside him, with a camera, is Dora Maar. She is a photographer, and she is his lover. Week after week she photographs the canvas. Figures arrive, move, vanish. A raised fist appears in the middle, then disappears. It is like watching someone think.

In about five weeks, it is finished. So now, a question to hold. Why did the most celebrated painter alive, with all the colours in the world, choose none of them? Keep that question. We will come back to it.


### The mother

Now turn around and face it.

Look to the far left. A bull stands calm, almost bored, its tail curling like smoke. And under the bull, a woman throws back her head and screams at the sky.

Look at what she is holding. A baby. Its head has fallen back. Its eyes are empty. Her tongue is a blade. Stay with her a moment.

Ask yourself whether you have ever seen grief drawn so simply. A few lines. No tears. Just a mouth that will not close.


### The flower in the rubble

Now let your eyes fall to the floor of the painting. A soldier lies there in pieces, like a smashed statue. His hand still grips a broken sword.

Look very closely, just beside that hand. A small flower is growing. It is tiny. You could walk past it for years. Many people see it as the one sign of hope in the whole picture.


### The horse and the light

In the centre, the horse. A spear has gone right through it. Its mouth is open in a scream, its tongue another blade. Its body is covered in little marks, like the columns of a newspaper.

Above it, a jagged eye of light, with an electric bulb inside.

> *Legend voice:* Here is a reading you will hear in Madrid, and it is only a reading. In Spanish, a light bulb is a bombilla. A bomb is a bomba. Some say Picasso hid the bombs inside the light. He never said so. He refused to explain his pictures.

To the right, a woman leans in through a window, pushing an oil lamp into the dark, as if to say, look. Below her, another woman staggers forward. And at the far right, a figure throws up both arms inside a burning house. There is no door.


### Why there is no colour

Now step back and see the whole wall at once. Here is the answer to our question.

Picasso met this horror the way the world did. In newsprint. In grey photographs. So he painted it like that, a front page as big as a house. And look at what is not here. No planes. No uniforms. No name of a town. That is why it could later belong to every bombed city in the world.

Stand with it in silence for a moment. Look back at that mother. What would you want the world to see, if this were your town?


### Walk on

Stay where you are. The next story is about where this painting went next, and it begins here. That is stop seven.


## Stop 7. Picasso: Guernica comes home · Room 205.10

*717 words, about 5.1 min*


### The secret passenger

Stop seven. You are still on the second floor of the Sabatini building, room two hundred and five point ten, in front of Guernica. If the rooms have changed, check the map or ask a guard.

Picture a scheduled flight from New York to Madrid, in September nineteen eighty-one. More than three hundred passengers. Trays, blankets, a long night over the Atlantic. Nobody on board knows what is in the hold. Not the passengers. Not even the crew.

The plane lands. And then the pilot speaks. He tells them who has been travelling with them. Guernica has come home.

To understand why that moment mattered so much, go back to where we left the painting, in Paris.


### A cold welcome

The world's fair opened. And Guernica was no triumph. Some on the painter's own side found it baffling. They had hoped for a poster with a clear message. Instead they got screaming horses and broken statues.

So the painting went on the road. It toured Scandinavia and Britain, then crossed to America, raising money for Spanish refugees. It was rolled, unrolled, rolled again. Then the Republic lost the war. General Franco ruled Spain. And a world war began.

Guernica could not go home. There was no home to go to.


### A promise to a country

It stayed in New York, at the Museum of Modern Art, for more than forty years. Picasso made one condition, and he never moved from it. The painting would go to Spain only when Spain was free again.

He did not live to see it. Nor did Franco. Two years after the painter died, the dictator died too. Then came elections, a constitution, and finally, that flight.


### The officer at the door

> *Legend voice:* Now for the story everyone in Madrid will tell you. It is a legend. During the war, in occupied Paris, a German officer is said to have come to Picasso's studio. He saw a photograph of Guernica, and he asked, did you do this? And Picasso said, no. You did.

> *Legend voice:* Nobody has ever found a record of it. But people keep telling it, because it says exactly what they feel when they stand here.


### Kill lies all

In New York, one morning, a young man walked up to the canvas with a can of red spray paint. His name was Tony Shafrazi. He wrote three words across it. Kill lies all. He was protesting a massacre in Vietnam. He was arrested on the spot.

For a moment, it looked as if Guernica was ruined. But the paint had landed on a thick layer of varnish. Restorers wiped it away. Look at the canvas now. There is no trace.


### Behind glass

When it reached Madrid, it was hung behind bulletproof glass, with armed guards. Only months before, soldiers had burst into the Spanish parliament, firing into the ceiling, trying to bring back military rule. The country's democracy was very young. And this painting had become its symbol. People queued to see it.

A few years after it moved here, the glass came down. Now it is just you and the paint.


### What it is worth

What is it worth? It has no price. It will never be sold. For comparison, another Picasso, Women of Algiers, sold at auction in New York for about one hundred and seventy-nine million dollars. Few would call that the more important painting. This one belongs to a country.

And it will not travel again. Gernika has asked for it. Basque leaders have asked, more than once. The answer is always no. All those years of rolling and shipping left their mark. The museum says it is too fragile to move.


### A curtain in New York

A woven copy of Guernica once hung at the United Nations in New York. One day, before a speech there making the case for war in Iraq, it was hidden behind a blue curtain. The official reason was the camera background. Many people did not believe it.

Think about that. Sixty-six years after Gernika, the picture was still too dangerous to stand behind.

Before you go, find Dora Maar's photographs on the walls here. Look for the raised fist that Picasso painted out. You can take photographs too, without flash, unless the signs now say otherwise.


### Walk on

Next door is the building where this story started, the pavilion of the Spanish Republic. Look for room two hundred and five point nine, or ask a guard. That is stop eight.


## Stop 8. Sert and Lacasa: The Spanish Pavilion · Room 205.09

*653 words, about 4.7 min*


### A small house among giants

Stop eight. You are on the second floor of the Sabatini building, in room two hundred and five point nine, next to Guernica. If the rooms have changed, check the map or ask a guard. Look for a small architectural model of a building.

Picture yourself in Paris, in the summer of nineteen thirty-seven. You are walking along the Seine, through the World's Fair. Two monsters face each other across your path. The pavilion of Nazi Germany, a stone tower crowned with an eagle. And the pavilion of the Soviet Union, topped by two giant steel figures, a worker and a farm woman, striding forward.

Then, nearby, something small. Light. Almost fragile. Steel and glass and panels. It belongs to a country at war. The Spanish Republic has built a house to tell the world one thing. Help us.

Find the model now. Look at how open it is. No grand staircase, no marble. A ramp carried you up and through it, past giant photographs of schools, farms and soldiers. It was built to argue, not to impress.


### The fountain of poison

Picture the ground floor. Guernica hangs there. And in front of it, something moves. Silver liquid slides down curved metal plates, slow and heavy. It is not water. It is mercury.

The American sculptor Alexander Calder made it. The mercury came from Almadén, Spain's great mercury mine, and the town lay close to the front. Imagine the fountain saying, look what we still hold, and look what they want to take.

It was beautiful. It was also poisonous. Today the original stands behind glass in Barcelona. The museum here owns a model of it, and if it is on show, look for it.


### The man with the sickle

Climb to the upper floor, and on the wall a giant peasant in a red cap raises a sickle. Joan Miró painted him straight onto the panels, taller than three men. He is a farmer in revolt.

When the fair closed, the pavilion was pulled down. And the peasant vanished.

> *Legend voice:* What happened to him? There are only stories. One version says the panels were taken down and shipped off to Spain, and lost somewhere on the way, in the chaos of a war being lost. Nobody knows. Only black and white photographs remain. A great Miró may be lying in a crate somewhere still, or long since burned.

Now look for something of Miró's that did survive, a small poster in this room. Help Spain, it says, in French. A figure raises a fist far bigger than its head. Miró meant it as a stamp. One franc each, to raise money for the Republic.


### A path to a star

Back at the entrance stood a strange, rough column, rising some twelve metres, like a plant reaching for light. Its maker, Alberto Sánchez, had worked as a baker. Its title is a sentence. The Spanish people have a path that leads to a star.

It was lost too. But a copy has been made, and it stands outside this museum. Look up at it on your way out, and think of who made the first one.


### The black planes

Now find a painting here of a sky full of dark aeroplanes, and below them, women running, clutching children. It is called Madrid nineteen thirty-seven, black planes. It hung in the pavilion. Gernika was not alone. Madrid was bombed again and again.

Look at the mothers in it. Then think back to the mother next door, with the dead child. The same scream, painted twice in one building, that summer.

The fair ended. The pavilion came down. Within two years the Republic was gone. What it left behind is in these two rooms. A model, a poster, a few photographs. And one painting that never stopped speaking.


### Walk on

This is the last stop in the collection. When you are ready, make your way down to the ground floor. Check the map if you need it. Your final story begins on the way out.


## Leaving the Reina Sofía

*361 words, about 2.6 min*


### Thirty-eight tonnes, missing

One last story before you go. It is about this museum, and something it lost.

Picture a warehouse on the edge of Madrid. Somewhere inside, there should be four blocks of rusted steel. Each one is about chest high. Together they weigh thirty-eight tonnes. You need a crane to move them.

The American sculptor Richard Serra made them for this museum when it first opened. He set them on the floor in two pairs, like a quiet argument. The title names two bombed places. Guernica, and an American air raid on Libya that same year.

After the show, the steel went into storage, with a private company. Years passed. Then a new director ordered a count of everything the museum owned. The company had gone bust years before. The steel was gone.

Thirty-eight tonnes of art had vanished.

> *Legend voice:* Where did it go? Nobody knows. The favourite guess is that someone sold it for scrap, and it was melted down. That is only a guess, never proved. It is still one of the strangest losses in the art world.

Serra agreed to make the work again, exactly the same. But he and the museum signed a rule. Only one may exist at a time. There can never be two. If you have time, the new version is listed in room one hundred and two. Check the map.


### On your way out

As you leave, look up at the glass lift towers on the face of the old hospital. Ride one if you can, and watch the city drop away.

If you pass through the newer red building, step into its courtyard. A giant brushstroke stands there, by Roy Lichtenstein, a painter's swipe of paint, frozen solid and standing upright. After Guernica, it feels like a joke about painting itself. Let yourself smile.


### Goodbye

You have followed a painting from a burning market town to a Paris attic, across an ocean, and home again. Think of that mother, and the tiny flower by the soldier's hand.

And think of your own road, Monica. Amsterdam, then Lisbon, then the courts and fountains of the Alhambra, and now Madrid. Thank you for walking with me all the way. Enjoy Madrid.
