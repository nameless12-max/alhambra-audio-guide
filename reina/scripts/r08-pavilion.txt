# title: Stop 8. Sert and Lacasa: The Spanish Pavilion
# stop: 8
# room: 205.09

## A small house among giants

N: Stop eight. You are on the second floor of the Sabatini building, in room two hundred and five point nine, next to Guernica. If the rooms have changed, check the map or ask a guard. Look for a small architectural model of a building.

N: Picture yourself in Paris, in the summer of nineteen thirty-seven. You are walking along the Seine, through the World's Fair. Two monsters face each other across your path. The pavilion of Nazi Germany, a stone tower crowned with an eagle. And the pavilion of the Soviet Union, topped by two giant steel figures, a worker and a farm woman, striding forward.

N: Then, nearby, something small. Light. Almost fragile. Steel and glass and panels. It belongs to a country at war. The Spanish Republic has built a house to tell the world one thing. Help us.

...

N: Find the model now. Look at how open it is. No grand staircase, no marble. A ramp carried you up and through it, past giant photographs of schools, farms and soldiers. It was built to argue, not to impress.

## The fountain of poison

N: Picture the ground floor. Guernica hangs there. And in front of it, something moves. Silver liquid slides down curved metal plates, slow and heavy. It is not water. It is mercury.

N: The American sculptor Alexander Calder made it. The mercury came from Almadén, Spain's great mercury mine, and the town lay close to the front. Imagine the fountain saying, look what we still hold, and look what they want to take.

N: It was beautiful. It was also poisonous. Today the original stands behind glass in Barcelona. The museum here owns a model of it, and if it is on show, look for it.

## The man with the sickle

N: Climb to the upper floor, and on the wall a giant peasant in a red cap raises a sickle. Joan Miró painted him straight onto the panels, taller than three men. He is a farmer in revolt.

N: When the fair closed, the pavilion was pulled down. And the peasant vanished.

...

A: What happened to him? There are only stories. One version says the panels were taken down and shipped off to Spain, and lost somewhere on the way, in the chaos of a war being lost. Nobody knows. Only black and white photographs remain. A great Miró may be lying in a crate somewhere still, or long since burned.

...

N: Now look for something of Miró's that did survive, a small poster in this room. Help Spain, it says, in French. A figure raises a fist far bigger than its head. Miró meant it as a stamp. One franc each, to raise money for the Republic.

## A path to a star

N: Back at the entrance stood a strange, rough column, rising some twelve metres, like a plant reaching for light. Its maker, Alberto Sánchez, had worked as a baker. Its title is a sentence. The Spanish people have a path that leads to a star.

N: It was lost too. But a copy has been made, and it stands outside this museum. Look up at it on your way out, and think of who made the first one.

## The black planes

...

N: Now find a painting here of a sky full of dark aeroplanes, and below them, women running, clutching children. It is called Madrid nineteen thirty-seven, black planes. It hung in the pavilion. Gernika was not alone. Madrid was bombed again and again.

...

N: Look at the mothers in it. Then think back to the mother next door, with the dead child. The same scream, painted twice in one building, that summer.

N: The fair ended. The pavilion came down. Within two years the Republic was gone. What it left behind is in these two rooms. A model, a poster, a few photographs. And one painting that never stopped speaking.

## Walk on

...

N: This is the last stop in the collection. When you are ready, make your way down to the ground floor. Check the map if you need it. Your final story begins on the way out.
