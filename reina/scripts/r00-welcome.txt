# title: Welcome to the Reina Sofía
# stop: 0

## Before we begin

N: Welcome to the Reina Sofía, Monica. Over the next hour, I will walk you through the stories behind some of the boldest paintings of twentieth-century Spain, and bring you to the most famous of them all, Picasso's Guernica.

N: Each stop is one track. When I ask you to look, I will pause. Take as long as you like. The rooms have numbers by their doors, and I will give you each one. If you lose your way, check the map or ask a guard.

N: Sometimes a second voice will join me. That voice tells legends and rumours. Enjoy them. Just know they are stories, not proven history.

A: That is me. I will try to behave.

...

## The house of the dying

N: Now, before any painting, imagine this building with no art in it at all.

N: Picture long wards, row after row of iron beds. The smell of vinegar and candle wax. Nuns moving between the sick. Outside, carts bringing in the poor of Madrid, the ones with nowhere else to go.

N: That is what this was. In the seventeen hundreds, the king decided Madrid needed a vast new hospital. His architect, an Italian named Sabatini, planned a palace for the sick, enormous, around great courtyards.

N: Then the king died. The money stopped. Only a third of the plan was ever built. They opened it anyway. And for nearly two centuries, Madrid came here to be cured, or to die.

## The wrecking ball

...

N: In the nineteen sixties, the hospital closed. Its wards emptied. The great granite shell stood silent in the middle of the city, and the talk was of knocking it down.

N: It came very close. Then, at the last moment, the state declared it a national monument. The building was saved, not for its patients, but for its walls.

N: What do you do with a hospital nobody needs? Someone had a daring idea. Fill it with the most rebellious art of the century.

## Glass, and red

...

N: If you came in from the square, you passed three towers of glass and steel climbing the old stone front. They are lifts, designed with a British architect, Ian Ritchie. The old hospital keeps its stone face, and the crowds ride up past it in glass.

N: Behind it, the museum grew a second body, under a huge red roof by the French architect Jean Nouvel. Old stone in front. Red and glass behind. A hospital that turned into a museum, and then kept growing.

## Night shift

...

N: But a building where so many died does not forget easily. When workers began converting it, they dug into the old ground. Reports from the time say they found human remains.

...

A: And that is where the stories start. They say that in the years after the museum opened, the guards on night duty began to dread certain rooms. Whispers in empty galleries. Screams with no one there. Doors swinging shut on their own. A lift that rose and stopped, rose and stopped, with nobody inside.

A: Some swear that three nuns were found in the old chapel, mummified. And the night guards, so the legend goes, gave their ghost a name. Ataúlfo. Nobody knows who he was. Nobody has ever proved he exists. But ask a guard about him. Watch their face.

...

N: Keep that in mind as you walk. Every gallery you enter today was once a ward. People lay where the paintings now hang.

## Walk on

N: Take a glass lift to the second floor of the Sabatini building. That is stop one.
