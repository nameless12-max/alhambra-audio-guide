# title: Stop 6. Picasso: Guernica
# stop: 6
# room: 205.10

## The empty wall

N: Stop six. You are on the second floor of the Sabatini building, room two hundred and five point ten. If the rooms have changed, check the map or ask a guard. You cannot miss it. It fills a whole wall, in black and white.

N: Do not look yet. Find a place a few steps back, and turn away from it for a moment. Listen first.

N: Picture a painter in Paris in the spring of nineteen thirty-seven. He is the most famous artist alive. His own country, Spain, is tearing itself apart in civil war. The Republic has asked him for one thing. A great painting for its pavilion at the World's Fair. The world will be watching.

N: And Picasso cannot find it. For weeks he draws a painter and a model in a studio. Pretty, quiet, pointless. The canvas waits, nearly eight metres wide. The fair is weeks away. He has nothing to say.

## A Monday in the market

N: Now picture a small town in the Basque hills. It is Monday afternoon, market day. Farmers have come in with cattle and carts. The square is full.

N: Then the planes come. They are German, the Condor Legion, sent by Hitler to help General Franco, with Italian planes beside them. For hours they bomb the town. Fighters chase people along the roads. By night, Gernika is burning.

N: A British reporter walks through the ruins and writes what he saw. His story crosses the world. In Paris, Picasso opens the newspapers and sees the photographs. Black, white and grey. A town that is no longer there.

N: Five days after the bombing, he starts to draw. He does not stop.

## Five weeks in the attic

N: His studio is an attic on the Left Bank, with beams and a long, dark room. The canvas is so tall he climbs a ladder and paints with long-handled brushes.

N: Beside him, with a camera, is Dora Maar. She is a photographer, and she is his lover. Week after week she photographs the canvas. Figures arrive, move, vanish. A raised fist appears in the middle, then disappears. It is like watching someone think.

N: In about five weeks, it is finished. So now, a question to hold. Why did the most celebrated painter alive, with all the colours in the world, choose none of them? Keep that question. We will come back to it.

## The mother

N: Now turn around and face it.

...

N: Look to the far left. A bull stands calm, almost bored, its tail curling like smoke. And under the bull, a woman throws back her head and screams at the sky.

...

N: Look at what she is holding. A baby. Its head has fallen back. Its eyes are empty. Her tongue is a blade. Stay with her a moment.

...

N: Ask yourself whether you have ever seen grief drawn so simply. A few lines. No tears. Just a mouth that will not close.

## The flower in the rubble

N: Now let your eyes fall to the floor of the painting. A soldier lies there in pieces, like a smashed statue. His hand still grips a broken sword.

...

N: Look very closely, just beside that hand. A small flower is growing. It is tiny. You could walk past it for years. Many people see it as the one sign of hope in the whole picture.

## The horse and the light

N: In the centre, the horse. A spear has gone right through it. Its mouth is open in a scream, its tongue another blade. Its body is covered in little marks, like the columns of a newspaper.

...

N: Above it, a jagged eye of light, with an electric bulb inside.

A: Here is a reading you will hear in Madrid, and it is only a reading. In Spanish, a light bulb is a bombilla. A bomb is a bomba. Some say Picasso hid the bombs inside the light. He never said so. He refused to explain his pictures.

...

N: To the right, a woman leans in through a window, pushing an oil lamp into the dark, as if to say, look. Below her, another woman staggers forward. And at the far right, a figure throws up both arms inside a burning house. There is no door.

## Why there is no colour

N: Now step back and see the whole wall at once. Here is the answer to our question.

N: Picasso met this horror the way the world did. In newsprint. In grey photographs. So he painted it like that, a front page as big as a house. And look at what is not here. No planes. No uniforms. No name of a town. That is why it could later belong to every bombed city in the world.

...

N: Stand with it in silence for a moment. Look back at that mother. What would you want the world to see, if this were your town?
