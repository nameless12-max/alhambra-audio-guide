# title: Stop 7. Picasso: Guernica comes home
# stop: 7
# room: 205.10

## The secret passenger

N: Stop seven. You are still on the second floor of the Sabatini building, room two hundred and five point ten, in front of Guernica. If the rooms have changed, check the map or ask a guard.

N: Picture a scheduled flight from New York to Madrid, in September nineteen eighty-one. More than three hundred passengers. Trays, blankets, a long night over the Atlantic. Nobody on board knows what is in the hold. Not the passengers. Not even the crew.

N: The plane lands. And then the pilot speaks. He tells them who has been travelling with them. Guernica has come home.

N: To understand why that moment mattered so much, go back to where we left the painting, in Paris.

## A cold welcome

N: The world's fair opened. And Guernica was no triumph. Some on the painter's own side found it baffling. They had hoped for a poster with a clear message. Instead they got screaming horses and broken statues.

N: So the painting went on the road. It toured Scandinavia and Britain, then crossed to America, raising money for Spanish refugees. It was rolled, unrolled, rolled again. Then the Republic lost the war. General Franco ruled Spain. And a world war began.

N: Guernica could not go home. There was no home to go to.

## A promise to a country

N: It stayed in New York, at the Museum of Modern Art, for more than forty years. Picasso made one condition, and he never moved from it. The painting would go to Spain only when Spain was free again.

N: He did not live to see it. Nor did Franco. Two years after the painter died, the dictator died too. Then came elections, a constitution, and finally, that flight.

## The officer at the door

...

A: Now for the story everyone in Madrid will tell you. It is a legend. During the war, in occupied Paris, a German officer is said to have come to Picasso's studio. He saw a photograph of Guernica, and he asked, did you do this? And Picasso said, no. You did.

A: Nobody has ever found a record of it. But people keep telling it, because it says exactly what they feel when they stand here.

...

## Kill lies all

N: In New York, one morning, a young man walked up to the canvas with a can of red spray paint. His name was Tony Shafrazi. He wrote three words across it. Kill lies all. He was protesting a massacre in Vietnam. He was arrested on the spot.

N: For a moment, it looked as if Guernica was ruined. But the paint had landed on a thick layer of varnish. Restorers wiped it away. Look at the canvas now. There is no trace.

## Behind glass

N: When it reached Madrid, it was hung behind bulletproof glass, with armed guards. Only months before, soldiers had burst into the Spanish parliament, firing into the ceiling, trying to bring back military rule. The country's democracy was very young. And this painting had become its symbol. People queued to see it.

N: A few years after it moved here, the glass came down. Now it is just you and the paint.

## What it is worth

N: What is it worth? It has no price. It will never be sold. For comparison, another Picasso, Women of Algiers, sold at auction in New York for about one hundred and seventy-nine million dollars. Few would call that the more important painting. This one belongs to a country.

N: And it will not travel again. Gernika has asked for it. Basque leaders have asked, more than once. The answer is always no. All those years of rolling and shipping left their mark. The museum says it is too fragile to move.

## A curtain in New York

N: A woven copy of Guernica once hung at the United Nations in New York. One day, before a speech there making the case for war in Iraq, it was hidden behind a blue curtain. The official reason was the camera background. Many people did not believe it.

N: Think about that. Sixty-six years after Gernika, the picture was still too dangerous to stand behind.
