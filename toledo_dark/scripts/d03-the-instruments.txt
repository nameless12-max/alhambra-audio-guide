# title: Toledo's dark side 3. The instruments: what was real, and what was made to frighten
# stop: 3
# room: The torture exhibition, or anywhere

## Three tools, and a rulebook

N: Here is a fact that surprises almost everyone. The Spanish Inquisition used only three methods of torture. Three.

N: The first is the pulley. In Spanish, the garrucha. A rope tied to the wrists, behind the back, and the prisoner lifted from the floor. The second is the water. The toca, a strip of linen in the mouth, and water poured onto it from a jar, a little at a time. The third is the rack. The potro, a frame where cords were tightened around the arms and legs.

N: A wooden frame like a ladder, a pulley with a rope, a jar and a cloth. Those are the ones to trust.

...

N: And they came with a rulebook. Torture only at the end of a long trial, never at the start. A doctor in the room. A clerk writing every word. No blood to be shed. Only once, in theory, though a session could be suspended. And a confession made in pain counted for nothing until the prisoner repeated it, a day later, away from the ropes.

N: In this city, a historian studied over a thousand trials from around the year sixteen hundred. About one prisoner in eight was tortured. The royal courts of the day were often harsher. That is not a comfort. It is a measure of the age.

## The maiden who never was

N: Now the famous pieces. The iron maiden. A tall iron woman, hinged, full of spikes. Did anyone ever die in one?

...

N: Almost certainly not. No record from the Middle Ages mentions it. The first story appears in a German city guidebook at the end of the seventeen hundreds. Showmen then built them for display and sold them to collectors. The most famous was destroyed in the Second World War, long known to be a fake.

N: The pear of anguish, the metal pear that opens with a screw? No sign of one as a torture tool before the eighteen hundreds. The chastity belt? Medieval writers mention it mostly as a joke. Most surviving belts are later curiosities. The Judas cradle, the pointed seat? The evidence is thin and late.

## The ones that were real, elsewhere

N: Some pieces are real, but not the Inquisition's. Masks of shame, iron faces with long ears or a pig's snout, were worn by gossips and cheats in German towns. Boots that crushed the legs were used by courts in Scotland and France.

## Why we wanted to believe

N: So who made all this? Some were forged for money, then gathered by collectors, and from the nineteen eighties sent around Europe in touring shows. Why did they settle so happily in Spain?

A: Because people had been told for centuries that Spain was the land of horrors. Protestant pamphlets, in the age of the wars of religion, painted the Inquisition as a cave of monsters. Then, in the eighteen forties, an American writer set his most famous terror tale in the dungeons of Toledo. A pit, a swinging blade. Pure invention.

N: Spanish historians later gave this a name. The Black Legend. The truth was bad enough. The legend made it worse.

## The one that was all too real

...

N: Now find the garrote, if they have one. A chair, a post, an iron collar with a screw at the back.

N: This one is real. Spain made it the official means of execution in the eighteen thirties. The last man put to death for heresy in Spain was a village schoolteacher near Valencia, garroted in eighteen twenty-six, because he would not teach his pupils the Catholic faith. And the last two garrotings in Spain took place in March, nineteen seventy-four. One in Barcelona, one in Tarragona.

N: Within living memory. Stand with that for a moment.
