# title: Toledo's dark side 2. Zocodover: the theatre of faith
# stop: 2
# room: Plaza de Zocodover, or anywhere

## A wedding present

N: If you can, stand in Zocodover, the big three-sided square at the top of the town. Cafés. Pigeons. A tourist train. Now take all of that away.

...

N: It is February, fifteen sixty. The king of Spain, Philip the Second, has just married a French princess. She is fourteen years old. And Toledo wants to give the royal couple a spectacle worthy of them.

N: For its greatest ceremonies, Toledo built a stage in this square. Picture it now. Wooden stands rise against the houses. Balconies are rented out like boxes at the opera. There is a raised platform for the inquisitors, under a canopy, and another for the accused. The king and his young queen come to watch.

N: What Toledo offers them is an auto-da-fé. An act of faith.

## What the crowd saw

N: The words sound like fire. But here is the surprise. An auto-da-fé was not a burning. It was a ceremony of sentences. A mass, a sermon, and then, one by one, the verdicts read aloud.

N: It begins at dawn with a procession. A green cross, the emblem of the Inquisition, carried through the streets. Then the accused, walking in file, each with a candle.

N: Watch their clothes. That is how the crowd knew everything. Many wear a yellow tunic with a red diagonal cross. That is the sanbenito. It means: I confessed, I was taken back, I am being punished and forgiven. Elvira del Campo wore one.

N: A few wear something darker. A tall pointed hat. A tunic painted with flames and devils. Those are the ones who will not be forgiven.

...

N: The sentences take hours. Most people are given penance. Prayer, fines, years of the yellow tunic, sometimes the lash, sometimes the galleys. A great many walk home that night.

## Outside the walls

N: And the ones in flames? The Church did not execute them itself. It released them, that was the word, into the hands of the royal courts. They were led out of the city, down to the open fields of the Vega, below the walls. There, at a place Toledo called the brasero, the brazier, the sentence was carried out. Those who repented at the end were strangled first.

N: Not in this square. The square was for the theatre. The fire was out of sight.

## The tunic in the church

N: Here is the cruellest part, and the least known. When a penitent's years were done, the yellow tunic was not thrown away. It was hung on the wall of their parish church. With their name on it.

N: Their children saw it every Sunday. Their grandchildren too. When the cloth rotted, the Inquisition had it replaced, with the same name, so that the shame would never fade. Families were refused marriages, offices, honours, for generations, because of a tunic on a wall.

...

N: How many died? Fewer than the legend says, and more than anyone should accept. Modern historians, counting the trial records, find that from fifteen forty onwards, fewer than two in every hundred people tried were executed. The worst killing came in the first fifty years. Across Spain and its whole history, the best estimates are a few thousand.

N: Look around the square. Picture the stands, the canopy, a fourteen-year-old queen watching.

## Walk on

N: Next, the instruments. Which of the things in those glass cases are real, and which were made to frighten you.
