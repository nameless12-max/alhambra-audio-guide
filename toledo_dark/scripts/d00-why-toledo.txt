# title: The day the inquisitors came
# stop: 0
# room: Anywhere

## A room full of silent things

N: Monica, this one is for the room that let you down.

N: You went down the steps. There was a rack. A chair of spikes. Iron collars behind glass. Little cards with a name and a date. And you walked out thinking, is that it?

N: It was not your fault. Objects cannot speak. A rope on a wall is only a rope until you know whose arms it held, and why, and what the people in the room were writing down while it happened.

N: So let me give the objects back their people. Real people, of this city. Some of their words survive, written by a clerk with a quill, in the very hours they spoke them.

...

N: But first, a question to carry with you. Why here? Why did Toledo, of all the cities in Spain, become one of the great homes of the Spanish Inquisition?

## The city of three faiths

N: Picture Toledo a few years before Columbus sails. Church bells, and the call of the synagogue, and in living memory the call to prayer. Christians, Jews and Muslims have shared these streets for centuries. Not always kindly. But they have shared them.

N: And there is a fourth group, the one that matters most to our story. The conversos. Jewish families who became Christian, many of them after terrible riots a century earlier, when converting was the price of staying alive. Their grandchildren are Christians born and baptised. Some are priests. Some are rich. Some sit on the city council.

N: And their neighbours do not trust them. Toledo has already passed one of the first laws in Spain to bar people of Jewish descent from office. The question in every whisper is simple. Do they still keep the old faith in secret, behind closed shutters, on a Friday night?

## Forty days

N: Now the inquisitors arrive. It is the spring of fourteen eighty-five. They come from a smaller town to the south, and in the cathedral one of them preaches a sermon announcing their powers.

N: Then they post an edict on the cathedral door. It is called the edict of grace. Anyone who has strayed from the faith has forty days to come forward and confess. Confess now, and you will be treated with mercy.

A: In the city, they said there was a plot. A group of conversos, it was told, planned to attack the Corpus Christi procession and kill the inquisitors. Some men were hanged for it. Whether the plot was real, historians still argue.

...

N: Imagine being a converso family that spring. Do you confess, even if you have nothing to confess, just to be safe? And if you do, which of your neighbours will you be asked to name?

## The first procession

N: The following February, on a cold Sunday morning, the first great ceremony takes place. About seven hundred and fifty people walk through these streets in procession. Men and women from seven parishes. Bareheaded. Barefoot in the cold. Each carries an unlit candle. Crowds come from the countryside to watch.

N: They are not burned. They are the ones who confessed. Their punishment is shame, fines, and a life marked forever.

N: A chronicler of the day wrote it all down. And this was only the beginning.

...

N: Hold that image. Seven hundred and fifty candles, none of them lit.
