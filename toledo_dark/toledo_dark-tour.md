## Toledo's dark side 0. The day the inquisitors came · Room Anywhere

*558 words, about 4.0 min*


### A room full of silent things

Monica, this one is for the room that let you down.

You went down the steps. There was a rack. A chair of spikes. Iron collars behind glass. Little cards with a name and a date. And you walked out thinking, is that it?

It was not your fault. Objects cannot speak. A rope on a wall is only a rope until you know whose arms it held, and why, and what the people in the room were writing down while it happened.

So let me give the objects back their people. Real people, of this city. Some of their words survive, written by a clerk with a quill, in the very hours they spoke them.

But first, a question to carry with you. Why here? Why did Toledo, of all the cities in Spain, become one of the great homes of the Spanish Inquisition?


### The city of three faiths

Picture Toledo a few years before Columbus sails. Church bells, and the call of the synagogue, and in living memory the call to prayer. Christians, Jews and Muslims have shared these streets for centuries. Not always kindly. But they have shared them.

And there is a fourth group, the one that matters most to our story. The conversos. Jewish families who became Christian, many of them after terrible riots a century earlier, when converting was the price of staying alive. Their grandchildren are Christians born and baptised. Some are priests. Some are rich. Some sit on the city council.

And their neighbours do not trust them. Toledo has already passed one of the first laws in Spain to bar people of Jewish descent from office. The question in every whisper is simple. Do they still keep the old faith in secret, behind closed shutters, on a Friday night?


### Forty days

Now the inquisitors arrive. It is the spring of fourteen eighty-five. They come from a smaller town to the south, and in the cathedral one of them preaches a sermon announcing their powers.

Then they post an edict on the cathedral door. It is called the edict of grace. Anyone who has strayed from the faith has forty days to come forward and confess. Confess now, and you will be treated with mercy.

> *Legend voice:* In the city, they said there was a plot. A group of conversos, it was told, planned to attack the Corpus Christi procession and kill the inquisitors. Some men were hanged for it. Whether the plot was real, historians still argue.

Imagine being a converso family that spring. Do you confess, even if you have nothing to confess, just to be safe? And if you do, which of your neighbours will you be asked to name?


### The first procession

The following February, on a cold Sunday morning, the first great ceremony takes place. About seven hundred and fifty people walk through these streets in procession. Men and women from seven parishes. Bareheaded. Barefoot in the cold. Each carries an unlit candle. Crowds come from the countryside to watch.

They are not burned. They are the ones who confessed. Their punishment is shame, fines, and a life marked forever.

A chronicler of the day wrote it all down. And this was only the beginning.

Hold that image. Seven hundred and fifty candles, none of them lit.

## Toledo's dark side 1. Elvira del Campo: the woman who would not eat pork · Room Anywhere

*544 words, about 3.9 min*


### Two men in the house

A summer day, in a village south of Toledo. A notary's wife is arrested and brought to the city. Her name is Elvira del Campo. She is pregnant. She is a Christian, baptised, a churchgoer. But her family were Jewish, generations ago.

The charge? Two men who lived in her own house have spoken to the Inquisition. They say she does not eat pork. And that she puts on clean linen on Saturdays, the Jewish sabbath.

That is all. A plate pushed away. A fresh shirt on the wrong day.

Now here is the first thing that makes this court so frightening. Elvira is never told who accused her. She is not told exactly what they said, or when. She has to guess.

So she guesses. She lists everyone she thinks might hate her. Twelve names. The judges agree to strike out two. One of them, it turns out, had a grudge against her.

And she fights back. Thirteen witnesses come to speak for her. A good Christian, they say, every one of them but one. Why no pork? Elvira says a doctor told her to avoid it, for an illness she was ashamed of.

Months pass in her cell. Her baby is born there.


### The room

Then the judges vote. The case is not proven. So they order the question, which is what they called torture.

She is taken to the chamber. In the room are an inquisitor, a representative of the bishop, a doctor, and a clerk. The clerk has one job. To write down every word she says.

Because of him, we can still hear her.

She is told to tell the truth. She says she has nothing to tell. The cords are tied to her arms, and tightened, turn by turn. And between each turn, she begs. Not for mercy, at first. For instructions.

> *Legend voice:* Tell me what you want, for I do not know what to say.

Again and again. Tell me what I have to say. She cannot give them what they want, because no one will tell her what it is. That is the rule. The court must never put words in the prisoner's mouth.

That first day, she confesses nothing. Four days later she is brought back to the room. And before the cords are touched, she breaks. Yes, she says. When I was a girl, my mother told me not to eat pork, and to keep the sabbath. The next day, outside the chamber, she repeats it, as the rules demand.


### The sentence

In June of fifteen sixty-eight, she walks in a great public ceremony in Toledo. She is reconciled, as they called it. Taken back into the Church. Her property is confiscated. She must wear a penitent's yellow tunic, and serve three years in prison.

After six months the prison term is lifted. She walks out free, and with nothing. The record says she was reduced to begging.

Her file is still in Spain's national archive in Madrid. Hundreds of pages. A woman who could not read, and whose every cry was written down.

Ask yourself what you would have said. Tell me what I have to say.

## Toledo's dark side 2. Zocodover: the theatre of faith · Room Plaza de Zocodover, or anywhere

*545 words, about 3.9 min*


### A wedding present

If you can, stand in Zocodover, the big three-sided square at the top of the town. Cafés. Pigeons. A tourist train. Now take all of that away.

It is February, fifteen sixty. The king of Spain, Philip the Second, has just married a French princess. She is fourteen years old. And Toledo wants to give the royal couple a spectacle worthy of them.

For its greatest ceremonies, Toledo built a stage in this square. Picture it now. Wooden stands rise against the houses. Balconies are rented out like boxes at the opera. There is a raised platform for the inquisitors, under a canopy, and another for the accused. The king and his young queen come to watch.

What Toledo offers them is an auto-da-fé. An act of faith.


### What the crowd saw

The words sound like fire. But here is the surprise. An auto-da-fé was not a burning. It was a ceremony of sentences. A mass, a sermon, and then, one by one, the verdicts read aloud.

It begins at dawn with a procession. A green cross, the emblem of the Inquisition, carried through the streets. Then the accused, walking in file, each with a candle.

Watch their clothes. That is how the crowd knew everything. Many wear a yellow tunic with a red diagonal cross. That is the sanbenito. It means: I confessed, I was taken back, I am being punished and forgiven. Elvira del Campo wore one.

A few wear something darker. A tall pointed hat. A tunic painted with flames and devils. Those are the ones who will not be forgiven.

The sentences take hours. Most people are given penance. Prayer, fines, years of the yellow tunic, sometimes the lash, sometimes the galleys. A great many walk home that night.


### Outside the walls

And the ones in flames? The Church did not execute them itself. It released them, that was the word, into the hands of the royal courts. They were led out of the city, down to the open fields of the Vega, below the walls. There, at a place Toledo called the brasero, the brazier, the sentence was carried out. Those who repented at the end were strangled first.

Not in this square. The square was for the theatre. The fire was out of sight.


### The tunic in the church

Here is the cruellest part, and the least known. When a penitent's years were done, the yellow tunic was not thrown away. It was hung on the wall of their parish church. With their name on it.

Their children saw it every Sunday. Their grandchildren too. When the cloth rotted, the Inquisition had it replaced, with the same name, so that the shame would never fade. Families were refused marriages, offices, honours, for generations, because of a tunic on a wall.

How many died? Fewer than the legend says, and more than anyone should accept. Modern historians, counting the trial records, find that from fifteen forty onwards, fewer than two in every hundred people tried were executed. The worst killing came in the first fifty years. Across Spain and its whole history, the best estimates are a few thousand.

Look around the square. Picture the stands, the canopy, a fourteen-year-old queen watching.

## Toledo's dark side 3. The instruments: what was real, and what was made to frighten · Room The torture exhibition, or anywhere

*610 words, about 4.4 min*


### Three tools, and a rulebook

Here is a fact that surprises almost everyone. The Spanish Inquisition used only three methods of torture. Three.

The first is the pulley. In Spanish, the garrucha. A rope tied to the wrists, behind the back, and the prisoner lifted from the floor. The second is the water. The toca, a strip of linen in the mouth, and water poured onto it from a jar, a little at a time. The third is the rack. The potro, a frame where cords were tightened around the arms and legs.

A wooden frame like a ladder, a pulley with a rope, a jar and a cloth. Those are the ones to trust.

And they came with a rulebook. Torture only at the end of a long trial, never at the start. A doctor in the room. A clerk writing every word. No blood to be shed. Only once, in theory, though a session could be suspended. And a confession made in pain counted for nothing until the prisoner repeated it, a day later, away from the ropes.

In this city, a historian studied over a thousand trials from around the year sixteen hundred. About one prisoner in eight was tortured. The royal courts of the day were often harsher. That is not a comfort. It is a measure of the age.


### The maiden who never was

Now the famous pieces. The iron maiden. A tall iron woman, hinged, full of spikes. Did anyone ever die in one?

Almost certainly not. No record from the Middle Ages mentions it. The first story appears in a German city guidebook at the end of the seventeen hundreds. Showmen then built them for display and sold them to collectors. The most famous was destroyed in the Second World War, long known to be a fake.

The pear of anguish, the metal pear that opens with a screw? No sign of one as a torture tool before the eighteen hundreds. The chastity belt? Medieval writers mention it mostly as a joke. Most surviving belts are later curiosities. The Judas cradle, the pointed seat? The evidence is thin and late.


### The ones that were real, elsewhere

Some pieces are real, but not the Inquisition's. Masks of shame, iron faces with long ears or a pig's snout, were worn by gossips and cheats in German towns. Boots that crushed the legs were used by courts in Scotland and France.


### Why we wanted to believe

So who made all this? Some were forged for money, then gathered by collectors, and from the nineteen eighties sent around Europe in touring shows. Why did they settle so happily in Spain?

> *Legend voice:* Because people had been told for centuries that Spain was the land of horrors. Protestant pamphlets, in the age of the wars of religion, painted the Inquisition as a cave of monsters. Then, in the eighteen forties, an American writer set his most famous terror tale in the dungeons of Toledo. A pit, a swinging blade. Pure invention.

Spanish historians later gave this a name. The Black Legend. The truth was bad enough. The legend made it worse.


### The one that was all too real

Now find the garrote, if they have one. A chair, a post, an iron collar with a screw at the back.

This one is real. Spain made it the official means of execution in the eighteen thirties. The last man put to death for heresy in Spain was a village schoolteacher near Valencia, garroted in eighteen twenty-six, because he would not teach his pupils the Catholic faith. And the last two garrotings in Spain took place in March, nineteen seventy-four. One in Barcelona, one in Tarragona.

Within living memory. Stand with that for a moment.

## Toledo's dark side 4. The cells: the brotherhood's prison, and what remains · Room Posada de la Hermandad, Calle de la Hermandad, near the cathedral, or anywhere

*587 words, about 4.2 min*


### The men with green sleeves

Near the cathedral, in a narrow street, there is an old stone doorway. Look up at it. Above the door, the arms of the Catholic Monarchs. And on either side, two small armed guards with crossbows.

This was the house of the Holy Brotherhood. Not the Inquisition. Something older, and in its way more feared on a lonely road.

Long before the inquisitors came, the hills south of Toledo were wild country. Beekeepers, hunters and shepherds lived out there, and so did bandits. So the country people banded together into an armed brotherhood, to hunt the robbers down themselves. Later the Catholic Monarchs took the idea across all of Castile. A rural police, with its own judges and its own jails.

> *Legend voice:* People mocked them, too. Their uniform had green sleeves. And when help came too late, Spaniards would say: at a fine hour, green sleeves. They still say it today.

In Don Quixote, after the knight frees a chain of convicts, his squire Sancho is terrified of one thing. Not the convicts. The Holy Brotherhood, coming after them.


### Down the steps

If the lower floor is open, go down. The cells are underground, vaulted rooms with iron bars, gathered around a shaft that drops daylight from the street above. Each room, it is said, could hold up to forty prisoners.

Stand by the bars and look up at that light. That pale square is all a prisoner had of the day. It is the most honest thing in the building. No label needed.


### The secret prisons

And the Inquisition's own cells? They were called the secret prisons. In Toledo, for more than two centuries, the tribunal had its house beside the church of San Vicente, in the upper town. Nothing of them is open to visit today.

Here is a twist the legend never tells. The Inquisition's cells were often cleaner and better fed than the king's jails. Historians have found prisoners in the king's jails who blasphemed on purpose, just to be moved into them.

But they were secret in a way no ordinary jail was. You did not know why you were there. You could not see your family. You might wait months or years, as Elvira del Campo waited, guessing at your accusers in the dark.

> *Legend voice:* In Toledo, people say tunnels ran from the Inquisition's house under the streets, to the cathedral and beyond. Guides will point to cellars and swear by them. No one has ever shown such a tunnel to be real. But the city loves the story.


### What remains

The tribunal of Toledo sat for the last time in eighteen twenty, and the Inquisition was abolished for good in eighteen thirty-four. Its papers went to Madrid. Thousands of trials. Names, recipes, prayers, the gossip of kitchens, the cries in the chamber, all written down.

That is the strange gift of this cruel court. It kept such careful records that the people it tried to erase are still here. We know what Elvira del Campo ate, and what she wore on a Saturday, and what she said when her arms hurt.

So if you walk past a glass case again, Monica, look for the plain things. A rope. A jar. A yellow tunic. And put a person beside each one.

Not monsters, on either side. A city that let fear of its neighbours become law. That is the darkest thing in Toledo, and it was never kept in a dungeon.


### Walk on

Walk up into the light. Toledo is still the city of three faiths, in its stones. Thank you for listening.
