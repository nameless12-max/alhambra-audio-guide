# title: Toledo in five minutes: the city on the rock
# stop: 0
# room: Anywhere. Listen first.

## The rock

N: Welcome to Toledo, Monica. Before any church or painting, here is the story of the city itself. It takes five minutes, and everything else today will make more sense.

N: Start with the ground under your feet. Toledo sits on a hill of granite, and the river Tagus wraps around it on three sides, in a deep gorge. Only one side is open. Whoever held this rock was very hard to remove. So everyone wanted it.

## Four owners

N: The Romans took it first, about two hundred years before Christ. One of their historians called it a small city, but strong because of its position. They built baths, a circus for chariot races, and the water tanks you can still visit underground.

N: When Rome collapsed, a people from the north moved in. The Visigoths. They made Toledo the capital of all Spain. Their kings were crowned here, and their bishops met here to decide what the country would believe. Remember them. Their last king, Rodrigo, is waiting for you in tonight's legends.

...

N: Then, in seven hundred and eleven, an army crossed from Africa. Within a few years, Toledo was a Muslim city, and it stayed one for nearly four hundred years. The narrow twisting lanes you keep getting lost in are their street plan. The little mosque by the city gate is theirs.

N: You have just come from Granada, the last Muslim kingdom in Spain. Granada fell in fourteen ninety-two. Toledo was taken by a Christian king four centuries earlier, in ten eighty-five. It was the first great Muslim city to fall. And that is the reason it matters so much.

## The city that translated the world

N: Because the new Christian king did something unusual. He did not empty the city. Muslims stayed. Jews stayed. And so did their libraries.

N: Those libraries held books Europe had lost. Greek medicine, astronomy, mathematics, Aristotle. All of it kept alive in Arabic. So scholars came to Toledo from England, Italy and Germany, and sat down with Jewish and Muslim translators. A book would go from Arabic into Spanish, spoken aloud, and from Spanish into Latin, written down.

...

N: Think about that, with your own story. Knowledge passing from one language to the next, across a table, between people who prayed differently. Much of what Europe later called its Renaissance came through rooms in this town.

N: It was not paradise. There were taxes, quarrels, and massacres. But for a few centuries, three faiths shared this small rock, and built in each other's styles. You will see churches that look like mosques, built by Muslim craftsmen for Christian priests.

## The end, and the freeze

N: It ended badly. In fourteen ninety-two, the Jews of Spain were told to convert or leave. Ten years later, the Muslims were told the same. The city of three faiths became a city of one.

N: For a while Toledo was still the heart of an empire. The emperor Charles the Fifth, the one you saw on horseback in the Prado, held court here. Then his son moved the capital to a small town in the middle of nowhere, called Madrid.

...

N: The court left. The money left. And Toledo simply stopped. Nobody could afford to knock it down and build it again. That is why you are walking through a medieval city today. It was preserved by being forgotten.

N: One man arrived a few years after the court had gone. A painter from Greece, hoping to work for the king. The king tried him once, and did not like the result. So he stayed here for the rest of his life, and painted this city's priests, its noblemen and its stormy sky. You know him already. El Greco.

## Walk on

N: That is Toledo. Romans, Goths, Muslims, Christians and Jews, all on one rock, and then four centuries of sleep. Now choose your next track. The cathedral, Santo Tomé, or whatever is in front of you.
