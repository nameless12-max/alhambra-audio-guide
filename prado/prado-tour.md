## Welcome to the Prado

*515 words, about 3.7 min*


### The stable that became a museum

Welcome to the Prado, Monica. After the Alhambra, here is another palace with a story behind every wall.

Picture these halls with no paintings in them. Straw on the floor. The smell of horses. Napoleon's cavalry have moved in, and the museum that does not exist yet is their stable. Down in the vaults, soldiers are stacking gunpowder.

The building was never meant for art. A Spanish king ordered it for science, a palace of rocks and plants and stuffed animals, beside the botanical garden next door. Then the king died, the French came, and his dream was left a roofless shell.


### How we will walk

Before the story goes on, how this works. There are about fifteen stops. We begin on the ground floor and then go upstairs. Each track opens with the floor and the room number. Find the room by the signs and the museum map, then press play. Take your time between stops.

You will hear two voices. I tell you what happened. The second voice tells you the legends, and says when that is what they are.


### A queen's idea

So who rescued the ruin? The answer is a young queen. María Isabel de Braganza, wife of King Ferdinand, is remembered by the museum as the one who pushed for the royal paintings to come here, for anyone to see. She was a Portuguese princess, born near Lisbon, the city you have just left.

For centuries those pictures had hung in palaces, for kings and their guests. Titian, Rubens, Velázquez, painted for Spanish monarchs, owned by Spanish monarchs. Now the doors would open to the public.

She never saw it. She died less than a year before the opening. The Prado opened on the nineteenth of November, eighteen nineteen. Tomorrow is its birthday.


### The night the Prado burned

Now jump forward. One November morning, Madrid opens its newspaper and reads that the Prado has burned in the night. The flames. The crowds in the street. A national catastrophe.

The city is in shock. And then, the next day, the writer confesses. The journalist Mariano de Cavia had made it all up. He invented the fire, he said, to prevent one. That summer there had been two small real fires, and nobody had acted. His lie went round Europe. This time, the government moved.


### The paintings that ran away

The real danger came with the Civil War. Bombs fell on Madrid. And the Republic did something astonishing. It loaded its greatest paintings onto lorries and drove them through the war to Valencia. Many were hidden inside the stone towers of an old city gate.

The new director of the Prado, named in those months, was Pablo Picasso. He was living in France. He never once sat at his desk.

As the war was being lost, the pictures moved again, through Catalonia and over the border to Geneva. They were safe. They came home just as another war broke out across Europe.

Some of the paintings you will meet today rode in those lorries. Look at them and remember that people risked their lives so you could stand here.

Walk on now to stop one, on the ground floor.


## Stop 1. Fra Angelico: The Annunciation · Room 56B

*582 words, about 4.2 min*


### A friar at his altar

Stop one. Ground floor, room fifty-six B. If it has moved, check the map. Look for a square altarpiece blazing with gold, an angel in pink kneeling before a woman in blue.

Imagine a hilltop convent above Florence. A friar kneels at a side altar before dawn. The man who painted this picture was one of those friars. He painted it for his own brothers, to pray in front of every day of their lives. He was not painting for a museum. He was painting for men who would look at it for the rest of their lives.

If you have been to Florence, you may have climbed the stairs in the convent of San Marco. The same friar painted the walls there, and at the top of those stairs another angel greets another Mary.


### The moment before

Now look at the two figures. The angel has just landed. His wings are still up. He bends forward, arms crossed on his chest. And Mary bends towards him, with her arms crossed the same way.

Stay with her face. Nothing has happened yet. In a moment she will say yes, and in the story the friars believed, the whole world turns on that word. But the painter stops just before it. She is listening.

Now find the line of gold light. It falls from the top left corner, straight across the picture. Ride down it with your eye. A tiny white dove is sliding along it, towards her.


### Who is running away

But there is someone else in this picture, and they are not happy. Look to the far left. Two small figures are being driven out of the garden by an angel.

Adam and Eve. Why put them here, at the edge of this quiet room? Because for the friars, this was one story. On the left, the first people lose paradise. Under the arches, a young woman says yes, and it begins to be won back. The fall and the rescue, side by side, read from left to right.


### Sold for a bell tower

This picture should still be on that hill. But early in the sixteen hundreds, the convent needed money. The story is that the friars sold it to build a new bell tower. One account says it was a gift instead. Either way, it left Italy for the court of Spain, as a present for the most powerful man in the kingdom after the king.

It spent the next centuries in a closed convent of nuns in Madrid, seen only by them. When the museum finally brought it here, it was the first time ordinary people could stand where you are now.

Even here, it had grown dim. Centuries of old varnish had turned the blue grey and the gold dull. For the museum's two hundredth birthday, restorers cleaned it, slowly, by hand. The blue of her cloak came back. The pink of the angel's robe came back. The gold began to burn again. It became the heart of a great exhibition on the friar who painted it.


### What it is worth

How much is it worth? It will never be sold. But in twenty sixteen the Prado bought a much smaller Fra Angelico, a little Virgin with a pomegranate, from a Spanish duke. The price was eighteen million euros. This altarpiece is far larger, and far more famous.

Before you go, look once more at the gap between the angel and the woman. A hand's breadth of air. What would you have said?

Walk on now to stop two, the other Mona Lisa.


## Stop 2. Leonardo's Workshop: The Prado Mona Lisa · Room check the map

*573 words, about 4.1 min*


### The woman in the dark

Stop two. Ground floor, among the Italian Renaissance paintings. Sources give room forty-nine or fifty-six B, so check the map. Look for a small portrait of a woman with folded hands, before a blue landscape of rivers and rocks.

For perhaps two centuries, she sat in darkness. Her whole background was painted solid black. Visitors to the Prado walked straight past her. Just another old copy of a famous face. Nobody knew who made it. Some thought a Flemish painter, far from Italy.

Hold that black in your mind. Because the question is, what was underneath it?


### The wrong wood

A few years before the answer came, the Louvre in Paris asked to borrow her for a Leonardo exhibition. So the Prado's experts looked at her properly, for the first time.

The first surprise was the wood. Not oak, the wood of Flemish painters. Walnut, a wood Leonardo often used himself.

Then they looked beneath the paint, with infrared light. Under the face, the painter's drawing had been corrected. He had changed his mind as he worked. And every change followed the changes Leonardo made to his own Mona Lisa.

Think about what that means. A copyist working from a finished picture has nothing to correct. This painter was correcting along with the master. He was sitting in the same room, at the same time, painting the same woman.


### Under the black

Then the restorers began to take the black away. Slowly. And out of the dark came a world. Now find it. Look behind her shoulders. Winding roads. A bridge. Water. Blue mountains melting into haze. The same dream country that stands behind the woman in Paris.

Now look at her face. The original sits under old yellow varnish, and it has never been fully cleaned. So she looks brown and ancient. Here, the skin is fresh. The sleeves are a warm red. Many people say she looks younger, like someone you might meet. This is the closest you can come to seeing what Leonardo's studio saw.


### The twin goes to Paris

When the museum announced the discovery, papers around the world called her the Mona Lisa's twin. Before she left for Paris, so many people crowded in to see her that her room was too small to hold them. The woman nobody had looked at for centuries had a queue.

In Paris she was under one roof with her sister, the most famous painting in the world. When the original went to New York in nineteen sixty-two, it was valued for insurance at a hundred million dollars. No painting has had a higher insurance valuation. That was the sister. This one spent those years hidden in black.


### The boy who stole

So who painted her? Perhaps one of two pupils. One was a young nobleman. The other was Salaì, a boy who came to live with Leonardo at ten years old. In his notes, Leonardo called him a thief, a liar, obstinate, a glutton. He kept him for decades, all the same.

> *Legend voice:* And here is the rumour you will hear. That Salaì was more than a pupil. That this portrait, painted at his master's side, was a kind of love letter. It is a romantic guess. Nothing proves whose hand this was.

Stand back. Two women, painted side by side, in one workshop. One became the most famous face on earth. The other was buried in black, and waited. Which one is looking at you?

Walk on now to stop three, the Descent from the Cross.


## Stop 3. Rogier van der Weyden: The Descent from the Cross · Room 58

*560 words, about 4.0 min*


### The archers' chapel

Stop three. Ground floor, room fifty-eight. If it has moved, check the map. Look for a wide panel on a flat gold ground, with ten figures, almost life size, lowering a pale dead body from the cross.

Imagine a small chapel outside the walls of a Flemish town. It belongs to a guild of crossbowmen, the citizens who drill with bows to defend their city. They have saved, and they want the best altarpiece money can buy. They go to the painter Rogier van der Weyden.

And they want the world to know it was theirs. So where did he hide their name?

Look into the top corners, in the gold carving. Find the tiny crossbows. There they are.

Now look again at the body of Christ. See how it bends, in one long curve, like a drawn bow. And below him, his mother has fainted. Her body makes the same curve. The son dies. The mother collapses into his shape.


### Grief you can count

Now step close, as close as the guard allows. Look at the faces. Find the tears.

They are clear and round. They run down the cheeks and catch the light, one by one. You could count them. Look at the faces around the mother. Look at the man holding the feet. Every person in this box is breaking in a different way.

Nearly six hundred years later, people still stop dead here. A great historian of art wrote that a single painted tear held everything the Italians envied in these Flemish painters.

Now look down, near the foot of the cross. Find the skull. It is Adam, the first man, buried, so the story went, right where the cross would stand. The first death, and this one.


### The queen's bargain

About a hundred years after it was painted, a very powerful woman came to see it. She was the emperor's sister, and she ruled these lands for him. She wanted it.

So the archers made a deal. They gave up their masterpiece. In return, the queen gave them a painted copy and a new organ for their chapel. A copy and an organ, for this. That was the price.


### The ship that sank

> *Legend voice:* Now a legend, and a good one. They say the panel was sent by sea to Spain, and the ship went down. All seemed lost. But the crate was packed so well that it floated, and the painting came ashore almost unharmed. A Flemish writer told it a few generations later. It may be only a story.


### The king who kept it

When the queen died, her nephew inherited it. He was the most powerful king in Europe, King Philip of Spain. He hung it in his chapel, and later in his great monastery palace in the mountains, El Escorial. It stayed there for more than three and a half centuries.

Then came the Civil War. It went with the Prado's treasures, in the lorries to Valencia, and on to Geneva. When it came home in nineteen thirty-nine, it came here, to the museum. It will never be sold. A queen paid for it with an organ. Today no amount of money could buy it.

Stand still for a moment. No sky. No landscape. Only gold, and these people, and their grief. Have you ever stood beside someone like the mother here?

Walk on now to stop four, Hieronymus Bosch's Garden of Earthly Delights, in room fifty-six A.


## Stop 4. Bosch: The Garden of Earthly Delights · Room 56A

*968 words, about 6.9 min*


### A king buys a garden of sin

Stop four. Ground floor, room fifty-six A. Look for the crowd, and above it a vast three-part painting swarming with tiny pale naked bodies.

Picture the most serious king in Europe. Philip the Second of Spain. He dresses in black. He rules half the world from a cell-like room inside a monastery he has built in the mountains.

And yet this man paid good money for a painting of hundreds of naked lovers, and hung it in his monastery. Why? Hold that question. We will come back to it.


### The doors open

If the back of the panels is showing, look there first. It is all grey. A glass globe, the world on the third day of creation. No people yet. In the top corner, very small, God, with a book. The Latin line above him says, he spoke, and it was done.

Now let the doors swing open in your mind. Colour floods in. And so do we.


### Paradise has a watcher

The left panel is Eden. God, young and gentle, holds Eve by the wrist and presents her to Adam, who has just woken up. It is the first wedding. Everything is calm.

Now find the tall pink fountain behind them. Look into the round hole at its base. Something is looking back. An owl.

In Bosch's world, the owl was a creature of the night. It could stand for wisdom. It could also stand for the soul that prefers darkness. Even in paradise, something is waiting.


### The party

Now the centre. Hundreds of naked people. Giant strawberries, cherries, blackberries, bigger than their heads. Lovers inside a glass bubble. Lovers hiding in a mussel shell. A ring of riders circles a pool of bathing women, on horses, camels, boars.

Nobody is old. Nobody is working. Nobody is ashamed. It is the most joyful thing in this museum, and it makes people uneasy. Stand here a moment. Is this a dream of freedom, or a trap?

Find the man in the lower left with his arms around an owl bigger than he is. The watcher has come into the garden.

> *Legend voice:* There are wild theories about this panel. The boldest says that Bosch worked for a secret sect who practised free love, and that this is their heaven. It is a wonderful story. There is no evidence for it at all.


### The bill arrives

Now the right panel. The light goes out. Cities burn on the horizon.

Find the musical instruments. A man is stretched across the strings of a giant harp. Another is crushed beneath a huge lute. Now look at the backside of that crushed man. Bosch has painted a line of music on it.

In two thousand fourteen, an American student transcribed those notes and played them online. Five hundred years after Bosch, a song from hell went round the world.

Now the tree man, in the centre of hell. His body is a cracked eggshell. His legs are dead trees standing in boats. Inside his hollow body, people drink at a tavern table. And his pale face turns over his shoulder, and looks straight at you. Of all the thousands of figures here, he is the one who sees you.

> *Legend voice:* People have long whispered that the tree man is Bosch himself, looking out from his own hell. It is legend. No certain portrait of Bosch survives. But stand in front of that face and the idea is hard to shake.

Last, the lower right corner. A blue monster with the head of a bird sits on a tall chair, a cooking pot for a crown. The prince of hell. He swallows sinners whole and passes them out below, into a pit.


### Stolen from a rebel

How did this reach Madrid? Through a war. The painting first hung in a palace in Brussels, owned by a great noble family. It passed to William of Orange, the prince who would lead the Dutch revolt against Spain.

In fifteen sixty-eight, Philip's iron general, the Duke of Alba, marched in to crush that revolt. He seized the rebel's property. The Garden was taken from Spain's greatest enemy, and years later the king bought it for himself.

You have just come from Amsterdam, and from the country that revolt would create. This painting was taken from the man who led it.


### The monk's answer

So, back to the question. Why would the gloomiest king in Europe want this?

At his monastery, some of the monks thought the painting was heresy. One of them, the librarian José de Sigüenza, wrote a defence. He said it was a sermon. The centre panel is the brief sweetness of pleasure, like a strawberry, gone almost before you taste it. Then comes the bill. In the monastery's records it is simply the painting of the strawberry tree.

Seen that way, this is not a party at all. It is a warning, painted for a man who wanted to be warned every day.

It will never be sold. No price has ever been put on it, because there is nothing else like it in the world.


### Two more warnings

In this room, look for two more works linked to Bosch. The Haywain shows a cart of hay rolling to hell, while pope, king and peasant all fight to grab a handful.

The other is a painted tabletop, The Table of the Seven Deadly Sins. In its centre is a great eye, with Christ in the pupil, and a Latin warning. Beware, beware, God sees. The king kept it in his own rooms.

And if you saw the great Bosch triptych in Lisbon, The Temptation of Saint Anthony, you have already met his demons. Here is its great rival.

Before you go, find the owl in the fountain one more time. It was watching the first couple. It is watching you.

Walk on to room fifty-five A, Bruegel's Triumph of Death. Check the map. That is stop five.


## Stop 5. Bruegel: The Triumph of Death · Room 55A

*587 words, about 4.2 min*


### The day nobody is spared

Stop five. Ground floor, room fifty-five A. Look for a wide brown panel under a burning sky, its whole landscape crawling with skeletons.

Imagine a city where the plague comes back every few years. Where the bell tolls, and the cart comes round, and you count who is missing from church. That was the world of Pieter Bruegel. Around fifteen sixty-two, he painted what everyone there feared. Not one death. Everyone's death, on the same afternoon.

So here is the question to carry with you. In this whole crowded picture, is there anyone who escapes?


### The army

Start on the right. An army of skeletons marches in ranks, behind shields made from coffin lids. On the left, a skeleton on a starved horse swings a scythe, driving the living before it. Behind him, more skeletons ring a bell.

Now follow where the living are being driven. In the middle right stands a huge box, shaped like a coffin, painted with crosses. Its door is open. People are crowding into it, pushed from behind. It is a trap, and they are running into it.


### The rich and the holy

Now the lower left corner. A king lies dying in red, still in his crown. Beside him, barrels of gold. A skeleton is already helping itself to his money. Another holds up an hourglass in front of his face. The sand has run out.

All his life he could buy anything. He cannot buy one more minute.

Just above him, a skeleton in a cardinal's red hat is steering a real cardinal to his end. The church cannot save its own princes.


### The dog and the child

Now slow down. Look at the ground in the lower left, near the cart. A woman lies dead, a baby in her arms. A thin dog has come to sniff at the child.

Nobody is fighting here. Nobody is screaming. That small, quiet horror is easy to miss, and hard to forget.


### The lovers

Now cross to the lower right corner. This is the answer to the question.

Two lovers sit apart from the slaughter. He plays a lute. She follows the music in a book. Their heads are close. They have noticed nothing at all. They are the only people in the painting who look happy.

Now look just behind them. A skeleton is playing along on its own instrument. Keeping time.

So does anyone escape? No. But Bruegel gives two people the one mercy left. They have not seen it coming yet.

Stand with them a moment. If the music stopped now, would you want to know?


### A Bruegel in the family

The Prado has a second Bruegel story, and a happier one. For centuries, a noble Spanish family owned a big, worn canvas of a village feast. Peasants crowd around a giant barrel, holding up jugs and bowls, scrambling for the new wine on Saint Martin's Day.

The family brought it to the Prado to be studied and restored, with an option for the museum to buy. Experts studied it for months. Then, under examination, fragments of a signature appeared. Bruegel.

In two thousand ten, the Spanish government confirmed the purchase for seven million euros, the figure at which it had been valued. It is painted in glue on linen, so fragile that almost nothing like it survives. If The Wine of Saint Martin's Day is on display today, check the map. It may hang near this room.

The Triumph of Death has no price at all. It will never be sold.

Walk on to room fifty-five B, for Albrecht Dürer. Check the map. That is stop six.


## Stop 6. Dürer: Self-portrait, and Adam and Eve · Room 55B

*626 words, about 4.5 min*


### The young man who wanted more

Stop six. Ground floor, room fifty-five B. Look for a small portrait of a young man with long golden curls, in black and white, a window behind him.

Picture a young man coming home over the Alps. He has been in Italy, and he has seen something he cannot forget. There, painters are treated like gentlemen. They talk with princes and scholars. Back home in Nuremberg, he is a craftsman. He belongs to a guild, like the man who mends your shoes.

His name is Albrecht Dürer. He is twenty-six. And he decides to paint the man he wants to be.


### Dressed for the part

Look at what he chose to wear. A striped cap with a tassel. A fine white jacket edged in black. A cloak thrown over one shoulder. And on his hands, soft leather gloves. Gloves said, these hands do not labour. It is a costume, and a claim.

Now look at his eyes. His body turns away, but his eyes come round to meet yours, cool and sure. Stand here a moment. Who is judging whom?

Below the window, find a line of writing in his own German. It says, I painted this from my own figure. I was twenty-six years old. And then his mark, a large A with a small D sheltering under it.


### A gift for a doomed king

Now follow the picture's journey, because it moves from king to king. More than a century later, his own city sent it to England, as a gift for Charles the First, a king who loved paintings more than almost anything.

Then the English turned on their king. Charles was put on trial, and beheaded. His enormous collection was sold off, piece by piece. Agents from across Europe came shopping. The King of Spain bought well, and Dürer's young gentleman came to Madrid.

The man who wanted to be seen among princes got his wish. He just had to wait for one to lose his head.


### The nudes that nearly burned

Now, if they are hanging nearby, find two tall narrow panels. A naked man and a naked woman, life-size, against black. Adam and Eve, painted in fifteen oh seven. If you cannot see them, check the map.

Dürer spent years measuring the human body, hunting for perfect proportion. Here he lets it go. Eve takes the apple from the serpent. Adam holds a branch of the tree. Look at their feet. They almost dance.

Their journey is a story of war. They hung in the castle in Prague, until a Swedish army sacked it and carried them north as loot. Years later, a queen of Sweden gave them away, to the King of Spain.

And in Madrid they became an embarrassment. Two naked bodies, in the most Catholic court in Europe. They were hidden in rooms kept for nudes, behind closed doors.

Then came a pious new king, Charles the Third. He looked at the royal nudes and decided they were indecent. They should be destroyed.

His court painter, Anton Raphael Mengs, persuaded him otherwise. These were works of genius, he argued. The king gave way. But Adam and Eve were locked away, out of sight, for decades. Even after they reached this museum, they waited years before the public was allowed to see them.

> *Legend voice:* You will hear that the bonfire was all but built, and the panels came within days of the flames. That is legend, polished by retelling. The order to destroy them is real. The bonfire may not be.

Look at them once more. Shame is what they were about to learn, in the story. For a century, it was what everyone made them carry.


### Walk on

That ends the ground floor. Now go up to the first floor, following the signs and the museum map. El Greco is next. That is stop seven.


## Stop 7. El Greco: The Nobleman with his Hand on his Chest · Room 8B

*674 words, about 4.8 min*


### A stranger is watching you

Stop seven. First floor, room eight B, in the El Greco rooms. Look for a small portrait of a man in black, a white ruff at his throat, his right hand pressed flat on his chest.

Stand in front of him. Wait a moment. Notice that he does not look at you. He looks just past you, as if someone has asked him a question and he is deciding whether to tell the truth.

Now look at the hand. The two middle fingers are pressed together. The outer ones spread. It is the gesture of a man swearing an oath. I give you my word.

Hold on to that question. What is he swearing to? We will come back to him.


### The painter who bet everything on a king

Picture a man in his late thirties, stepping off the road into Spain. He is Greek, from the island of Crete. He has painted in Venice and in Rome, and he has not been made famous by either. He comes to Spain for one reason. The king.

Philip the Second is building El Escorial, a monastery and palace in the hills outside Madrid. It is the biggest commission in Europe. Every painter wants a wall there. The Greek wants an altar.

He gets his chance. The king orders a great altarpiece, the martyrdom of a Roman saint and his soldiers. The Greek pours everything into it. Strange acid colours. Long bodies. Saints who seem to float.

In fifteen eighty-two, he delivers it. The king looks. And the king does not like it.

The picture is not hung on the altar it was made for. Another painter is hired to do it again, plainly. The Greek is never asked back to court. His one great bet has failed.


### The city that took him in

So he goes to Toledo, an old city on a rock above a river, full of priests and scholars and money. And there, the rejected foreigner becomes somebody. They call him El Greco, the Greek.

He is proud. He lives in style, beyond his means. And when the churches will not pay his price, he takes them to court. Again and again. He never lets anyone tell him what his work is worth.

Now turn back to the man in black.

This is one of the people of Toledo who did take him seriously. A nobleman, around the time the king turned the painter away. Look at the face again. Thin. Grey. Serious. Look at the gold hilt of the sword, just showing at his side. A gentleman's oath, sworn on his honour.

> *Legend voice:* Who is he? Nobody knows. The legend most often told is that he is a nobleman named Juan de Silva, who held an office in the city. Others have other names. The painting keeps its secret.


### Three hundred years of silence

El Greco dies in Toledo in sixteen fourteen. Almost at once, the world forgets him. For two centuries his saints are dismissed as the work of a madman. His pictures hang in dark chapels and nobody comes.

> *Legend voice:* And people needed a reason for the strangeness. So the story grew that his eyes were faulty. In the early nineteen hundreds, doctors claimed he had astigmatism, that he painted long bodies because he saw them long. It is a myth. A painter who saw everything stretched would see his canvas stretched too. And this man, look at him, is not stretched at all.

Then, at the end of the eighteen hundreds, young painters begin to walk into these rooms and stop dead. A young Picasso studies him. The madman becomes a prophet of modern art.


### The oath

If you have time, find his last great altarpiece in these rooms, the Adoration of the Shepherds. Check the map. He painted it to hang over his own grave.

Now one last look at the nobleman. Four hundred years, and his hand is still on his heart. The king did not believe in this painter. This man did. Stand with him for a moment. Do you believe him?


### Walk on

When you are ready, follow the signs for Titian, to room twenty-seven. That is stop eight.


## Stop 8. Titian: Charles the Fifth at Mühlberg · Room 27

*722 words, about 5.2 min*


### An emperor who cannot walk

Stop eight. First floor, room twenty-seven, in the central gallery. Look for a huge canvas of a lone rider in armour on a dark horse, a long lance in his hand, and a red and gold sky behind him.

Picture a river in Germany, on a spring morning. Mist on the water. On the far bank, the army of the Protestant princes who have defied him. On this side, the most powerful man in the world.

And he is in agony. The emperor, Charles the Fifth, has gout. His joints are swollen. In the days before, he has had to be carried in a litter. He is in his late forties, and his body is giving out.

On the morning of the battle, he climbs onto his horse anyway.

His soldiers cross the river. The enemy breaks. Their leader is taken prisoner. It is the greatest victory of Charles's life. The year is fifteen forty-seven.

You may have met this man before. He is the emperor who ordered the great palace with the round courtyard inside the Alhambra. If you stood in that circle of columns in Granada, you stood in his dream of power.


### What the painter left out

Now look at the picture. Look for the battle.

There is none. No bodies. No river. No enemy. No pain. Only one man, riding calmly out of a dark wood, as evening comes on.

The painter, Titian, has taken a sick, exhausted man and made him into an idea. A Christian knight, alone, steady, unbeatable. Look at the face. Pale, the jaw set hard. Is that calm, or is it a man holding himself together?

Now look at the armour. Every plate, every gold edge. That is not invention. It is the very suit Charles wore that day. It still exists. It is kept in the royal armoury of Madrid, a short walk from this room.


### The night the palace burned

For almost two hundred years this picture hung in the old royal palace of Madrid. Then, on Christmas Eve, seventeen thirty-four, the palace caught fire.

Imagine the servants running through smoke, cutting canvases from their frames, throwing them from windows. Hundreds of pictures burned that night.

This one was carried out. Scorched, damaged, but alive. Look at the lower part of the picture, around the horse's legs and the ground. Some of what you see there is the patient work of the restorers who saved it.

No price has ever been put on it. It belongs to the Spanish state, and it will never be sold. For many, it is the picture that taught Spain how to paint a king.


### The brush on the floor

Charles loved this painter as he loved no other. He made him a count, an extraordinary honour for a painter. And when Charles gave up his crowns and went to die in a monastery, he took Titian's pictures with him.

> *Legend voice:* There is a story everyone tells. One day in the studio, Titian drops his brush. Before anyone can move, the emperor bends down, picks it up, and hands it back. The court is shocked. Charles says only that Titian deserves to be served by an emperor. It is a legend, written down long after both men were dead. But people have loved it for four hundred years.


### The son's secret pictures

The emperor's son, Philip the Second, wanted something very different from Titian. Not power. Desire.

In room forty-four, check the map, are two of the paintings Titian sent him. In one, the goddess Venus clings to her young lover, begging him not to go hunting. He pulls away. He will be killed. In the other, Danaë lies back as a god comes to her as a shower of gold.

Titian wrote to the prince that the two were a pair. One woman seen from the front. One from the back.

The series did not stop here. Another of the paintings Titian made for the same king, the Rape of Europa, crossed the Atlantic. It hangs in the Gardner Museum in Boston. You may have walked past it there.

So here is the father, painted as a knight who feels no pain. And there, nearby, the son's pictures, all longing and loss. Stand with the emperor a moment longer. Which of them is the truer portrait of a man?


### Walk on

When you are ready, follow the signs for Velázquez and Las Meninas. That is stop nine.


## Stop 9. Velázquez: Las Meninas · Room 12

*946 words, about 6.8 min*


### Caught in the act

Stop nine. First floor, room twelve, the great Velázquez hall. Look for the tallest picture in the room, and the crowd: a little girl in pale silver, and a painter with his brush raised.

Picture a morning in the old royal palace of Madrid. A dim, high room. The king's painter is at work. And the household has drifted in to watch, the way children and servants do.

Imagine it. The little princess is five. A maid kneels and offers her water in a small red clay cup. Another maid bends in a curtsey. A dog sleeps on the floor.

Then something happens. Look at their faces. The princess turns her eyes. The painter stops and stares. The woman on the right looks straight out. Someone important has just walked in.

Who? Hold that question. The answer is in the picture, and it is you.


### The dog and the boy

Find the dog first, lower right. A big mastiff, half asleep, eyes drooping. Now find the small boy beside him. His foot is planted on the dog's back. He is teasing. The dog is too dignified to care.

The boy and the woman beside him were court dwarfs, servants of the queen. Velázquez gives them the same light, the same seriousness, as the princess. In this room, nobody is a joke.


### The man who wanted a cross

Now look at the painter himself, on the left. Look at his chest. A red cross, shaped like a sword.

That cross is the story of his life. Velázquez wanted one thing the king could not simply give him. He wanted to be a knight of Santiago, one of the proudest orders in Spain.

The obstacle was painting itself. To the nobles, a painter worked with his hands for money. A craftsman. Knights had to prove noble blood, and that they had never earned a living by a trade.

So the Order investigated him. More than a hundred witnesses were questioned. One after another, they swore he had never sold a picture, that he served only the king. The Order still said no. In the end the king went over their heads, to the Pope. Velázquez got his cross. Less than a year later, he was dead.

And here is the turn. When he painted this picture, he did not have the cross yet. It was added later.

> *Legend voice:* Now the legend, and it is a good one. They say that after Velázquez died, the king walked into this room, took up a brush, and painted the cross on his friend's chest himself. A last honour from a king to his painter. It is only a story. But look at the cross, and you will want it to be true.


### The visitors in the mirror

Now the answer to the question. Look at the back wall, left of the open door. A small frame glows faintly. It is a mirror.

In it are two blurred figures. The king and the queen. They are standing where you are standing. That is why every eye in the room is on you.

Stay there a moment. You have walked into the king's place. The princess is looking at her parents, and finding you. The painter is measuring you for his canvas. Who is looking at whom?

Now look at the man on the stairs in the doorway, holding back a curtain. The queen's chamberlain. Is he coming in or going out? Velázquez never tells. He leaves the door open, and your question with it.


### Fire

On Christmas Eve, seventeen thirty-four, the palace caught fire. It burned for days. Hundreds of paintings were lost in the flames, other works by Velázquez among them. This one was carried out through the smoke. It survived, scarred, and has been mended since.

Two centuries later it was carried out again. During the Spanish Civil War, the Prado's treasures were packed into trucks and taken away, eventually to Geneva. Las Meninas went too, and came home.


### What it is worth

People ask what it is worth. It has no price. It will never be sold. For a sense of scale, in nineteen seventy a single portrait by Velázquez, of his assistant, sold in London for more than any painting had ever fetched at auction. That was a small picture of one man. This is the whole court, and the painter's last word on his art.


### What it does to people

> *Legend voice:* You will hear that a French writer, standing here, asked: but where is the painting? As if the room itself were real. The story is often told, and we could not pin down where he said it.

Another Spaniard could not leave it alone. Three centuries after Velázquez, Pablo Picasso shut himself away for months and made fifty-eight paintings answering this one. In them, the sleepy mastiff becomes Picasso's own little dachshund.

Picasso was not the first to answer it. A young American painter, John Singer Sargent, copied Las Meninas in this museum. A few years later he painted four sisters in a dim, deep room, the Daughters of Edward Darley Boit. If you have stood in front of it in Boston, at the Museum of Fine Arts, you were already looking at this room.


### The girl who never grew old

Look once more at the princess. She was being raised to be a bride. At fifteen she was sent to Vienna to marry her uncle, the Emperor. Velázquez had painted her again and again, partly so that her future husband could watch her grow up.

She had several children. Only one survived. She died at twenty-one.

Here she will always be five, reaching for a cup of water, turning to see who has come in. And the person who has come in is you.

When you are ready, stay with Velázquez in the rooms nearby. That is stop ten.


## Stop 10. Velázquez: The Surrender of Breda and the Court Jesters · Room 9A, check the map

*760 words, about 5.4 min*


### A key in a starving town

Stop ten. First floor, room nine A, near the Velázquez hall, but room numbers shift, so check the map. Look for a wide picture of two commanders meeting, with a forest of tall lances on the right.

Imagine a Dutch town, not far south of the Amsterdam you have just left, after almost a year under siege. The food is gone. The town gives up. Its governor, Justin of Nassau, rides out to hand the key of the town to the Spanish general, Ambrogio Spinola.

This is the moment of humiliation. In most wars, the loser kneels. The winner enjoys it. What will Spinola do?

Look at the two men in the centre. The Dutchman on the left bows, holding out the key. He is starting to bend his knee. Now look at Spinola's hand. He has stepped down from his horse. He does not grab the key. He lays his hand on his enemy's shoulder, and stops him from kneeling.

That gesture is real history. Spinola let the defeated garrison march out with their weapons and their flags. Europe talked about his courtesy for years.


### A friend painted from memory

The painter was a boy from Seville, in the Andalusia you have just travelled through. He had never seen Breda. But he knew Spinola. The two men had sailed to Italy together, on the painter's first journey there. By the time Velázquez painted this, about ten years after the surrender, Spinola was dead. He had died, it was said, feeling his king had not valued him.

So this is a tribute to a friend. Velázquez gives him the best thing a painter can give. Not glory. Kindness, caught in a single touch.


### The lances

Now compare the two armies. On the left, the Dutch pikes, few, ragged, leaning every way. On the right, the Spanish lances, straight and close, like a fence against the sky. No one is fighting, yet you know at once who won.

The picture was made for a palace hall, a few minutes' walk from here, built to glorify the king's victories. Every other battle picture in that hall shows force. This one shows mercy.

> *Legend voice:* You will hear that the man on the far right edge, under a big hat, looking out at you, is Velázquez himself, standing among the winners. It is a tradition, never proven. Find him, and decide.


### The people nobody painted

Now find the room of Velázquez's jesters and dwarfs, near the Velázquez hall. Check the map.

The royal court kept dozens of entertainers. Some were people of short stature. Some had learning disabilities. They were laughed at, and sometimes treated as playthings. Painters before Velázquez usually showed them as curiosities, beside a royal master.

Velázquez painted them alone. And he painted them the way he painted the king.

Find the small man seated on the ground, legs straight out towards you, fists clenched. For more than a century he had one name. A few years ago the Prado gave him another, the jester called El Primo, because old palace inventories suggested the labels had been swapped. Scholars still argue. He does not care what we call him.

Look at his eyes. He is not performing. He is not asking to be liked. He is looking at you the way you are looking at him, and he is not impressed.

Stand there a moment. Who is being judged here?


### The man with no floor

Now find a man in black standing alone, arms out, as if speaking. There is no floor. No wall. Only his shadow tells you where the ground is.

He was a jester at court. When the French painter Édouard Manet came to Madrid and saw him, he wrote home that it might be the most astonishing piece of painting ever done. Manet went back to Paris and painted figures standing in empty light, just like this one.


### A myth in the back room

If The Spinners hangs nearby, check the map and find it. In front, women spin wool in a dusty workshop. For a long time, that was all people saw.

Now look past them, into the bright room at the back. A goddess in a helmet raises her arm. Before her stands a young weaver who boasted she could weave better than a god. In the old myth, the goddess turns her into a spider.

Velázquez hides the gods in the back, and puts working women in the light. That was his whole way of seeing. Look at the people everyone else walks past.


### Walk on

When you are ready, leave Velázquez for a painter who loved flesh and colour: Rubens and his Three Graces. That is stop eleven.


## Stop 11. Rubens: The Three Graces · Room 29

*600 words, about 4.3 min*


### A picture nobody was meant to see

Stop eleven. First floor, room twenty-nine. Look for three naked women standing in a ring, arms around each other, under a garland of flowers in a green landscape.

Before you look closely, here is a question to hold. Why did a king of Spain order this painting burned? And how did it survive?


### The old man and the girl

Picture Antwerp, a rich port city in Flanders. A painter in his fifties, the most famous in Europe, is grieving. His wife of many years has died. He is rich, honoured, knighted by kings. And he is alone.

Then he does something that sets the city talking. He marries again. She is a merchant's daughter, and she is sixteen years old. Her name is Helena.

He does not hide how he feels. For the last ten years of his life he paints her again and again. As a goddess. As a saint. As herself, laughing, with their children.

Now look at the woman on the left. Fair hair, soft round face, turning towards her sisters. Many believe this is Helena. An old man's young wife, painted into a myth, welcomed by the goddesses as one of their own.

Look at the hands. Each woman holds another, lightly, at the shoulder, the arm, the waist. Nobody grips. Look at the skin, the faint pink of blood under it. This is not marble. This is someone he touched.


### Kept behind a door

This was not painted for a patron. Rubens kept it for himself, in his own house. It was still there when he died.

> *Legend voice:* The story is often told that Helena, after his death, was ashamed of the intimate pictures of her, and wanted some of them destroyed. It is gossip, and it usually attaches to another painting of her. But you can see why the rumour clung.

His belongings were sold. The king of Spain, who adored his work, bought this one. And so it came to Madrid.


### The king who wanted it burned

Now the answer to the question.

More than a century later, a different king of Spain looked at the royal nudes and saw sin. He ordered them burned. This picture was on the list.

It was saved by being hidden. It was locked away with other nudes in a closed room at the Royal Academy of Fine Arts, where only a few were allowed to see it. The fire never came.

Stand back now and see the three of them whole. A painting one man made out of love, and another man wanted to destroy out of shame. Which of them do you think was looking more honestly?


### The painter who carried secrets

Rubens knew this court well. He had come to Madrid as a diplomat, on a secret mission to make peace with England.

Here he met the king's young painter, Velázquez, not yet thirty. The famous visitor kept his distance from most court painters. Not this one. The two went out together to the royal monastery to study its great Venetian paintings. Soon after, the young man left for Italy. Many believe Rubens told him to go.


### A god who eats his children

The museum also has a darker Rubens. Saturn, the god who ate his own children so none could overthrow him. It is listed in room seventy-nine, so check the map.

Rubens shows a powerful old god, stars above his head, tearing into a baby's chest. It is horrible, but it is still a myth.

Keep it in your mind. Before the end of this tour, Goya will show you the same god. And Goya's will not let you sleep.


### Walk on

When you are ready, follow the signs for Goya and his portrait of the royal family. That is stop twelve.


## Stop 12. Goya: The Family of Charles the Fourth · Room 32

*628 words, about 4.5 min*


### The man who could not hear

Stop twelve. First floor, room thirty-two. Look for the biggest canvas in the room, a royal family standing in one long glittering row.

Picture a palace in spring. Servants hurry through the halls. One by one, princes and princesses sit for a painter, and he sketches each face fast, in oil, on small canvases. They talk to him. He does not answer. He cannot hear a word they say.

His name is Goya. A few years before, a sudden illness nearly killed him. He lived, but the world went silent, and it stayed silent for the rest of his life. A deaf man, in a court that runs on whispers.

And yet here he is, at the very top. The king has just made him First Court Painter. Now he must paint the whole family, in one picture, and every one of them will look at the result. What will a man who cannot hear the flattery around him choose to show?


### The woman at the centre

Find the king. Big, solid, in brown, his chest heavy with medals, standing a little right of centre. He is not the middle of this picture.

Now look to the true centre. A woman in a silver dress, bare arms, a small boy holding her hand. She is the queen, María Luisa. Everyone at court knew where power lived in this family. Goya has simply put it where it belonged.

And beside her should stand the one man who is missing. Manuel Godoy. A handsome young guardsman who rose, through her favour, to rule Spain.

> *Legend voice:* Madrid whispered that Godoy was the queen's lover. Pamphlets said it. Cartoons drew it. The king, they said, was the last to know. It was never proved, and it hardly mattered. Everyone believed it.


### A face that turns away

Now look to the far left. A young man in blue steps forward. He is the heir to the throne. Beside him a young woman has turned her head away, as if she cannot bear to be seen.

Nobody knows who she is. One idea is that she stands for the prince's future bride, not yet chosen. A place kept open in the family, with no face to fill it. Stay with her a moment. In a room full of people showing themselves off, she is the only one hiding.


### The painter in the dark

Now look behind the prince, into the shadows at the back. A man stands at a huge canvas, half lost in the gloom, looking straight out at you.

It is Goya. And you have seen this before. A few rooms ago, in Las Meninas, Velázquez painted himself at his easel beside the king's daughter. Goya loved Velázquez above all painters. Here he takes the same place. But Velázquez stands in warm light, among friends. Goya stands in the dark, watching.


### The grocer and the lottery

So, flattery or mockery? Look at the faces. The queen's is sharp and hard. The king's is kindly, and empty.

> *Legend voice:* The famous jibe is usually credited to the French writer Théophile Gautier. He is said to have called them the corner grocer and his wife, just after winning the lottery. It is a wicked line, and it stuck.

Yet the family seems to have been pleased. Goya kept his post. Perhaps that is the most unsettling thing of all. He painted them exactly as they were, and they liked it.


### The glitter goes out

Look one last time at all that silk and all those diamonds. Within eight years, every bit of it is gone. Godoy is toppled by a mob. The king gives up his crown. Napoleon lures father and son to France and keeps the throne for himself.

The painter in the shadows will still be here, watching, when the lights go out.

Stay on the first floor, and follow the map to room thirty-eight. That is stop thirteen.


## Stop 13. Goya: The Naked Maja and The Clothed Maja · Room 38

*583 words, about 4.2 min*


### A room behind a door

Stop thirteen. First floor, room thirty-eight. Look for two paintings side by side of the same young woman, lying on a green couch, hands behind her head. In one she is dressed. In the other she is not.

Imagine a palace in Madrid, and a door most guests never pass. Behind it, the most powerful man in Spain keeps the pictures no one is supposed to own.

He is Godoy, the queen's favourite, the man missing from the royal family portrait. In his secret room hangs a nude by Velázquez, a goddess seen from behind. And beside it hangs something far more dangerous.


### The woman who looks back

Look at the naked one first. Look at her eyes.

In this country, the Church frowned on painted nudes. The few that existed were goddesses, nymphs, figures from old myths, safely far away. This woman has no myth to hide behind. She is a real Spaniard of her own day. And she is not shy. She looks straight at you, calm, a little amused.

Stand with that for a moment. Who is looking at whom? Is she on show for you, or are you on show for her?


### Whose face

Now look at her face again. It sits oddly on the body, almost as if it belongs to someone else. For two hundred years, people have tried to put a name to it.

> *Legend voice:* The great legend says she is the Duchess of Alba. She was the richest, most daring woman in Madrid. Goya painted her, and after her husband died, he stayed with her at her country estate. Gossip made them lovers, and the gossip made her the Maja. You will even hear that her descendants opened her tomb to prove the body was not hers. It is legend, and a delicious one.

The likelier answer is closer to home. Godoy had a young mistress named Pepita Tudó. Years later he married her. A private picture of the private man's own lover. But no one knows for certain. The Maja has kept her secret.


### The Inquisition knocks

Then the world turns. A mob brings Godoy down. His palace is stripped, his pictures listed, and the secret room is opened to strangers. Among them, the Church.

In eighteen fifteen, the Inquisition orders Goya to appear before it. Whose are these paintings? Why did you make them? For whom?

Goya is nearly seventy. The king who has just returned is hunting down men like him. This summons could ruin him.

And then the record goes silent. We do not know if he went, or what he said. We only know that he went on painting, and the Maja survived.


### Dressed for company

Now look at the clothed Maja. The same pose. The same gaze. But here the paint is quicker, looser, the jacket and sash dashed on.

> *Legend voice:* You will often hear that the clothed Maja hung in front of the naked one, and that a hidden device slid it aside for special guests. It is told everywhere. No document proves it. But stand between the two, and you can see why the story was invented.


### Scandal by post

Her power to shock outlived them all. In nineteen thirty, Spain put the naked Maja on postage stamps. The United States banned letters that carried her. A woman painted for one man's locked room was now crossing oceans, and still causing trouble.

Look at her eyes one last time. She is still not embarrassed. You might be.


### Walk on

Now go down to the ground floor and follow the map to room sixty-four. That is stop fourteen.


## Stop 14. Goya: The Second and Third of May · Room 64

*724 words, about 5.2 min*


### A crowd at the palace gate

Stop fourteen. Ground floor, room sixty-four. Look for two huge paintings hung as a pair, a street battle with horsemen in turbans, and a firing squad at night.

Picture a May morning in Madrid. French soldiers fill the city. The king and his family are already gone, taken to France. Now a carriage waits at the palace, ready to take away the last royal child, a small boy.

A crowd gathers at the gate. Someone shouts that they are stealing the prince. And the people of Madrid, with knives and sticks and their bare hands, throw themselves at the best army in Europe.


### Knives against sabres

Look at the first painting, the Second of May. Find the horseman in the turban, falling backwards off his horse. He is one of Napoleon's Mamelukes, cavalry brought from Egypt. A foreigner twice over, to the men pulling him down.

Look for a hero. You will not find one. No general, no flag, no centre. Only a knot of men and horses and blades, and a man driving a knife into a falling rider. This is not glory. It is butchery, close up, on a city street.


### The lantern

The French crush the rising by nightfall. And then they take their revenge. Through the night and into the dawn, they shoot prisoners in batches, against hillsides and walls around the city.

Now stand in front of the second painting, the Third of May. Find the lantern first. A square box on the ground, between the guns and the men. It is the only light in the picture. It is not the light of heaven. It is there so the soldiers can see to aim.

Look at the soldiers. Can you see a single face? No. Only backs, and boots, and a row of rifles leaning forward together, like one machine.


### The man in the white shirt

Now find the man the light falls on. He kneels in a white shirt, his arms flung wide. His face is brown from the sun, a labourer, no one important. His eyes are wide open. He is looking into the barrels.

Look at his right hand. In the palm there is a mark, like the wound of a nail. Goya has given a nameless workman the wounds of Christ.

Stay with him for a moment. He knows. In one second the guns will fire, and he has chosen to face them. Look at the man beside him, hiding his eyes, and the dead in front of him. Which one would you be?


### Why the old painter told it

Goya did not paint this in the year it happened. He painted it six years later, when the French had gone and the old royal line was coming home.

And he had something to prove. Through the war he had kept his post at court, under Napoleon's brother. Now he asked the new government to let him record the heroism of the rising. It was a statement, and perhaps a plea. I was on your side all along.

> *Legend voice:* There is a story that Goya watched the shootings from his window, then went out that night with a lantern to sketch the dead. It was told by a servant, many years later. It is almost certainly legend. But look at that lantern, and you see why people wanted to believe it.


### A crash on the coast road

These two canvases nearly died in another war. In the Spanish Civil War, the Prado sent its treasures away from the bombs. In nineteen thirty-eight, the lorry carrying these paintings crashed in a coastal town called Benicarló. The Second of May was torn open. Pieces of paint were lost. The scars stayed for seventy years, until a restoration brought its colour back.

Today the museum puts no price on them. They are among the treasures of Spain. They will never be sold.


### What it set loose

The art historian Kenneth Clark called the Third of May the first great picture that could be called revolutionary. Before it, war in paint was heroes on horseback. After it, war was the man in the white shirt.

Painters never forgot him. Manet borrowed this firing squad. Picasso carried it into Guernica. Look once more at those open arms. That gesture has been copied for two hundred years, because nobody has found a better way to say it.


### Walk on

Stay on the ground floor. Follow the map to rooms sixty-six and sixty-seven, for the Black Paintings. That is stop fifteen.


## Stop 15. Goya: The Black Paintings · Room 66 and 67, check the map

*729 words, about 5.2 min*


### The house of the deaf man

Stop fifteen. Ground floor, rooms sixty-six and sixty-seven, so check the map. Look for dark, muddy pictures in black and brown, many long and low, as if cut from a wall.

Picture a farmhouse across the river from Madrid. An old man lives there. He is in his seventies, deaf for more than twenty-five years, and he has just come close to dying, again.

He is Goya. The court painter of the glittering family upstairs. Spain has been through invasion, war and a returning king who jails his enemies. Goya has painted all of it. Now he shuts the door.

The house is known by a local nickname, the Quinta del Sordo, the house of the deaf man. And on its plaster walls, in the room where he eats and the room above, he begins to paint.

No one has asked for these pictures. No king, no church, no buyer. He never names them. He never writes a word about them. What does a man paint when he is painting only for himself?


### The god who eats his children

Find the giant. He is in room sixty-seven. A wild, naked figure clutches a human body and tears into it with his teeth. This is Saturn, the old god who devoured his children, afraid one of them would take his throne.

Now look only at his eyes. They are not hungry. They are terrified. He stares out of the dark as if he cannot believe what his own hands are doing. And he cannot stop.

Goya hung this in his dining room. Picture him at the table, every evening, with this on the wall.

> *Legend voice:* People have read Saturn as Spain devouring its own sons, as a tyrant king, as old age eating a life, as Goya's terror of death. All of them are guesses. He left no answer.


### The goat and the crowd

Now find a long, wide crowd scene. A huddle of faces sits in the dark, gaping, wide-eyed, gathered around a huge black goat, the devil, in a black cloak. This is the Witches' Sabbath.

All his life, Goya mocked superstition. He drew priests and witches as fools. Here, at the end, the fools have won. Look at those faces. Not one of them is thinking. They are all listening, hungry, to the goat.


### Two men in the mud

Now find two men in an open landscape, swinging clubs at each other. Look at their legs. They are sunk to the knees in the ground. Neither can run. Neither can win. They will go on until one of them dies. Spain had just torn itself apart, and would again. Goya, it seems, saw no way out.


### The dog

Now find the quietest picture of all. Almost the whole canvas is empty. A wash of brown and yellow, rising up like a wall. At the very bottom, a small dog's head peers over a dark slope.

Is he sinking? Is he waiting for someone? He is looking up, at something we cannot see. Or at nothing at all.

Stay with the dog. It is so bare, and so lonely, it looks a hundred years ahead of its time. What do you think he is looking for?


### Bordeaux

Soon after, the king's men come for the liberals. Goya's friends are arrested, or shot, or run. He asks permission to take the waters in France. He is nearly eighty. He does not come back.

He dies in exile in Bordeaux, still drawing, still curious. His last pictures there show beggars, old men, a figure on a swing.


### Cut from the walls

The house passes from owner to owner, until a banker buys it, Baron d'Erlanger. He has the paintings peeled from the plaster and laid onto canvas. It is brutal work, and much of the paint is lost. He takes them to Paris, to the great world exhibition of eighteen seventy-eight, hoping to sell. Hardly anyone notices. No one buys.

In the end he gives them to Spain, and they come here. The house itself is pulled down.

> *Legend voice:* One historian once argued that Goya did not paint them at all, but his son did. Most experts rejected it, and the Prado has no doubt.

Look at the dog one last time. Pictures painted for no one, on the walls of a house that no longer exists. And now the whole world comes to look.


### Walk on

This is our last stop. When you are ready, follow the signs to the exit, and play the final track.


## Leaving the Prado

*236 words, about 1.7 min*


### The painter in the shadows

Think back to the first room of this story. A deaf man stands in the dark at the back of a royal portrait, watching a family glitter.

He went on watching. He watched the most powerful man in Spain unlock a secret room for a naked woman who stared right back. He watched the Inquisition come knocking. He watched his city rise with knives against an empire, and he watched it shot down by lantern light.


### The house with the black walls

And when it was all over, he went home, shut the door, and painted for no one. A god eating his child. Two men sinking as they fight. A small dog, looking up at nothing. Then exile, and a quiet death far from home.

He began his life painting picnics and dances for the king's tapestries. He ended it painting the darkest pictures of his age. Between the two, he taught painting how to look at power, and at war, and not look away.


### Where the story goes next

The story does not end here. A short walk from this building, at the Reina Sofía museum, hangs Picasso's Guernica. It is a scream about a town bombed from the air, and Picasso painted it with the man in the white shirt in mind. Go and stand in front of it, tomorrow or on your next visit, and look for Goya's open arms.

Thank you for walking with me through the Prado, Monica. Goodbye for now.
