# title: Stop 9. Velázquez: Las Meninas
# stop: 9
# room: 12

## Caught in the act

N: Stop nine. First floor, room twelve, the great Velázquez hall. Look for the tallest picture in the room, and the crowd: a little girl in pale silver, and a painter with his brush raised.

...

N: Picture a morning in the old royal palace of Madrid. A dim, high room. The king's painter is at work. And the household has drifted in to watch, the way children and servants do.

N: Imagine it. The little princess is five. A maid kneels and offers her water in a small red clay cup. Another maid bends in a curtsey. A dog sleeps on the floor.

...

N: Then something happens. Look at their faces. The princess turns her eyes. The painter stops and stares. The woman on the right looks straight out. Someone important has just walked in.

N: Who? Hold that question. The answer is in the picture, and it is you.

## The dog and the boy

...

N: Find the dog first, lower right. A big mastiff, half asleep, eyes drooping. Now find the small boy beside him. His foot is planted on the dog's back. He is teasing. The dog is too dignified to care.

N: The boy and the woman beside him were court dwarfs, servants of the queen. Velázquez gives them the same light, the same seriousness, as the princess. In this room, nobody is a joke.

## The man who wanted a cross

...

N: Now look at the painter himself, on the left. Look at his chest. A red cross, shaped like a sword.

N: That cross is the story of his life. Velázquez wanted one thing the king could not simply give him. He wanted to be a knight of Santiago, one of the proudest orders in Spain.

N: The obstacle was painting itself. To the nobles, a painter worked with his hands for money. A craftsman. Knights had to prove noble blood, and that they had never earned a living by a trade.

N: So the Order investigated him. More than a hundred witnesses were questioned. One after another, they swore he had never sold a picture, that he served only the king. The Order still said no. In the end the king went over their heads, to the Pope. Velázquez got his cross. Less than a year later, he was dead.

N: And here is the turn. When he painted this picture, he did not have the cross yet. It was added later.

...

A: Now the legend, and it is a good one. They say that after Velázquez died, the king walked into this room, took up a brush, and painted the cross on his friend's chest himself. A last honour from a king to his painter. It is only a story. But look at the cross, and you will want it to be true.

...

## The visitors in the mirror

N: Now the answer to the question. Look at the back wall, left of the open door. A small frame glows faintly. It is a mirror.

...

N: In it are two blurred figures. The king and the queen. They are standing where you are standing. That is why every eye in the room is on you.

N: Stay there a moment. You have walked into the king's place. The princess is looking at her parents, and finding you. The painter is measuring you for his canvas. Who is looking at whom?

...

N: Now look at the man on the stairs in the doorway, holding back a curtain. The queen's chamberlain. Is he coming in or going out? Velázquez never tells. He leaves the door open, and your question with it.

## Fire

...

N: On Christmas Eve, seventeen thirty-four, the palace caught fire. It burned for days. Hundreds of paintings were lost in the flames, other works by Velázquez among them. This one was carried out through the smoke. It survived, scarred, and has been mended since.

N: Two centuries later it was carried out again. During the Spanish Civil War, the Prado's treasures were packed into trucks and taken away, eventually to Geneva. Las Meninas went too, and came home.

## What it is worth

N: People ask what it is worth. It has no price. It will never be sold. For a sense of scale, in nineteen seventy a single portrait by Velázquez, of his assistant, sold in London for more than any painting had ever fetched at auction. That was a small picture of one man. This is the whole court, and the painter's last word on his art.

## What it does to people

...

A: You will hear that a French writer, standing here, asked: but where is the painting? As if the room itself were real. The story is often told, and we could not pin down where he said it.

N: Another Spaniard could not leave it alone. Three centuries after Velázquez, Pablo Picasso shut himself away for months and made fifty-eight paintings answering this one. In them, the sleepy mastiff becomes Picasso's own little dachshund.

N: Picasso was not the first to answer it. A young American painter, John Singer Sargent, copied Las Meninas in this museum. A few years later he painted four sisters in a dim, deep room, the Daughters of Edward Darley Boit. If you have stood in front of it in Boston, at the Museum of Fine Arts, you were already looking at this room.

## The girl who never grew old

...

N: Look once more at the princess. She was being raised to be a bride. At fifteen she was sent to Vienna to marry her uncle, the Emperor. Velázquez had painted her again and again, partly so that her future husband could watch her grow up.

N: She had several children. Only one survived. She died at twenty-one.

...

N: Here she will always be five, reaching for a cup of water, turning to see who has come in. And the person who has come in is you.

## Walk on

...

N: When you are ready, stay with Velázquez in the rooms nearby. That is stop ten.
