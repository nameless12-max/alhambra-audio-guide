# title: Stop 14. Goya: The Second and Third of May
# stop: 14
# room: 64

## A crowd at the palace gate

N: Stop fourteen. Ground floor, room sixty-four. Look for two huge paintings hung as a pair, a street battle with horsemen in turbans, and a firing squad at night.

...

N: Picture a May morning in Madrid. French soldiers fill the city. The king and his family are already gone, taken to France. Now a carriage waits at the palace, ready to take away the last royal child, a small boy.

N: A crowd gathers at the gate. Someone shouts that they are stealing the prince. And the people of Madrid, with knives and sticks and their bare hands, throw themselves at the best army in Europe.

## Knives against sabres

...

N: Look at the first painting, the Second of May. Find the horseman in the turban, falling backwards off his horse. He is one of Napoleon's Mamelukes, cavalry brought from Egypt. A foreigner twice over, to the men pulling him down.

N: Look for a hero. You will not find one. No general, no flag, no centre. Only a knot of men and horses and blades, and a man driving a knife into a falling rider. This is not glory. It is butchery, close up, on a city street.

## The lantern

...

N: The French crush the rising by nightfall. And then they take their revenge. Through the night and into the dawn, they shoot prisoners in batches, against hillsides and walls around the city.

N: Now stand in front of the second painting, the Third of May. Find the lantern first. A square box on the ground, between the guns and the men. It is the only light in the picture. It is not the light of heaven. It is there so the soldiers can see to aim.

...

N: Look at the soldiers. Can you see a single face? No. Only backs, and boots, and a row of rifles leaning forward together, like one machine.

## The man in the white shirt

...

N: Now find the man the light falls on. He kneels in a white shirt, his arms flung wide. His face is brown from the sun, a labourer, no one important. His eyes are wide open. He is looking into the barrels.

N: Look at his right hand. In the palm there is a mark, like the wound of a nail. Goya has given a nameless workman the wounds of Christ.

...

N: Stay with him for a moment. He knows. In one second the guns will fire, and he has chosen to face them. Look at the man beside him, hiding his eyes, and the dead in front of him. Which one would you be?

## Why the old painter told it

...

N: Goya did not paint this in the year it happened. He painted it six years later, when the French had gone and the old royal line was coming home.

N: And he had something to prove. Through the war he had kept his post at court, under Napoleon's brother. Now he asked the new government to let him record the heroism of the rising. It was a statement, and perhaps a plea. I was on your side all along.

...

A: There is a story that Goya watched the shootings from his window, then went out that night with a lantern to sketch the dead. It was told by a servant, many years later. It is almost certainly legend. But look at that lantern, and you see why people wanted to believe it.

## A crash on the coast road

...

N: These two canvases nearly died in another war. In the Spanish Civil War, the Prado sent its treasures away from the bombs. In nineteen thirty-eight, the lorry carrying these paintings crashed in a coastal town called Benicarló. The Second of May was torn open. Pieces of paint were lost. The scars stayed for seventy years, until a restoration brought its colour back.

N: Today the museum puts no price on them. They are among the treasures of Spain. They will never be sold.

## What it set loose

...

N: The art historian Kenneth Clark called the Third of May the first great picture that could be called revolutionary. Before it, war in paint was heroes on horseback. After it, war was the man in the white shirt.

N: Painters never forgot him. Manet borrowed this firing squad. Picasso carried it into Guernica. Look once more at those open arms. That gesture has been copied for two hundred years, because nobody has found a better way to say it.
