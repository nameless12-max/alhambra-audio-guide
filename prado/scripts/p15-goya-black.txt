# title: Stop 15. Goya: The Black Paintings
# stop: 15
# room: 66 and 67, check the map

## The house of the deaf man

N: Stop fifteen. Ground floor, rooms sixty-six and sixty-seven, so check the map. Look for dark, muddy pictures in black and brown, many long and low, as if cut from a wall.

...

N: Picture a farmhouse across the river from Madrid. An old man lives there. He is in his seventies, deaf for more than twenty-five years, and he has just come close to dying, again.

N: He is Goya. The court painter of the glittering family upstairs. Spain has been through invasion, war and a returning king who jails his enemies. Goya has painted all of it. Now he shuts the door.

N: The house is known by a local nickname, the Quinta del Sordo, the house of the deaf man. And on its plaster walls, in the room where he eats and the room above, he begins to paint.

N: No one has asked for these pictures. No king, no church, no buyer. He never names them. He never writes a word about them. What does a man paint when he is painting only for himself?

## The god who eats his children

...

N: Find the giant. He is in room sixty-seven. A wild, naked figure clutches a human body and tears into it with his teeth. This is Saturn, the old god who devoured his children, afraid one of them would take his throne.

N: Now look only at his eyes. They are not hungry. They are terrified. He stares out of the dark as if he cannot believe what his own hands are doing. And he cannot stop.

N: Goya hung this in his dining room. Picture him at the table, every evening, with this on the wall.

...

A: People have read Saturn as Spain devouring its own sons, as a tyrant king, as old age eating a life, as Goya's terror of death. All of them are guesses. He left no answer.

## The goat and the crowd

...

N: Now find a long, wide crowd scene. A huddle of faces sits in the dark, gaping, wide-eyed, gathered around a huge black goat, the devil, in a black cloak. This is the Witches' Sabbath.

N: All his life, Goya mocked superstition. He drew priests and witches as fools. Here, at the end, the fools have won. Look at those faces. Not one of them is thinking. They are all listening, hungry, to the goat.

## Two men in the mud

...

N: Now find two men in an open landscape, swinging clubs at each other. Look at their legs. They are sunk to the knees in the ground. Neither can run. Neither can win. They will go on until one of them dies. Spain had just torn itself apart, and would again. Goya, it seems, saw no way out.

## The dog

...

N: Now find the quietest picture of all. Almost the whole canvas is empty. A wash of brown and yellow, rising up like a wall. At the very bottom, a small dog's head peers over a dark slope.

N: Is he sinking? Is he waiting for someone? He is looking up, at something we cannot see. Or at nothing at all.

...

N: Stay with the dog. It is so bare, and so lonely, it looks a hundred years ahead of its time. What do you think he is looking for?

## Bordeaux

...

N: Soon after, the king's men come for the liberals. Goya's friends are arrested, or shot, or run. He asks permission to take the waters in France. He is nearly eighty. He does not come back.

N: He dies in exile in Bordeaux, still drawing, still curious. His last pictures there show beggars, old men, a figure on a swing.

## Cut from the walls

...

N: The house passes from owner to owner, until a banker buys it, Baron d'Erlanger. He has the paintings peeled from the plaster and laid onto canvas. It is brutal work, and much of the paint is lost. He takes them to Paris, to the great world exhibition of eighteen seventy-eight, hoping to sell. Hardly anyone notices. No one buys.

N: In the end he gives them to Spain, and they come here. The house itself is pulled down.

A: One historian once argued that Goya did not paint them at all, but his son did. Most experts rejected it, and the Prado has no doubt.

N: Look at the dog one last time. Pictures painted for no one, on the walls of a house that no longer exists. And now the whole world comes to look.
