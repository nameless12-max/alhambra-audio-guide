# title: Stop 10. Velázquez: The Surrender of Breda and the Court Jesters
# stop: 10
# room: 9A, check the map

## A key in a starving town

N: Stop ten. First floor, room nine A, near the Velázquez hall, but room numbers shift, so check the map. Look for a wide picture of two commanders meeting, with a forest of tall lances on the right.

...

N: Imagine a Dutch town, not far south of the Amsterdam you have just left, after almost a year under siege. The food is gone. The town gives up. Its governor, Justin of Nassau, rides out to hand the key of the town to the Spanish general, Ambrogio Spinola.

N: This is the moment of humiliation. In most wars, the loser kneels. The winner enjoys it. What will Spinola do?

...

N: Look at the two men in the centre. The Dutchman on the left bows, holding out the key. He is starting to bend his knee. Now look at Spinola's hand. He has stepped down from his horse. He does not grab the key. He lays his hand on his enemy's shoulder, and stops him from kneeling.

N: That gesture is real history. Spinola let the defeated garrison march out with their weapons and their flags. Europe talked about his courtesy for years.

## A friend painted from memory

...

N: The painter was a boy from Seville, in the Andalusia you have just travelled through. He had never seen Breda. But he knew Spinola. The two men had sailed to Italy together, on the painter's first journey there. By the time Velázquez painted this, about ten years after the surrender, Spinola was dead. He had died, it was said, feeling his king had not valued him.

N: So this is a tribute to a friend. Velázquez gives him the best thing a painter can give. Not glory. Kindness, caught in a single touch.

## The lances

...

N: Now compare the two armies. On the left, the Dutch pikes, few, ragged, leaning every way. On the right, the Spanish lances, straight and close, like a fence against the sky. No one is fighting, yet you know at once who won.

N: The picture was made for a palace hall, a few minutes' walk from here, built to glorify the king's victories. Every other battle picture in that hall shows force. This one shows mercy.

...

A: You will hear that the man on the far right edge, under a big hat, looking out at you, is Velázquez himself, standing among the winners. It is a tradition, never proven. Find him, and decide.

...

## The people nobody painted

N: Now find the room of Velázquez's jesters and dwarfs, near the Velázquez hall. Check the map.

N: The royal court kept dozens of entertainers. Some were people of short stature. Some had learning disabilities. They were laughed at, and sometimes treated as playthings. Painters before Velázquez usually showed them as curiosities, beside a royal master.

N: Velázquez painted them alone. And he painted them the way he painted the king.

...

N: Find the small man seated on the ground, legs straight out towards you, fists clenched. For more than a century he had one name. A few years ago the Prado gave him another, the jester called El Primo, because old palace inventories suggested the labels had been swapped. Scholars still argue. He does not care what we call him.

N: Look at his eyes. He is not performing. He is not asking to be liked. He is looking at you the way you are looking at him, and he is not impressed.

...

N: Stand there a moment. Who is being judged here?

## The man with no floor

...

N: Now find a man in black standing alone, arms out, as if speaking. There is no floor. No wall. Only his shadow tells you where the ground is.

N: He was a jester at court. When the French painter Édouard Manet came to Madrid and saw him, he wrote home that it might be the most astonishing piece of painting ever done. Manet went back to Paris and painted figures standing in empty light, just like this one.

## A myth in the back room

...

N: If The Spinners hangs nearby, check the map and find it. In front, women spin wool in a dusty workshop. For a long time, that was all people saw.

N: Now look past them, into the bright room at the back. A goddess in a helmet raises her arm. Before her stands a young weaver who boasted she could weave better than a god. In the old myth, the goddess turns her into a spider.

N: Velázquez hides the gods in the back, and puts working women in the light. That was his whole way of seeing. Look at the people everyone else walks past.

...
