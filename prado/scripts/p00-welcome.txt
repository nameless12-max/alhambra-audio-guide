## The stable that became a museum

N: Welcome to the Prado, Monica. After the Alhambra, here is another palace with a story behind every wall.

N: Picture these halls with no paintings in them. Straw on the floor. The smell of horses. Napoleon's cavalry have moved in, and the museum that does not exist yet is their stable. Down in the vaults, soldiers are stacking gunpowder.

N: The building was never meant for art. A Spanish king ordered it for science, a palace of rocks and plants and stuffed animals, beside the botanical garden next door. Then the king died, the French came, and his dream was left a roofless shell.

## How we will walk

N: Before the story goes on, how this works. There are about fifteen stops. We begin on the ground floor and then go upstairs. Each track opens with the floor and the room number. Find the room by the signs and the museum map, then press play. Take your time between stops.

N: You will hear two voices. I tell you what happened. The second voice tells you the legends, and says when that is what they are.

## A queen's idea

N: So who rescued the ruin? The answer is a young queen. María Isabel de Braganza, wife of King Ferdinand, is remembered by the museum as the one who pushed for the royal paintings to come here, for anyone to see. She was a Portuguese princess, born near Lisbon, the city you have just left.

N: For centuries those pictures had hung in palaces, for kings and their guests. Titian, Rubens, Velázquez, painted for Spanish monarchs, owned by Spanish monarchs. Now the doors would open to the public.

N: She never saw it. She died less than a year before the opening. The Prado opened on the nineteenth of November, eighteen nineteen. Tomorrow is its birthday.

## The night the Prado burned

...

N: Now jump forward. One November morning, Madrid opens its newspaper and reads that the Prado has burned in the night. The flames. The crowds in the street. A national catastrophe.

N: The city is in shock. And then, the next day, the writer confesses. The journalist Mariano de Cavia had made it all up. He invented the fire, he said, to prevent one. That summer there had been two small real fires, and nobody had acted. His lie went round Europe. This time, the government moved.

## The paintings that ran away

N: The real danger came with the Civil War. Bombs fell on Madrid. And the Republic did something astonishing. It loaded its greatest paintings onto lorries and drove them through the war to Valencia. Many were hidden inside the stone towers of an old city gate.

N: The new director of the Prado, named in those months, was Pablo Picasso. He was living in France. He never once sat at his desk.

N: As the war was being lost, the pictures moved again, through Catalonia and over the border to Geneva. They were safe. They came home just as another war broke out across Europe.

...

N: Some of the paintings you will meet today rode in those lorries. Look at them and remember that people risked their lives so you could stand here.

N: Walk on now to stop one, on the ground floor.
