# title: Stop 4. Bosch: The Garden of Earthly Delights
# stop: 4
# room: 56A

## A king buys a garden of sin

N: Stop four. Ground floor, room fifty-six A. Look for the crowd, and above it a vast three-part painting swarming with tiny pale naked bodies.

N: Picture the most serious king in Europe. Philip the Second of Spain. He dresses in black. He rules half the world from a cell-like room inside a monastery he has built in the mountains.

N: And yet this man paid good money for a painting of hundreds of naked lovers, and hung it in his monastery. Why? Hold that question. We will come back to it.

## The doors open

...

N: If the back of the panels is showing, look there first. It is all grey. A glass globe, the world on the third day of creation. No people yet. In the top corner, very small, God, with a book. The Latin line above him says, he spoke, and it was done.

N: Now let the doors swing open in your mind. Colour floods in. And so do we.

## Paradise has a watcher

...

N: The left panel is Eden. God, young and gentle, holds Eve by the wrist and presents her to Adam, who has just woken up. It is the first wedding. Everything is calm.

...

N: Now find the tall pink fountain behind them. Look into the round hole at its base. Something is looking back. An owl.

N: In Bosch's world, the owl was a creature of the night. It could stand for wisdom. It could also stand for the soul that prefers darkness. Even in paradise, something is waiting.

## The party

...

N: Now the centre. Hundreds of naked people. Giant strawberries, cherries, blackberries, bigger than their heads. Lovers inside a glass bubble. Lovers hiding in a mussel shell. A ring of riders circles a pool of bathing women, on horses, camels, boars.

N: Nobody is old. Nobody is working. Nobody is ashamed. It is the most joyful thing in this museum, and it makes people uneasy. Stand here a moment. Is this a dream of freedom, or a trap?

...

N: Find the man in the lower left with his arms around an owl bigger than he is. The watcher has come into the garden.

A: There are wild theories about this panel. The boldest says that Bosch worked for a secret sect who practised free love, and that this is their heaven. It is a wonderful story. There is no evidence for it at all.

## The bill arrives

...

N: Now the right panel. The light goes out. Cities burn on the horizon.

...

N: Find the musical instruments. A man is stretched across the strings of a giant harp. Another is crushed beneath a huge lute. Now look at the backside of that crushed man. Bosch has painted a line of music on it.

N: In two thousand fourteen, an American student transcribed those notes and played them online. Five hundred years after Bosch, a song from hell went round the world.

...

N: Now the tree man, in the centre of hell. His body is a cracked eggshell. His legs are dead trees standing in boats. Inside his hollow body, people drink at a tavern table. And his pale face turns over his shoulder, and looks straight at you. Of all the thousands of figures here, he is the one who sees you.

A: People have long whispered that the tree man is Bosch himself, looking out from his own hell. It is legend. No certain portrait of Bosch survives. But stand in front of that face and the idea is hard to shake.

...

N: Last, the lower right corner. A blue monster with the head of a bird sits on a tall chair, a cooking pot for a crown. The prince of hell. He swallows sinners whole and passes them out below, into a pit.

## Stolen from a rebel

...

N: How did this reach Madrid? Through a war. The painting first hung in a palace in Brussels, owned by a great noble family. It passed to William of Orange, the prince who would lead the Dutch revolt against Spain.

N: In fifteen sixty-eight, Philip's iron general, the Duke of Alba, marched in to crush that revolt. He seized the rebel's property. The Garden was taken from Spain's greatest enemy, and years later the king bought it for himself.

N: You have just come from Amsterdam, and from the country that revolt would create. This painting was taken from the man who led it.

## The monk's answer

...

N: So, back to the question. Why would the gloomiest king in Europe want this?

N: At his monastery, some of the monks thought the painting was heresy. One of them, the librarian José de Sigüenza, wrote a defence. He said it was a sermon. The centre panel is the brief sweetness of pleasure, like a strawberry, gone almost before you taste it. Then comes the bill. In the monastery's records it is simply the painting of the strawberry tree.

N: Seen that way, this is not a party at all. It is a warning, painted for a man who wanted to be warned every day.

N: It will never be sold. No price has ever been put on it, because there is nothing else like it in the world.

## Two more warnings

...

N: In this room, look for two more works linked to Bosch. The Haywain shows a cart of hay rolling to hell, while pope, king and peasant all fight to grab a handful.

N: The other is a painted tabletop, The Table of the Seven Deadly Sins. In its centre is a great eye, with Christ in the pupil, and a Latin warning. Beware, beware, God sees. The king kept it in his own rooms.

N: And if you saw the great Bosch triptych in Lisbon, The Temptation of Saint Anthony, you have already met his demons. Here is its great rival.

...

N: Before you go, find the owl in the fountain one more time. It was watching the first couple. It is watching you.

N: Walk on to room fifty-five A, Bruegel's Triumph of Death. Check the map. That is stop five.
