# title: Stop 8. Titian: Charles the Fifth at Mühlberg
# stop: 8
# room: 27

## An emperor who cannot walk

N: Stop eight. First floor, room twenty-seven, in the central gallery. Look for a huge canvas of a lone rider in armour on a dark horse, a long lance in his hand, and a red and gold sky behind him.

...

N: Picture a river in Germany, on a spring morning. Mist on the water. On the far bank, the army of the Protestant princes who have defied him. On this side, the most powerful man in the world.

N: And he is in agony. The emperor, Charles the Fifth, has gout. His joints are swollen. In the days before, he has had to be carried in a litter. He is in his late forties, and his body is giving out.

N: On the morning of the battle, he climbs onto his horse anyway.

...

N: His soldiers cross the river. The enemy breaks. Their leader is taken prisoner. It is the greatest victory of Charles's life. The year is fifteen forty-seven.

N: You may have met this man before. He is the emperor who ordered the great palace with the round courtyard inside the Alhambra. If you stood in that circle of columns in Granada, you stood in his dream of power.

## What the painter left out

N: Now look at the picture. Look for the battle.

...

N: There is none. No bodies. No river. No enemy. No pain. Only one man, riding calmly out of a dark wood, as evening comes on.

N: The painter, Titian, has taken a sick, exhausted man and made him into an idea. A Christian knight, alone, steady, unbeatable. Look at the face. Pale, the jaw set hard. Is that calm, or is it a man holding himself together?

N: Now look at the armour. Every plate, every gold edge. That is not invention. It is the very suit Charles wore that day. It still exists. It is kept in the royal armoury of Madrid, a short walk from this room.

## The night the palace burned

N: For almost two hundred years this picture hung in the old royal palace of Madrid. Then, on Christmas Eve, seventeen thirty-four, the palace caught fire.

N: Imagine the servants running through smoke, cutting canvases from their frames, throwing them from windows. Hundreds of pictures burned that night.

N: This one was carried out. Scorched, damaged, but alive. Look at the lower part of the picture, around the horse's legs and the ground. Some of what you see there is the patient work of the restorers who saved it.

...

N: No price has ever been put on it. It belongs to the Spanish state, and it will never be sold. For many, it is the picture that taught Spain how to paint a king.

## The brush on the floor

N: Charles loved this painter as he loved no other. He made him a count, an extraordinary honour for a painter. And when Charles gave up his crowns and went to die in a monastery, he took Titian's pictures with him.

...

A: There is a story everyone tells. One day in the studio, Titian drops his brush. Before anyone can move, the emperor bends down, picks it up, and hands it back. The court is shocked. Charles says only that Titian deserves to be served by an emperor. It is a legend, written down long after both men were dead. But people have loved it for four hundred years.

...

## The son's secret pictures

N: The emperor's son, Philip the Second, wanted something very different from Titian. Not power. Desire.

N: In room forty-four, check the map, are two of the paintings Titian sent him. In one, the goddess Venus clings to her young lover, begging him not to go hunting. He pulls away. He will be killed. In the other, Danaë lies back as a god comes to her as a shower of gold.

N: Titian wrote to the prince that the two were a pair. One woman seen from the front. One from the back.

N: The series did not stop here. Another of the paintings Titian made for the same king, the Rape of Europa, crossed the Atlantic. It hangs in the Gardner Museum in Boston. You may have walked past it there.

...

N: So here is the father, painted as a knight who feels no pain. And there, nearby, the son's pictures, all longing and loss. Stand with the emperor a moment longer. Which of them is the truer portrait of a man?

...
