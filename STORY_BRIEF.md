# Rewrite brief: stories, not facts

The reader of the first drafts said: "too factual. his name. his other name. he died. we need to make stories, not throw facts." Rewrite every track you own, in place, to fix that. Keep the file format, ids and stop numbers.

## What a track is now
One story, told by a storyteller standing next to her. It has a person who wants something, something in the way, a turn, and an ending that changes how she sees the picture. Everything else is cut.

- Open inside a scene: a place, a moment, someone doing something. Never open with a description, a date, or "this painting was made by".
- Build suspense. Ask a question early and hold the answer. Reveal, then tell her where to look to see the proof on the canvas.
- Tell it in scenes with stakes and feeling: what he feared, what she risked, what it cost. Present tense is welcome for scenes.
- The looking is part of the plot: "Find the dog. He is asleep. In a second that boy's foot will land on him."
- Legends and rumours are welcome and wanted. Give them the A voice and tell them with relish. One short line saying it is legend is enough; do not lecture about sources.
- End each story on an image or a line worth remembering, then a one-line "Walk on".

## What moves people
Each track must land one human feeling, not just a plot. Say what people feel in front of this picture and why it gets to them: grief, tenderness, dread, desire, shame, awe, the shock of being looked at. Find the human moment inside the work (a mother's hand, a dog nobody notices, a man who knows he is about to die) and slow down on it. Where it is documented, tell how real people have reacted: who wept, who fainted, who could not look, who came back every day. Let her feel it herself: give her a silence and one question to stand with. Do not tell her what to feel; show her the thing that causes it.

## What to cut
- Biography recitals: born, trained, moved, died. Alternative names and titles. Lists of owners. Strings of dates.
- At most three dates and five named people per track. Prefer "the king", "his rival", "twenty years later" to names and years that do not matter to the story.
- Encyclopedic sentences ("It is considered one of the most important..."). Hedging paragraphs. Catalogue descriptions.
- Do not explain art-history terms unless the story needs one.

## What it is worth
Where a work is among the most valuable in the museum, say so in one or two vivid lines at a natural point. Never invent a figure. Use only: a real documented price or insurance valuation, or a clearly labelled comparison ("a lesser picture by him sold for X in YEAR; this will never be sold"). If you have no sourced number, say it is beyond price and why, or say nothing. Record the source in your notes file.

## Still true
- Every fact must be true. Vivid does not mean invented: no made-up dialogue, weather, thoughts or gestures presented as fact. You may say "imagine" or "picture" to mark a scene you are staging.
- Keep the opening locator short: "Stop N." then floor and room in one sentence, then one sentence on how to spot the picture. Keep "check the map" where the room is unconfirmed.
- Add a header line `# room: 56A` (or `# room: check the map`) under `# stop:` in every stop track.
- Change `# title:` to the form `Stop N. Artist: Work`, e.g. `Stop 4. Bosch: The Garden of Earthly Delights`.
- Same length limits as before. Numbers as words. Short sentences. `...` pauses before she looks. `## ` headings should sound like chapter titles of a story, not labels like "Where you are".
- Update your names file if you add or drop names.

## Flat versus alive (style only; verify any fact before using)
Flat: "The building was designed by the architect Juan de Villanueva in seventeen eighty-five, for King Charles the Third. It was meant for natural science."
Alive: "Picture this hall with no paintings in it. Straw on the floor. The smell of horses. Napoleon's cavalry have moved in, and the museum that does not exist yet is their stable."

Report under 80 words: files rewritten, any value figures used with source, doubts.
